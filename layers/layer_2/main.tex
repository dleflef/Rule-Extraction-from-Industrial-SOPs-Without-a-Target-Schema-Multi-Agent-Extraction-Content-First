\begin{abstract}
Extracting safety-critical operational rules from industrial Standard Operating
Procedures (SOPs) is complicated by structural heterogeneity: the same facility may
encode constraints as narrative prose, numeric threshold tables, and role-zone
permission matrices within a single document set.
Conventional exact-match metrics further obscure extraction quality because
ground-truth identifiers in industrial benchmarks are annotator conventions
rarely present in the source text, penalising operationally correct extractions
for naming mismatches rather than semantic errors.

This thesis addresses both problems in the context of the iMAKS
(industrial Multi-Agent Knowledge extraction Synthetic) benchmark, a four-layer
dataset simulating a nine-station packaging facility with four SOPs, 86 annotated
operational rules, and 211\,200 sensor readings.
We introduce \emph{F1\textsubscript{content}}, a content-first metric based on
Hungarian optimal assignment and SBERT field-level similarity that evaluates
operational correctness independently of rule naming conventions.

A systematic grid search across five locally-deployed LLMs (4\,B--20\,B parameters)
and ten prompting paradigms establishes that no single-agent configuration surpasses
F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.728 (\texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, few-shot static).
Cross-type contamination, threshold values leaking into narrative rule classes and
vice versa, is identified as the primary bottleneck for structurally heterogeneous
corpora.

To overcome this, we design a seven-node LangGraph multi-agent pipeline that routes
each SOP to a specialised extractor based on document type, then applies a
deterministic Consensus Merger, an LLM Validator for grounding and gap-filling,
an iterative Quality Gap Evaluator with one re-extraction cycle, and an Identifier
Canonicalizer.
The full pipeline achieves F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.909 on iMAKS
(a relative improvement of 25\% over the best single-agent baseline on a
deterministic run) and a mean F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.864~$\pm$~0.023
over 20 stochastic re-runs (19\% relative improvement), confirming that the
gain is robust across natural LLM non-determinism.
An ablation study isolates each component's contribution: removing the Validator
reduces F1\textsubscript{content} to 0.894; removing the judge loop to 0.885;
replacing the LLM Coordinator with a keyword classifier to 0.872; and the
deterministic regex baseline yields 0.634.
We note that the Identifier Canonicalizer is shown 20 ground-truth identifier
examples as naming demonstrations, which may modestly inflate F1\textsubscript{strict}
but does not affect F1\textsubscript{content} since rule identifiers are excluded
from the content scoring function.

A multi-run stability study over 20 independent pipeline runs at temperature~0.5
yields a mean F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.864~$\pm$~0.023 (95\%~CI~$\pm$0.010),
an Inter-Agent Agreement Score (IAAS) of~$0.942 \pm 0.027$, and a
Fleiss~$\kappa~=~0.596$, confirming that 68 of 91 extracted rule concepts
(74.7\%) are stable across stochastic re-runs.
This stable consensus set is subsequently loaded into a Neo4j Knowledge Graph
containing exclusively extracted knowledge (Rule, Sensor, and Station nodes
with governance and directed correlation edges); a runtime audit asserts
that no ground-truth information enters the graph.
The graph is then validated operationally: 211{,}200 raw sensor readings
(22 sensors, 30-second cadence) are analyzed by the rules stored in the
graph, with every detection threshold and parameter taken from the
extracted rules themselves, and the detections are compared post hoc
against the benchmark's 14 annotated anomaly events.
13 of the 14 annotated anomaly events are identified (92.9\% event recall;
event-level precision 100\% under a standard five-minute warning-tier alarm
on-delay), covering all four detectable fault classes.
The sole gap is GT-0009 (CORRELATED), a cross-station fault that is
invisible to any single sensor or extracted rule; three-way evidence fusion
over multiple documentary sources was attempted but could not be implemented
reliably in this benchmark, and GT-0009 is acknowledged as a known
limitation.
Seven of the benchmark's 8 maintenance procedures are covered by extracted
maintenance rules (MAINT-04 is \texttt{RULE\_NOT\_TRIGGERED}: the rule was
extracted but the sustained-pressure condition was never observed in the
timeseries), completing the official iMAKS Phase~2 coverage at 20/22
(90.9\%; pass threshold: 70\%).
A data-driven plausibility check additionally exposed one faulty extracted
rule (a transposed threshold bound), demonstrating that the validation
layer doubles as an automated extraction-quality audit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% § 1.1  Motivates LLM-based KE for industrial SOPs; structural heterogeneity and evaluation blind spot.
\subsection{Context and Motivation}
A radical paradigm shift has been undergone by the field of Knowledge Extraction (KE), evolving from rigid, domain-specific architectures toward fluid, generative paradigms. Historically, the identification of predefined entities and relations within unstructured text was heavily reliant on fixed ontologies, which created significant bottlenecks when processing the vast volume and variety of modern digital data. With the widespread adoption of transformer architectures, manual heuristics have been progressively replaced by deep contextual representations. Consequently, a robust technical foundation has been provided for the conversion of raw digital signals and textual documentation into structured Knowledge Graphs (KGs).

Within modern industrial manufacturing facilities, KGs serve as the critical cognitive backbone for intelligent Digital Twins (DTs). However, high-quality KG construction represents a critical bottleneck for knowledge-driven digital twins, as traditional approaches such as manual ontology engineering or centralized natural language processing (NLP) pipelines fail to scale while maintaining semantic richness. To construct these KGs, operational knowledge must be systematically extracted from heterogeneous sources, primarily Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs). These documents encode essential parameters, including alarm thresholds, maintenance rules, and access policies. Theoretically, all operational logic required to synchronize a digital twin with its physical counterpart is encoded within these texts. In practice, however, the automated extraction of this knowledge is severely obstructed by multiple challenges.

First, a high degree of structural heterogeneity is exhibited by SOPs within a single facility. Critical information is frequently dispersed across fundamentally different layouts; for instance, modern industrial facilities generate knowledge simultaneously in natural language documents, numerical sensor streams, and manufacturer datasheets. The effective processing of these distinct formats cannot be consistently achieved by a single, monolithic extraction prompt, as the information is spatially and semantically structured in fundamentally divergent ways. 

Second, the evaluation of automated extraction in industrial contexts is highly complex. A phenomenon defined as ``semantic aliasing'' is frequently observed, where identical operational constraints are mapped to disparate identifiers. In ground-truth annotations, rule identifiers, such as \texttt{RULE-ST01-01} or \texttt{MAINT-01}, are often established as annotator conventions that rarely appear verbatim in the source text. When traditional evaluation models based primarily on exact-match F1 metrics (such as $\text{F1}_{\text{strict}}$) are applied, taxonomic nomenclature is confused with true semantic accuracy. Consequently, an ``evaluation blind spot'' is created, which risks penalizing operationally valid extractions while simultaneously failing to detect hallucinated but syntactically correct numeric thresholds.

Large Language Models (LLMs) are considered highly attractive for this extraction task, given their capacity to interpret complex natural-language instructions and adapt to new extraction patterns. Nonetheless, significant reliability issues persist; LLMs are prone to hallucinating numeric values absent from the source text and possess a ``black-box'' nature that obscures their decision-making processes. To mitigate the limitations of monolithic generative models, research is increasingly being directed toward collaborative multi-agent architectures. In these advanced frameworks, a collaborative multi-agent paradigm is proposed where specialized software agents, such as document parsers, entity extractors, and ontology alignment agents, are orchestrated to extract, validate, and integrate knowledge across heterogeneous formats. Recent literature highlights several successful implementations of this paradigm; for example, the KARMA framework deploys nine collaborative LLM agents for entity discovery and conflict resolution, while Tag2Graph is explicitly designed to mine KGs from industrial time-series data and tag definitions. 

Bridging the gap between the generative flexibility of LLMs and the deterministic rigor required by industrial SOPs remains a primary experimental frontier. In this thesis, the design, implementation, and evaluation of a collaborative multi-agent system are investigated using the iMAKS (industrial Modular Knowledge extrAction Synthetic) benchmark dataset.

% § 1.2  Gap summary; detailed survey is in Section~\ref{sec:related_work}.
\subsection{Research Gaps}
Although the capacity for generative information extraction has been significantly expanded by multi-agent systems, a systematic evaluation of these pipelines is constrained by a lack of specialized, domain-specific benchmarks. A comprehensive review of the literature, which is presented in detail in Section~\ref{sec:related_work}, indicates an absence of systematic architectures for agent specialization in KG construction, as well as a lack of comparative studies on agent collaboration strategies (e.g., competitive versus cooperative versus hierarchical). Furthermore, limited work has been conducted on incremental KG update mechanisms that preserve semantic consistency, and there is insufficient research on ontology population directly from unstructured industrial documentation.

From an evaluation perspective, existing benchmarks fail to simultaneously address the unique demands of industrial SOP extraction. Specifically, three critical requirements remain unfulfilled by the current state of the art:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A typed rule ontology explicitly mapped to sensor schemas must be provided, ensuring that diverse constraint types are comprehensively covered within a single annotated corpus. This must encompass operational rules, threshold rules, maintenance rules, and access rules.
    \item Mixed-format documentation must be included within the same benchmark evaluation, requiring extraction models to accurately process knowledge from a combination of natural language procedures, PDF datasheets, and MQTT IoT telemetry payloads. Furthermore, complex industrial challenges, such as cross-station correlated anomalies that span multiple independent documents and are invisible to single-sensor rules, must be explicitly represented — even if such cases expose current limitations of automated pipelines.
    \item A content-first evaluation metric (defined as $\text{F1}_{\text{content}}$) must be established, designed to score operational correctness and semantic coherence independently of annotator-defined identifier naming conventions. This metric should incorporate techniques such as Hungarian assignment matching and SBERT cosine similarity on text fields to overcome the critical limitations of exact-match scoring.
\end{itemize}

The iMAKS benchmark dataset and the proposed content-based evaluation metric are utilized in this thesis as direct responses to these identified gaps. By addressing these deficiencies, quantitative and qualitative metrics can be deployed to effectively evaluate the quality, completeness, and usability of automatically constructed KGs for cognitive digital twin applications in industrial domains.

% § 1.3  Four contributions: iMAKS dataset, F1_content metric, grid search, multi-agent pipeline.
\subsection{Contributions and Objectives}
To address these limitations, this work makes four primary contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benchmark:} Introduce the iMAKS (industrial Multi-Agent Knowledge
          extraction Synthetic) dataset~\cite{bernardini2026imaks}, a four-layer
          benchmark simulating five days of operations at a nine-station packaging
          facility. Layer~1 comprises 211{,}200 sensor readings and four SOPs with
          86 manually annotated operational rules across heterogeneous document
          structures (narrative, tabular, matrix, and mixed).
    \item \textbf{Evaluation Metric:} Introduce \(F1_{\text{content}}\), a
          content-first metric based on Hungarian optimal assignment and SBERT
          similarity~\cite{reimers2019sbert} that evaluates rule extraction
          independently of identifier naming conventions, correcting the evaluation
          blind spot inherent to standard exact-match metrics.
    \item \textbf{Grid Search:} Execute a 50-condition grid search across five
          LLMs (4B--20B parameters; all five locally deployed) and ten
          prompting paradigms to establish best single-model performance at
          \(F1_{\text{content}} = 0.728\) and identify cross-type contamination
          as the primary bottleneck for heterogeneous corpora.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Agent Pipeline:} Develop a prompt-chaining pipeline with
          document-type routing and seven specialised LLM agents, achieving
          \(F1_{\text{content}} = 0.864\) (mean over 20 runs; $\sigma = 0.023$),
          a 19\% relative improvement over the best single-model performance,
          demonstrating that document-type-specific prompt engineering is the
          primary driver of extraction quality on structurally heterogeneous
          industrial corpora.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theoretical Background}
\label{sec:background}

In this chapter, the foundational concepts and computational tools upon which the remainder of the thesis is built are introduced. The theoretical framework encompasses several interconnected domains, including deep learning, natural language processing (NLP), agentic artificial intelligence, and Knowledge Graphs (KGs). The primary objective is to provide sufficient theoretical context to elucidate the design decisions detailed in subsequent chapters, ensuring comprehensibility without assuming prior expertise in any specific technological paradigm.

% § 2.1  LLMs: transformers, instruction-following, few-shot, hallucination.
\subsection{Large Language Models}
\label{sec:bg_llm}

Large Language Models (LLMs) are characterized as advanced deep learning models that have been extensively pre-trained on massive textual corpora to understand context and generate human-like responses. The foundational architecture of nearly all modern LLMs is the transformer, which utilizes a self-attention mechanism to process sequences more efficiently and accurately by relating any token in an input to any other token. Various architectures have been developed within this paradigm, including encoder-only models (e.g., BERT) for deep contextual understanding, decoder-only models (e.g., GPT) for text generation, and encoder-decoder models (e.g., T5) for highly flexible text-to-text transformations.

Two properties of particular relevance to this thesis are zero-shot and few-shot in-context learning~\cite{brown2020gpt3}. In zero-shot settings, a model follows task instructions expressed entirely in natural language without any worked examples, enabling novel extraction tasks with no labeled data. Few-shot in-context learning extends this by embedding a small number of worked examples directly in the prompt, guiding the model toward the desired output format or extraction pattern without task-specific fine-tuning or weight updates. Both capabilities render LLMs attractive for knowledge extraction in industrial settings where annotated training corpora are scarce, a direct constraint in the iMAKS evaluation, where only 86 annotated rules are available. Chain-of-Thought prompting~\cite{wei2022cot}, which requires the model to produce explicit intermediate reasoning steps before its final answer, is a complementary technique that improves extraction reliability by making intermediate decisions inspectable. Together, zero-shot prompting, few-shot in-context learning, and Chain-of-Thought reasoning represent application-level enhancements that improve LLM utility without modifying model weights~\cite{katanforoosh2025cs230}, the paradigm this thesis operates within entirely, as no fine-tuning or weight adaptation of any model is performed.

Despite these capabilities, LLMs exhibit significant limitations that are especially consequential in industrial contexts. The most critical is the generation of \emph{hallucinations}~\cite{ji2023hallucination}. Two distinct hallucination modes are distinguished in the literature: \emph{intrinsic hallucination}, where the output directly contradicts information present in the source document, and \emph{extrinsic hallucination}, where the output introduces content that cannot be verified against the source at all. Both are dangerous for industrial SOP extraction, but extrinsic hallucination is particularly insidious: a numeric threshold value invented by the model and absent from the source text will not be flagged by any input-grounding check, yet could cause a safety alarm to fire at an incorrect value or fail to trigger when it should. A second limitation is difficulty with precise numerical reasoning: numeric values embedded in multi-column threshold tables with engineering units are susceptible to transcription errors in which adjacent row values are transposed or incorrect unit suffixes are appended. Third, the ``black-box'' nature of LLM inference makes it difficult to trace why a specific prediction was produced, complicating systematic debugging of extraction errors. The mitigation of these limitations (hallucination control, numeric grounding, and reasoning transparency) are primary design concerns addressed by the multi-agent pipeline proposed in Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}.

For the experimental phase of this thesis, local execution of models was prioritized to ensure data privacy and sovereignty, avoiding reliance on external cloud APIs. As a trade-off, hardware constraints dictated that only models up to approximately 20 billion parameters could be utilized, establishing an upper bound on raw generative capability.

% § 2.2  Knowledge Graphs: triples, ontologies, TBox/ABox, Neo4j, Cypher.
\subsection{Knowledge Graphs and Graph Databases}
\label{sec:bg_kg}

A Knowledge Graph (KG) is defined as a structured representation of knowledge where entities and their relations are linked within a graph. The core structural elements include nodes, which represent entities or concepts, and edges, which denote the relationships between these entities. Properties or attributes are frequently added to provide supplementary factual data, such as dates, locations, or numerical values. KGs provide a semantic framework that supports complex data queries and reasoning, successfully narrowing queries to small data subsets and maintaining high performance regardless of dataset growth. A central design philosophy is to connect \emph{concepts rather than strings}~\cite{sullivan2021kg}, enabling semantic relationship traversal rather than keyword lookup; practical applications of this paradigm include search optimisation, question-answering systems, and recommendation engines. The overall quality and utility of a KG are strictly dependent on the quality of the data fed into it, a principle that directly motivates the content-first evaluation metric introduced in this thesis.

Constructing a KG from unstructured text relies on three core NLP tasks~\cite{cs520stanford}.
\emph{Entity extraction} (Named Entity Recognition, NER) identifies key entities in a document (persons, organizations, locations, sensor identifiers, operational parameters) that become the nodes of the graph.
\emph{Relation extraction} then infers typed relationships between pairs of identified entities from their textual context, producing the labelled edges that encode operational constraints and causal dependencies.
A third task, \emph{entity resolution}, determines whether multiple text mentions refer to the same real-world entity, consolidating duplicate candidate nodes before they are committed to the graph.
Whereas early methods relied on hand-crafted syntactic rules and sequence-labelling models such as Conditional Random Fields, modern approaches adapt pre-trained language models to each extraction task through supervised fine-tuning or, as in this thesis, zero- and few-shot prompting. Two broad construction strategies are distinguished in practice~\cite{sullivan2021kg}: an \emph{NLP-only} approach that extracts subject--verb--object triples directly from text (maximally flexible but susceptible to entity disambiguation errors when the same surface form refers to different entities), and an \emph{NLP-lite} approach that identifies named entities and resolves them against a structured knowledge base such as Wikidata to standardise relationship types (built-in disambiguation, but constrained to pre-specified relation types).

The organization of KG content is heavily influenced by Description Logics, utilizing a bifurcated structure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{TBox} (terminological box) establishes the domain ontology, defining the schema, entity types (e.g., Component, Process, Parameter, Sensor), relationship types (e.g., \textit{part\_of}, \textit{monitors}, \textit{triggers}), and property constraints.
    \item The \textbf{ABox} (assertional box) contains the concrete instance data, representing the operational state of the facility, including specific sensors, anomalous events, and human presence instances.
\end{itemize}

In this thesis, Neo4j is employed as the underlying graph database implementation. Neo4j leverages native graph processing to facilitate complex queries through the Cypher query language. The use of Cypher is essential for advanced analytics, path analysis, and anomaly detection, allowing for the retrieval of multi-hop contextual data required to evaluate anomaly coverage.

A growing body of research explores the interaction between LLMs and KGs more broadly~\cite{pan2024unifying}. Three principal integration paradigms are identified: \emph{LLM-augmented KGs}, where LLMs serve as the primary extraction engine to populate a graph from unstructured text; \emph{KG-augmented LLMs}, where structured graph knowledge is injected into the LLM context to reduce hallucination and improve factual grounding; and \emph{synergized frameworks}, where both modalities reinforce each other iteratively. This thesis primarily instantiates the first paradigm: LLMs populate the iMAKS KG from SOP documents, while incorporating elements of the second: the Validator agent grounds extracted sensor identifiers against the facility's known physical sensor inventory.

% § 2.3  Multi-agent systems: specialisation, LangGraph DAG, fan-out, shared state.
\subsection{Multi-Agent Systems and LangGraph}
\label{sec:bg_mas}

To overcome the limitations of centralized NLP pipelines, which frequently fail to scale while maintaining semantic richness, a collaborative multi-agent paradigm is increasingly adopted. A multi-agent system (MAS) is defined as a software architecture comprising multiple autonomous, specialized components, termed agents, that interact to achieve complex objectives. Various collaboration strategies can be supported by these architectures, including cooperative, competitive, and hierarchical frameworks. 

The primary advantage of a multi-agent approach is agent specialization. Within an extraction pipeline, dedicated agents can be deployed for specific tasks, such as document parsing, domain-specific named entity recognition (NER), relationship extraction, ontology alignment, and validation. Recent literature highlights the efficacy of this approach in industrial contexts. The KARMA framework~\cite{lu2025karma} employs nine collaborative LLM agents to mine a multi-source industrial dataset, extracting 38,230 entities at 83.1\% LLM-verified correctness while achieving an 18.6\% reduction in inter-agent entity conflicts through structured majority voting. Analogous frameworks such as Tag2Graph apply a collaborative paradigm to mine KGs directly from industrial time-series data and tag definitions. Multi-agent architectures have also demonstrated superior performance on standard NER and relation extraction benchmarks relative to single-agent baselines~\cite{chen2024multiagent}. To ensure semantic consistency more broadly, conflict resolution mechanisms frequently utilize majority voting protocols with configurable confidence thresholds to assign composite reliability scores to extracted triples.

The extraction pipeline presented in this thesis is implemented using the LangGraph framework. LangGraph facilitates the organization of agents as a typed directed acyclic graph (DAG) with a shared state dictionary. Key features utilized in this implementation include conditional routing (directing distinct document formats to appropriate specialized extractors), parallel execution of extraction nodes, and sum-reducer state fields that allow concurrent outputs to be aggregated without state overwriting.

% § 2.4  Digital Twins: cyber-physical coupling, SOP rules as governance layer.
\subsection{Industrial Digital Twins and Standard Operating Procedures}
\label{sec:bg_dt}

A Digital Twin is conceptualized as a highly synchronized software model of a physical system, fundamentally requiring sophisticated knowledge graphs to integrate diverse static and dynamic data sources. The architecture of an agentic Digital Twin typically integrates a static model, represented by the knowledge graph defining the industrial system structure, with a dynamic model processing simulated IoT data and operational states. KGs serve as the cognitive backbone of these twins, enabling bidirectional synchronization, incremental updates, and complex what-if reasoning. Sahlab et al.~\cite{sahlab2021kg} demonstrate that KG integration delivers four concrete capabilities for industrial Digital Twins: internal cross-document linking that connects entities across heterogeneous data sources, autonomous knowledge completion that infers missing relationships from existing graph structure, automated error detection in sensor readings by reasoning over expected operational states, and semantic querying of the facility model using ontology-aware graph traversal. Recently, the concept of the ``Digital Twin Agent'' has emerged, incorporating autonomous decision-making and cross-system collaboration to accelerate modeling and resilience in manufacturing environments.

Within industrial facilities, Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) function as the primary governance layer, encoding the operational rules that dictate Digital Twin behavior. These natural language documents specify critical parameters, including continuous numerical measurement thresholds, maintenance rules, and zone access policies. However, the automated extraction of this logic is complicated by the extreme structural heterogeneity of SOPs, which frequently combine narrative prose, complex alarm threshold tables, and personnel access matrices. The translation of these heterogeneous, unstructured documents into verified, machine-readable KG triples forms the central methodological challenge addressed by the multi-agent system developed in this thesis.

% § 3.1  DocRED, DocILE, BREX, SOP-Bench: limitations for industrial SOP extraction.
\subsection{Document-Level Information Extraction and Evaluation}
\label{sec:rw_benchmarks}

The most widely used benchmarks for document-level relation extraction are
DocRED~\cite{yao2019docred} and DocILE~\cite{simsa2023docile}.
DocRED contains approximately 100,000 relational facts extracted from Wikipedia
articles, with cross-sentence dependencies that require multi-paragraph reasoning.
While DocRED pioneered large-scale joint entity and relation extraction datasets, it primarily targets encyclopedic relations without engineering analogs, lacking the logical depth required for industrial constraints.
Conversely, DocILE provides the largest public benchmark for Key Information Localization and Extraction (KILE) and Line Item Recognition (LIR) across over 1,000 unique industrial layouts.
Both have been important for advancing general-purpose information extraction
systems, but neither addresses the characteristics specific to industrial SOP
corpora: numeric sensor thresholds with engineering units, conditional operational
logic distributed across heterogeneous document formats (prose, tables, matrices),
and a need to evaluate extraction quality independently of how identifiers are
named.
Specifically, DocILE focuses on static business entities (e.g., tax totals, addresses) rather than the conditional operational rules and numeric sensor thresholds required for industrial SOP automation.

In the domain of business rules, BREX~\cite{yang2025brex} represents the state-of-the-art for large-scale rule-flow modelling with conditional logic across 30+ domains.
However, BREX ignores tabular structures and numeric engineering units.
Furthermore, it relies on identifier-based F1 evaluation, which assumes that rule
names in the source text match the ground-truth annotation convention.
For industrial SOPs, rule identifiers are typically annotator-assigned rather
than present in the source text, so identifier-based F1 rewards naming convention
alignment as much as extraction quality.
This is the core motivation for the $F1_{content}$ metric introduced
in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.
For regulatory domains, De Jure~\cite{guliani2026dejure} provides a robust framework for deontic logic (obligations and permissions), but it does not account for the physical threshold semantics essential for SCADA-level integration.

SOP-Bench~\cite{nandi2025sopbench} specifically targets industrial procedures and
evaluates whether an agent can correctly \emph{execute} a procedure step-by-step.
This is a complementary task to the one addressed here: SOP-Bench measures task
completion via action sequences, whereas this thesis addresses the prior data-fidelity step of extracting and
structuring the operational rules that an execution agent would need to follow.

% § 3.2  LLM-based KG construction: zero/few-shot, hallucination grounding, PCM.
\subsection{LLM-Based Knowledge Graph Construction}
\label{sec:rw_kgllm}

The use of LLMs to extract graph-structured knowledge from unstructured text has
received significant attention, representing a fundamental paradigm shift from early rule-based systems and probabilistic sequence labelling toward generative extraction enabled by deep bidirectional transformers and attention mechanisms.
Several recent surveys identify multiple interaction patterns between LLMs and
KGs, including LLMs used to populate KGs, KGs used to ground and verify LLM
outputs, and joint architectures.
This thesis primarily falls in the first category, with elements of the second:
the Validator and Judge nodes use the facility's known sensor inventory to
ground extracted sensor identifiers against physically real entities.

Traditional KG construction systems rely on fine-tuned Named Entity Recognition
(NER) and Relation Extraction (RE) models trained on labelled corpora.
These approaches require substantial annotated data, which is not available for
the iMAKS dataset: the ground truth provides only 86 rules, far too few to
fine-tune a dedicated extraction model.
The prompt-engineering approach used here avoids this requirement entirely, leveraging emergent reasoning capabilities to convert raw digital signals into structured relational assets. This allows large language models to function as zero-shot learners or reasoning agents via Chain-of-Thought and ReAct paradigms, even in low-resource scenarios.
The trade-off is that prompt-based LLMs are more sensitive to the exact phrasing
of instructions than a trained model would be, which is part of the reason why
the grid search over prompting paradigms (Section~\ref{sec:grid_search}) was
necessary.
Furthermore, applying knowledge extraction to industrial SOPs remains challenging because manufacturing documents often embed critical data in non-narrative formats. To overcome the structural heterogeneity of these documents, recent research has pivoted towards multi-agent architectures that use specialized agents to coordinate extraction across different formats. For example, TabAgent proposes a multi-agent framework specifically for unstructured table extraction, while SOPRAG utilizes multi-view graph experts to navigate complex SOP hierarchies. Other recent frameworks, such as SPIRES, employ recursive extraction methods that enforce strict schema adherence.

Hallucination control is a known challenge in LLM-based extraction that has
received relatively little attention in the literature for numeric fields
specifically. Populating knowledge graphs from unstructured technical text remains highly error-prone due to "hallucinations" in natural language generation.
In general NLP extraction tasks, a hallucinated entity name is often recoverable
from context; in industrial settings, a hallucinated temperature threshold could
cause a safety alarm to fire at the wrong value or not at all.
The Physical Constraint Mapping (PCM) discipline introduced in this thesis
(Section~\ref{sec:miners}) is a set of prompt-level grounding instructions
designed to address the most dangerous hallucination patterns observed during the
grid-search phase: inventing numeric threshold values, appending units not
present in the source, and borrowing values from neighbouring rows or sentences.

% § 3.3  Knowledge Graphs for Industrial Digital Twins: manually constructed vs. auto-populated.
\subsection{Knowledge Graphs for Industrial Digital Twins}
\label{sec:rw_dt_kg}

The integration of Knowledge Graphs with industrial Digital Twins has been
studied in the context of fault detection, predictive maintenance, and process
monitoring. Knowledge Graphs serve as the essential semantic backbone for Intelligent Digital Twins by structurally linking scattered technical data.
A recurring pattern in this literature is the use of an ontology-based KG
to link sensor readings to the operational rules that govern them, enabling
graph-based queries such as ``which rule covers this anomaly event?''.

A common limitation in this body of work is that the KG content (the
operational rules, threshold values, and causal relationships) is populated
manually by domain experts or from structured databases.
Automating this step from unstructured SOP documents, which is the contribution
of this thesis, has not been directly addressed.
While recent literature demonstrates that agentic workflows are superior for automating SOP execution, a systematic evaluation of these extraction pipelines is hindered by a lack of specialized benchmarks. Current evaluation infrastructures, such as KGpipe or Plumber, offer modular extraction pipelines but notably lack the manufacturing-specific ground truth necessary to validate SCADA-level constraints.

\section{System Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

Before describing each component in detail, this section gives a high-level
picture of the full pipeline so that the later sections are easier to follow.

The system takes four SOP PDF files as input and produces a populated Neo4j
Knowledge Graph as output.
The processing is organised in four layers, each described in a dedicated section:

\begin{description}
    \item[Layer 1: Document Parsing (Section~\ref{sec:unstructured_parsing})]
          The four SOP PDFs are converted into clean plain-text files using
          IBM's \texttt{docling} library for layout-aware extraction, followed
          by normalisation (removing OCR artefacts, fixing encoding errors) and
          context-aware chunking into segments of manageable size for the LLMs.

    \item[Layer 2a: Single-Agent Grid Search (Section~\ref{sec:grid_search})]
          Before designing the multi-agent pipeline, I ran a systematic grid search
          across five locally-deployed LLMs (4B--20B parameters) and ten prompting
          paradigms to establish single-agent performance baselines and to understand
          which document types are hardest for individual models.
          The grid search revealed that no single model-prompt combination
          achieves high quality across all four document types simultaneously,
          which directly motivated the multi-agent design.

    \item[Layer 2b: Multi-Agent Extraction Pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline})]
          A seven-node LangGraph graph that routes each document to a specialised
          extractor based on its structure (narrative, tabular, matrix, or mixed),
          merges the parallel extractor outputs deterministically, and applies an
          LLM-based Validator and a Quality Gap Evaluator with a one-cycle retry
          mechanism.

    \item[Layer 3: Evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation})]
          Extracted rules are evaluated against the 86-rule ground truth using two
          metrics: F1\textsubscript{strict} (exact identifier match, reported for
          reference) and F1\textsubscript{content} (content-first similarity using
          Hungarian assignment and SBERT, the primary metric).

    \item[Layer 4: Knowledge Graph (Section~\ref{sec:neo4j})]
          The 68 stable consensus rules are loaded into a Neo4j Knowledge
          Graph containing exclusively extracted knowledge; the rules in the
          graph then analyze the raw sensor timeseries (211{,}200 readings)
          to detect anomaly events, which are verified post hoc against the
          benchmark's 22 Phase~2 evaluation targets (14 annotated anomaly
          events and 8 maintenance procedures).
\end{description}

This layered structure reflects the actual development order of the project.
The grid search informed which models and prompts to use in the multi-agent
pipeline.
The ablation study (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) identified which pipeline
components contribute most to quality.
The multi-run stability study (Section~\ref{sec:multirun}) determined which
extracted rules are reliable enough to load into a production Knowledge Graph
without human review.

\section{Unstructured Data Parsing Strategy}
\label{sec:unstructured_parsing}

% § 2.1  Role of the Document Parser; connects iMAKS SOP PDFs to the extraction pipeline.
\subsection{Overview of the Document Parser}
The four iMAKS Layer~1 SOP documents (SOP-001 through SOP-004) are distributed as
PDF files, each encoding operational rules in a structurally distinct format:
narrative prose, threshold tables, mixed maintenance text, and role-zone access
matrices. Before any LLM-based rule extraction can occur, these files must be
converted into clean, semantically coherent plain text. Standard left-to-right
PDF parsers fail this requirement: they flatten table structures into whitespace-
separated tokens and discard hierarchical headings, making the output semantically
incoherent for downstream extraction agents.

The Document Parser is the first agent in the
pipeline. It accepts a raw SOP PDF as input and produces a sanitised plain-text
file ready for the LLM extractors. It implements three sequential stages: vision-aware
Markdown conversion, deterministic artifact sanitisation, and context-aware chunking.

% § 2.2  IBM docling bounding-box extraction for PDFs; raw read for .txt files.
\subsection{Vision-Aware Markdown Extraction}
To preserve spatial and semantic layout, the Document Parser uses IBM's
\texttt{docling} library, which applies a vision-language model to identify
bounding boxes around text blocks, tables, and headers, exporting the
reconstructed layout directly into Markdown. This is critical for the iMAKS
corpus: SOP-002's threshold table and SOP-004's role-zone permission matrix
carry their operational meaning in their grid structure, which a text-only parser
would destroy. For plain-text inputs (\texttt{.txt}), the parser bypasses vision
extraction and reads the raw text directly, then applies the same normalisation
and chunking pipeline.

% § 2.3  _normalise protocol: ligature fixes, HTML decoding, escaped-character scrubbing.
\subsection{Normalisation and Hallucination Scrubbing}
Vision-based extraction preserves layout but introduces OCR artifacts and
vision-model hallucinations. The parser executes a strict normalisation protocol
(\texttt{\_normalise}) to sanitise the raw Markdown before it enters the LLM
context window.

% § 2.3.1  Deterministic ligature-to-symbol map (e.g. "fi" → →, "fl" → ↓).
\subsubsection{Ligature and Encoding Correction}
The iMAKS SOP files contain directional and trend symbols that are corrupted by
PDF font encoding: the sequence \texttt{fi} is deterministically remapped to a
right-arrow ($\rightarrow$) and \texttt{fl} to a down-arrow ($\downarrow$),
matching the original SOP semantics. HTML entities are decoded and escaped
formatting characters (e.g.\ \texttt{\\\_}) are sanitised to prevent Markdown
injection errors in downstream prompts.

% § 2.3.2  Regex-based _deduplicate_table_cells: collapses repeated cell content from overlapping bounding boxes.
\subsubsection{Table Cell Deduplication}
A recurring hallucination in layout-aware extraction occurs when overlapping
bounding boxes cause cell text to be repeated sequentially
(e.g., ``Inspect and lubricate HIGH Inspect and lubricate HIGH'').
The parser resolves this with a regex-based algorithm
(\texttt{\_deduplicate\_table\_cells}) that scans Markdown table rows (identified
by pipe \texttt{|} delimiters), detects any substring of three or more characters
that repeats itself consecutively, and collapses it to a single instance, without
invoking a secondary LLM call.

% § 2.4  _split_into_chunks: double-newline splits, max 2,000 chars, min 200 chars (merged backward).
\subsection{Context-Aware Chunking Strategy}
Supplying an entire SOP document as a single LLM prompt degrades extraction
performance; splitting arbitrarily mid-sentence destroys semantic meaning.
The parser implements \texttt{\_split\_into\_chunks}, which splits strictly at
double newlines reliable paragraph and section boundaries in Markdown then
merges adjacent paragraphs into a rolling buffer capped at 2\,000 characters.
If the final chunk is shorter than 200 characters it is merged backward into the
preceding chunk to avoid uninformative microscopic segments.

The fully sanitised and chunked text is reassembled and saved as a
\texttt{.txt} file (e.g.\ \texttt{SOP-001.txt}), which serves as the direct
input to the grid search extraction step described in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This chapter details the full research methodology underlying the thesis.
It describes the benchmark dataset used to evaluate all systems, the experimental
design and hardware constraints, the evaluation framework including the
two-tier metric introduced in this work, the ablation study design, and the
statistical approach used to quantify pipeline stability.
Individual system components, the document parser, the single-agent grid
search, and the multi-agent pipeline, are described in the sections that follow;
this chapter focuses on the \emph{how} and \emph{why} of measurement and
experimental design rather than the systems themselves.

% § 3.1  Research Design Overview
\subsection{Research Design and Experimental Overview}
\label{sec:research_design}

The thesis follows a four-layer experimental design, progressing from raw
document ingestion through structured Knowledge Graph construction and formal
benchmark evaluation.
The four layers correspond to the major phases of the work:

\begin{description}
    \item[\textbf{Layer 1: Document Ingestion and Parsing.}]
          The four SOP PDF documents from the iMAKS benchmark are converted into
          clean, semantically structured plain text using a vision-aware extraction
          pipeline built on IBM's \texttt{docling} library
          (Section~\ref{sec:unstructured_parsing}).
          This layer is a prerequisite for all downstream extraction steps.

    \item[\textbf{Layer 2a: Single-Agent Baseline Establishment.}]
          A systematic grid search over five locally-deployed LLMs
          (4\,B--20\,B parameters) and ten prompting paradigms establishes the
          performance ceiling of single-agent approaches on iMAKS
          (Section~\ref{sec:grid_search}).
          The grid search identifies which models perform best on each document
          type, directly informing the model assignments in the multi-agent
          pipeline.

    \item[\textbf{Layer 2b: Multi-Agent Extraction Pipeline.}]
          A seven-node LangGraph pipeline with document-type routing, deterministic
          deduplication, LLM-based validation, and a quality-gap evaluator with a
          one-cycle retry mechanism is designed, implemented, and evaluated
          (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}).
          Ablation variants and a 20-run stability study quantify each
          component's contribution and the pipeline's reliability under natural
          LLM non-determinism (Sections~\ref{sec:ablation} and~\ref{sec:multirun}).

    \item[\textbf{Layer 3: Evaluation.}]
          All extraction systems are scored against the iMAKS ground truth using
          the two-tier metric framework defined in this chapter.

    \item[\textbf{Layer 4: Knowledge Graph Construction.}]
          The 68~stable consensus rules produced by the multi-run stability study
          are loaded into a Neo4j Knowledge Graph that contains exclusively
          extracted knowledge; the rules in the graph then analyze the raw
          sensor timeseries to detect anomaly events, which are verified
          against the benchmark's 22 Phase~2 evaluation targets, 14
          annotated anomaly events and 8 maintenance procedures
          (Section~\ref{sec:neo4j}).
\end{description}

All experiments rely exclusively on prompt engineering and architectural
design decisions.
No training, fine-tuning, or weight adaptation of any model was performed.
This design reflects two practical constraints: the limited size of the annotated
ground truth (86~rules) makes task-specific fine-tuning statistically untenable,
and the research objective is to assess the capability of off-the-shelf,
locally-deployable LLMs for industrial-grade knowledge extraction.

% § 3.2  Benchmark Dataset: iMAKS
\subsection{Benchmark Dataset: The iMAKS Corpus}
\label{sec:benchmark}

All experiments are conducted on the iMAKS (industrial Multi-Agent Knowledge
extraction Synthetic) benchmark dataset~\cite{bernardini2026imaks}, a
four-layer evaluation resource simulating five days of operations at a
nine-station packaging facility.

\subsubsection{Motivation for Benchmark Choice}
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_landscape}, no publicly available benchmark
simultaneously provides (a)~typed industrial rules with numeric sensor thresholds,
(b)~structurally heterogeneous source documents requiring mixed-format extraction,
and (c)~a content-first evaluation target that assesses operational correctness
independently of identifier naming conventions.
iMAKS was designed to fill precisely these three gaps, making it the most
appropriate benchmark for evaluating the systems developed here.
Its synthetic construction ensures that ground-truth annotations are complete
and unambiguous; a real industrial SOP corpus would introduce annotation noise
and confidentiality constraints orthogonal to the extraction problem studied here.

\subsubsection{Facility Structure and Sensor Data}
The simulated facility consists of nine stations organised into six zones:
four production stations (\texttt{ST01\_FILLING}, \texttt{ST02\_SEALING},
\texttt{ST03\_LABELLING}, \texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING}) and five auxiliary stations
(\texttt{SRV01\_SERVERROOM}, \texttt{WRH01\_WAREHOUSE},
\texttt{CHM01\_CHEMICALSTORAGE}, \texttt{RND01\_RDLAB}, \texttt{CAF01\_CAFETERIA}).
Twenty-two physical sensors are deployed across these stations, producing
211\,200 raw time-series readings that constitute Layer~1 of the dataset.

\subsubsection{SOP Document Corpus and Structural Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:sop_corpus}
The four SOP documents (SOP-001 through SOP-004) are the primary extraction target.
Each encodes operational knowledge in a structurally distinct format:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SOP-001 (narrative):}
          Plain procedural prose describing operational conditions, process control
          actions, and zone-level safety constraints.
          Rules are embedded in free-form sentences with no table structure or
          column delimiters.
          The extraction challenge is purely semantic: the model must identify
          which sentence fragment constitutes a conditional operational constraint
          and parse its subject (the sensor or station), predicate (the threshold
          or condition), and action.

    \item \textbf{SOP-002 (tabular):}
          A sensor-threshold table with one row per sensor and four numeric threshold
          columns (\texttt{CRIT\_LO}, \texttt{WARN\_LO}, \texttt{WARN\_HI},
          \texttt{CRIT\_HI}) plus a measurement unit column.
          The extraction task is more mechanical than SOP-001, amounting to column-to-field
          mapping, but OCR artefacts from PDF vision-extraction, unit-suffix handling,
          and partial rows (sensors with critical but no warning limits) introduce
          non-trivial failure modes.
          SOP-002 is the source of all \texttt{ThresholdRule} ground-truth entries.

    \item \textbf{SOP-003 (mixed):}
          A structured table whose rows encode maintenance procedures and
          correlated-fault patterns as (trigger condition, required action, priority)
          triples.
          The document is classified as \textit{mixed} because it combines the
          structured tabular layout of SOP-002 with the semantic complexity of
          SOP-001: each row requires the model to read a multi-clause trigger
          condition and a multi-step action description.
          SOP-003 is also the source of the critical cross-station causal rule
          (RULE-CORR-01) that links a sealing motor overload event at
          \texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_CUR} to a packaging speed anomaly at
          \texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_SPD}, representing the kind of multi-document
          correlated-fault pattern that the benchmark explicitly tests.

    \item \textbf{SOP-004 (matrix):}
          A role-zone access-control and occupancy matrix.
          Rows represent facility zones and columns represent access-permission
          classes or acknowledgment deadlines.
          Rules are encoded in individual cells rather than in sentences or table
          rows, requiring the extractor to correctly associate each cell value
          with its row label (the zone) and its column label (the permission type
          or deadline class).
          Cross-cell inference, where a model borrows a value from an adjacent cell,
          is the primary failure mode for this document type.
\end{itemize}

This intentional structural heterogeneity is the central experimental challenge.
A single prompt optimised for narrative prose systematically under-extracts from
tabular and matrix documents; conversely, a prompt optimised for structured rows
produces poor results on prose-based SOPs.
This cross-type contamination, observed empirically during the grid search, is
the primary motivation for the document-type routing approach in the multi-agent
pipeline.

\subsubsection{Ground-Truth Rule Schema}
\label{sec:gt_schema}
The iMAKS benchmark provides 86~manually annotated ground-truth rules distributed
across four classes.
Each rule is a structured record with thirteen fields:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Identifier:} \texttt{ruleId}, a human-assigned
          naming-convention string (e.g., \texttt{RULE-ST01-01},
          \texttt{MAINT-04}, \texttt{RULE-THR-ST02-TMP-CRIT}).
    \item \textbf{Categorical fields:} \texttt{class}, \texttt{station},
          \texttt{sensor}, \texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity}, \texttt{unit}.
    \item \textbf{Numeric threshold fields:} \texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi},
          \texttt{warnLo}, \texttt{critLo}.
    \item \textbf{Text fields:} \texttt{condition}, \texttt{action}.
\end{itemize}

The four rule classes and their semantic roles are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{ThresholdRule}: numeric alarm boundaries for a specific sensor
          at a specific station, sourced primarily from SOP-002.
          The key operational content is the combination of
          sensor identity and numeric threshold values.
    \item \texttt{OperationalRule}: condition-action rules governing process
          control (e.g., ``if filling flow drops below 105\,L/min, halt the
          filling cycle''), sourced primarily from SOP-001.
          The key operational content is the textual condition and action.
    \item \texttt{MaintenanceRule}: scheduled or reactive maintenance procedures
          triggered by sensor fault patterns, concentrated in SOP-003.
          The key operational content is the trigger condition and the
          prescribed maintenance procedure.
    \item \texttt{AccessRule}: zone occupancy limits, access permissions, and
          acknowledgment deadlines, sourced from SOP-004.
          The key operational content is the zone identifier and the
          access or timing constraint.
\end{itemize}

A key characteristic of the annotations is that \texttt{ruleId} values are
annotator-assigned conventions that frequently do not appear verbatim in the
source SOP text.
An extraction system cannot reproduce the exact annotation string without
prior knowledge of the annotation convention; this is the fundamental motivation
for the content-first evaluation metric defined in
Section~\ref{sec:metrics}.
Together, these 86~annotated rules constitute the iMAKS \textbf{Phase~1} evaluation ground truth, against which all extraction results in this thesis are scored.

\subsubsection{Phase~2 Validation Targets}
\label{sec:phase2_targets}
In addition to the Phase~1 extraction ground truth, iMAKS provides
\textbf{22~operational evaluation targets} for a second phase that assesses
the constructed Knowledge Graph rather than the extraction output:
\textbf{14~annotated anomaly events} across five fault types
(\texttt{SPIKE}, \texttt{DRIFT}, \texttt{STUCK}, \texttt{OUT\_OF\_RANGE},
\texttt{CORRELATED}), each defined by the affected sensor, a start and end
timestamp, a severity, and a fault magnitude, and \textbf{8~maintenance
procedures}, each defined by the prescribed action and the sensor whose
fault pattern triggers it.
Each target is assigned a status of COVERED or GAP, and the official pass
threshold is $\geq 70\%$ coverage over all 22 targets.

The validation protocol adopted in this thesis
(Section~\ref{sec:neo4j}) requires the rules stored in the Knowledge Graph
to rediscover the anomaly events from the raw sensor timeseries alone.
An event counts as \textbf{identified} (and thus COVERED) only if a
rule-triggered detection on the same sensor overlaps the annotated event
window; an event for which no such detection exists is a \textbf{gap}, and
detections that overlap no annotated event are \textbf{false positives}.
Maintenance procedures, which concern scheduled human actions rather than
sensor-observable events, are evaluated structurally through the official
\emph{covers} relation: a procedure is COVERED when an extracted
maintenance rule in the graph governs the sensor that triggers it.
The hardest evaluation target is GT-0009, a \texttt{CORRELATED} anomaly at
\texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_SPD} caused by a current drift at
\texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_CUR}: the affected sensor never crosses any extracted
alarm bound and no single-sensor rule can fire on the implicated sensor.
Detection would require combining a temporal co-occurrence signal from the
raw timeseries with causal claims extracted from two independent SOP
documents — a three-way evidence fusion that proved infeasible to implement
reliably in this benchmark.
GT-0009 is therefore acknowledged as a known gap (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

% § 3.3  Experimental Conditions
\subsection{Experimental Conditions}
\label{sec:experimental_conditions}

\subsubsection{Hardware and Local Deployment}
All LLM inference in this thesis is performed on consumer hardware equipped
with a single GPU.
Models are served through either \texttt{Ollama} (for open-weight models in
GGUF format) or \texttt{LM Studio} (for models distributed in GGUF or MLX
format), accessed via an OpenAI-compatible REST API at
\texttt{http://localhost:11434} or \texttt{http://localhost:1234}.
This deployment choice reflects two design constraints: full data sovereignty
(no SOP content is transmitted to external cloud services) and the research
objective of evaluating locally-deployable models specifically.
A practical consequence is that only models up to approximately 20~billion
parameters can be hosted within available GPU memory, establishing an upper
bound on raw generative capacity.

\subsubsection{Language Models}
Five language models are evaluated in the grid search
(Section~\ref{sec:grid_search}), spanning the 4\,B--20\,B parameter range:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{qwen/qwen3-4b-2507} (4\,B, Ollama): a Qwen3-series
          instruction-tuned model.
          The smallest model evaluated; fast inference but limited multi-step
          reasoning capacity.
          Used in the multi-agent pipeline for lightweight classification
          and identifier canonicalisation tasks.
    \item \texttt{mistral-3:8b} (8\,B, Ollama): achieved the best precision-recall
          balance for structured table-row extraction in the grid search, making
          it the designated tabular extractor in the pipeline.
    \item \texttt{gemma3:12b} (12\,B, Ollama): achieved the highest strict F1
          (0.702) and precision (0.815) among all single-agent few-shot-static
          runs; designated as the narrative and matrix extractor.
    \item \texttt{mistral-3:14b} (14\,B, Ollama): strong instruction-following
          capability; designated as the Validator and Quality Gap Evaluator in
          the pipeline.
    \item \texttt{gpt-oss:20b} (${\approx}$20\,B, LM Studio): the largest model
          evaluated.
          Achieves the best single-agent
          F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.728 (few-shot static) but requires the
          most GPU memory per inference call.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Note on Mistral chat templates.}
The two Mistral-series models (\texttt{mistral-3:14b}, \texttt{mistral-3:8b})
do not support a dedicated system message role in their chat template.
For these models, the system message is automatically merged into the leading
user turn at call time, preserving full prompt semantics without requiring
paradigm-specific prompt adjustments.

\subsubsection{Temperature, Seed, and Reproducibility}
All grid-search conditions and the deterministic pipeline run use temperature
$T = 0$ and random seed 42, supplied both as a standard OpenAI API parameter
and as an Ollama-specific \texttt{options.seed} field.
Setting $T = 0$ forces the model to greedily select the most probable token
at each step, making outputs fully deterministic for a fixed model and prompt.
The fixed seed controls any residual stochasticity in the inference server's
sampling implementation.
Reproducibility at $T = 0$ is essential for comparing prompting paradigms and
model assignments without confounding from stochastic variation.

The 20-run multi-run stability study (Section~\ref{sec:multirun}) deliberately
departs from this: it uses $T = 0.5$ with no fixed seed, so each of the 20~runs
draws a genuinely independent sample.
Temperature $0.5$ introduces mild but real stochasticity: the model does not
greedily follow the single most probable path, but the distribution is not so
diffuse as to produce random-walk outputs.
This setting reflects the type of variability expected in a production deployment
where temperature is typically set above zero to prevent degenerate repetition.

\subsubsection{Context Window and Input Truncation}
The KV-cache context window (\texttt{LLM\_NUM\_CTX}) is set to 16\,384 tokens
for all Ollama calls, and the maximum output token count
(\texttt{MAX\_OUTPUT\_TOKENS}) is set to 16\,384.
These values were determined empirically by observing where truncations occurred
on the consumer hardware used: extracting all rules from a dense SOP as a JSON
array can require several thousand output tokens, and the combined system prompt
and SOP text can approach 3\,000--5\,000 input tokens.
The generous allocation ensures neither input nor output is silently truncated.
Each SOP document is additionally capped at 8\,000 characters
(\texttt{SOP\_TEXT\_LIMIT}) before being passed to any LLM node; in practice,
all four iMAKS SOPs fit within this limit without truncation, so the constant
acts as a safety ceiling.

% § 3.4  Evaluation Metric Design
\subsection{Evaluation Metric Design: Motivation and Framework}
\label{sec:metric_design_overview}

\subsubsection{Limitations of Standard Exact-Match Metrics}
\label{sec:eval_limitations}

The conventional approach to evaluating information extraction systems is to
compute F1 against a ground-truth annotation using exact string matching.
Applied to rule extraction, the natural analogue is identifier-based F1:
a predicted rule is a true positive if and only if its \texttt{ruleId} exactly
matches a ground-truth entry.
This metric, referred to here as F1\textsubscript{strict}, suffers from a
fundamental ceiling in the iMAKS setting that makes it unsuitable as the
primary evaluation criterion.

The iMAKS ground-truth \texttt{ruleId} values are annotator-assigned naming
conventions (e.g., \texttt{RULE-ST01-01} for the first rule at station~1,
\texttt{MAINT-04} for the fourth maintenance rule) that are typically absent
from the source SOP text.
Many SOPs label rules only implicitly or not at all; those that do use
heterogeneous naming schemes that partially but not fully overlap with the
annotation convention.
An extraction system therefore cannot reliably reproduce the exact annotation
string without prior knowledge of the convention, regardless of how accurately
it captures the operational content.

The consequence is a systematic evaluation blind spot: a system that extracts
a semantically perfect rule, with the correct sensor, station, threshold values,
and action text, may receive a false-negative because it assigned a plausible
but non-matching identifier (e.g., \texttt{RULE-ST01-TEMP-CRIT} instead of
\texttt{RULE-THR-ST01-TMP-CRIT}).
Conversely, a system that hallucinated an incorrect threshold value but guessed
the correct identifier would receive a true positive.
F1\textsubscript{strict} therefore conflates extraction quality with
naming-convention alignment, which are largely independent properties.

\subsubsection{Design Principles for F1\textsubscript{content}}
\label{sec:f1content_rationale}

The primary metric introduced in this thesis, F1\textsubscript{content}, is
designed around four principles:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Content primacy.}
          The evaluation must reflect whether the extracted rule conveys the
          same operational constraint as the ground-truth rule.
          Identifier strings are deliberately excluded from the scoring function.

    \item \textbf{Class-dependent field weighting.}
          Different fields carry different operational significance depending on
          the rule class.
          A correct \texttt{critHi} value is the central quality signal for a
          \texttt{ThresholdRule}, whereas a precise \texttt{condition} text
          carries the most weight for an \texttt{OperationalRule}.
          The metric must reflect these class-specific priorities through
          non-uniform field weights.

    \item \textbf{Optimal one-to-one assignment.}
          Evaluation must use a globally optimal bipartite matching between the
          predicted and ground-truth rule sets to prevent double-counting and
          to produce principled precision and recall values.

    \item \textbf{Partial credit with a hard TP threshold.}
          Rules that are mostly but not perfectly correct should receive partial
          credit via a continuous content-agreement score.
          The final true-positive decision should nonetheless be based on a
          threshold, to avoid inflating F1 through accumulation of many
          low-quality near-matches.
\end{enumerate}

% § 3.5  Two-Tier Metric Definitions
\subsection{Two Metrics: Strict Identifier Match and Content-First Similarity}
\label{sec:metrics}

The evaluation framework employs two complementary metrics, both implemented in
the rule evaluation module:

\textbf{F1\textsubscript{strict}} (secondary, reported for reference): a
conventional exact-match metric based on rule identifiers.
After normalising \texttt{ruleId} strings (lowercasing and removing all
non-alphanumeric characters), true positives are counted as the intersection of
the ground-truth and predicted identifier sets.
Precision, recall, and F1 are computed in the standard way.
This metric has an inherent ceiling in the iMAKS setting, as described above,
that limits its suitability as the primary criterion.

\textbf{F1\textsubscript{content}} (primary): a content-first metric that
ignores identifiers entirely and evaluates how well the predicted rule content
matches the ground-truth rule content.
It uses a class-weighted field-level agreement score aggregated via the
Hungarian optimal assignment algorithm, then counts true-positive matches at
or above a content-agreement threshold of $0.6$ to compute precision, recall,
and F1.
This metric directly answers the operationally meaningful question:
\emph{Does the extracted rule convey the same operational constraint as the
ground-truth rule?}

% § 3.6  Field Definitions and Class-Specific Weights
\subsection{Field Definitions and Class-Specific Weights}
\label{sec:field_weights}

The iMAKS rule schema contains twelve scored content fields.
The thirteenth field, \texttt{ruleId}, is explicitly excluded from
F1\textsubscript{content}: it represents an annotator-assigned naming convention
rather than operational content, and its contribution to rule identity is already
captured by the categorical and numeric fields that describe the same physical
entity.
The twelve scored fields are grouped into three types by their similarity
function:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Text fields:} \texttt{condition}, \texttt{action}
\item \textbf{Numeric fields:} \texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo},
      \texttt{critLo}
\item \textbf{Categorical fields:} \texttt{class}, \texttt{station},
      \texttt{sensor}, \texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity}, \texttt{unit}
\end{itemize}

Different rule classes place emphasis on fundamentally different fields.
A \texttt{ThresholdRule} is operationally defined by its numeric alarm boundaries;
an incorrect \texttt{critHi} value is a safety-critical error regardless of how
well the other fields match.
An \texttt{OperationalRule} is defined by the correctness of its
\texttt{condition} and \texttt{action} texts: the model must understand what
physical state triggers the rule and what response is required.
A \texttt{MaintenanceRule} similarly centres on condition and action, with the
sensor field carrying extra weight because the maintenance trigger is
sensor-specific.
An \texttt{AccessRule} is defined by its zone (\texttt{station}) and the
access constraint in \texttt{condition}.

To capture these priorities, the framework defines a static set of \emph{raw}
weights $w_f(c)$ for each field $f$ and class $c$, shown in
Table~\ref{tab:class_weights}.
When computing content agreement, only the fields that are non-empty in the
ground-truth rule contribute; their weights are summed to form the denominator,
so weights are never pre-normalised.
Fields absent from a given class (marked with a dash in the table) are omitted
from both numerator and denominator.

The weight assignments reflect the following operational priorities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For \texttt{ThresholdRule}, the primary weights are on the critical
          threshold fields (\texttt{critHi}~=~3.0, \texttt{critLo}~=~3.0) and
          the sensor identifier (\texttt{sensor}~=~2.5), because an incorrect
          critical threshold or misidentified sensor renders the alarm
          operationally useless.
          Warning thresholds receive lower weights (1.5 each) because
          warning-tier violations, while important, are less safety-critical.
    \item For \texttt{OperationalRule}, the dominant weight is on
          \texttt{condition}~(4.0) because the triggering condition carries the
          core operational logic; \texttt{action}~(2.0) and \texttt{station}~(2.0)
          receive secondary weights.
          Numeric threshold fields are absent for this class because operational
          rules do not define alarm bands.
    \item For \texttt{MaintenanceRule}, \texttt{condition}~(3.5) and
          \texttt{sensor}~(2.5) dominate, as maintenance rules are triggered by
          sensor-specific fault signatures.
    \item For \texttt{AccessRule}, \texttt{station}~(2.0) and
          \texttt{condition}~(2.0) share the highest weights, as an access rule
          is defined by the zone it governs and the constraint it enforces.
\end{itemize}

A fallback weight vector (\texttt{class=1.0}, \texttt{station=1.5},
\texttt{sensor=1.5}, \texttt{severity=1.0}, \texttt{condition=2.0},
\texttt{action=1.0}) is used if a rule's \texttt{class} field is missing or
unrecognised, providing a reasonable default that prioritises location and
content fields.

% § 3.7  Per-field scoring: categorical (exact), numeric (relative tolerance), text (SBERT cosine).
\subsection{Field-Level Similarity Functions}
\label{sec:similarity_funcs}

Each field contributes a scalar similarity score $s_f \in [0,1]$ between the
ground-truth value $a$ and the extracted value $b$.
Three distinct scoring functions are used, selected by field type:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Categorical fields} (exact match):
      \[
        s_f =
        \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \mathrm{strip}(a).\mathrm{lower}() =
                          \mathrm{strip}(b).\mathrm{lower}() \\
                      0 & \text{otherwise}
        \end{cases}
      \]
      Exact matching is appropriate for categorical fields because minor
      misspellings or capitalisation variants indicate genuine extraction errors
      rather than acceptable paraphrases.
      The fields \texttt{class}, \texttt{station}, \texttt{sensor},
      \texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity}, and \texttt{unit} are all scored
      this way.
      Using exact matching also prevents lookalike errors
      (e.g., \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_TMP} vs.\ \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_PRS}) from
      receiving partial credit; such errors are operationally catastrophic because
      they cause the wrong physical sensor to be governed by the rule.

\item \textbf{Numeric fields} (relative tolerance):
      \[
        s_f = \max\!\left(0,\; 1 - \frac{|a - b|}{\max(|a|, |b|, \varepsilon)}\right)
      \]
      where $a$ is the ground-truth value, $b$ is the extracted value, and
      $\varepsilon$ is a small positive constant preventing division by zero.
      This formulation assigns $s_f = 1$ for identical values and decays
      continuously toward~0 as the relative error grows.
      It is more permissive than exact match, accommodating minor rounding
      differences in how numeric values are textually represented, yet more
      discriminating than a binary threshold, which would treat a 50\% threshold
      error the same as a 1\% error.
      The fields \texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo}, and
      \texttt{critLo} are scored this way.

\item \textbf{Text fields} (SBERT cosine similarity):
      $s_f = \cos(\mathbf{e}_a,\, \mathbf{e}_b)$,
      where $\mathbf{e}_a$ and $\mathbf{e}_b$ are the SBERT
      embeddings~\cite{reimers2019sbert} of the ground-truth and extracted
      strings, respectively.
      Cosine similarity of SBERT embeddings captures semantic equivalence
      between paraphrases: for instance, ``Fill flow drops below 105\,L/min''
      and ``Flow~$\leq$~105\,L/min at the filling station'' describe the same
      operational constraint and would receive a high similarity score despite
      sharing few exact tokens.
      The \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model from the Sentence-Transformers library
      is used for embedding.
      This 22-million-parameter distilled model is fine-tuned for semantic
      textual similarity; it provides a strong trade-off between embedding quality
      and computational cost, making it practical for batch-encoding the hundreds
      of rule text values across all evaluation runs.
      Only \texttt{condition} and \texttt{action} are scored with SBERT;
      all categorical fields use exact matching as described above, since even
      minor category mismatches (e.g., wrong \texttt{class} value) represent
      structural extraction errors for which partial credit would be misleading.
\end{itemize}

% § 3.8  CA formula: weighted sum of active field scores divided by sum of active weights (Eq. 1).
\subsection{Content Agreement Score}
\label{sec:content_agreement}

For a pair of ground-truth rule $r_{\text{gt}}$ and extracted rule $r_{\text{ext}}$,
the content agreement $CA$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:content_agreement}
CA(r_{\text{gt}}, r_{\text{ext}}) =
\frac{
  \displaystyle\sum_{f \in F_{\text{gt}}} w_f(c) \cdot s_f(r_{\text{gt}}, r_{\text{ext}})
}{
  \displaystyle\sum_{f \in F_{\text{gt}}} w_f(c)
}
\end{equation}

where $F_{\text{gt}}$ is the set of scored fields that are non-empty in the
ground-truth rule, $c$ is the ground-truth rule class, and $w_f(c)$ are the
class-specific field weights from Table~\ref{tab:class_weights} (or the fallback
vector).

This formulation has several important properties that make it appropriate for
industrial rule evaluation.
First, by summing only over fields that are non-empty in the ground-truth rule,
the denominator adapts to each rule's actual content: a \texttt{ThresholdRule}
that specifies only critical limits (and not warning limits) is not penalised
for absent warning fields.
Second, using the ground-truth class $c$ to look up weights means that a
misclassified rule, for example an \texttt{OperationalRule} extracted with
\texttt{class = ThresholdRule}, receives a lower $CA$ because its text fields
are evaluated at the lower weights assigned for threshold-type rules rather than
the higher weights appropriate for operational rules.
Misclassification therefore propagates naturally into a reduced agreement score
without requiring a separate class-check penalty.
Third, partial matches are rewarded proportionally: a rule that correctly
captures eight of twelve active fields still receives a high $CA$ rather than
being treated as a complete false negative.

% § 3.9  Hungarian assignment: optimal bipartite matching on the CA matrix; TP threshold = 0.6.
\subsection{Hungarian Optimal Assignment and Content F1}
\label{sec:hungarian}

Computing F1\textsubscript{content} requires solving an optimal bipartite
matching problem between the ground-truth rule set
$R_{\text{gt}} = \{r^{\text{gt}}_1, \ldots, r^{\text{gt}}_{n_{\text{gt}}}\}$
and the predicted rule set
$R_{\text{pred}} = \{r^{\text{pred}}_1, \ldots, r^{\text{pred}}_{n_{\text{pred}}}\}$.

\paragraph{Motivation for bipartite matching.}
A naive greedy approach would match each predicted rule to the ground-truth rule
with the highest $CA$ score.
This is problematic for two reasons: (a)~multiple predicted rules could
``claim'' the same ground-truth rule, inflating true-positive count and
precision; and (b)~the locally optimal greedy assignment may not be globally
optimal when the predicted and ground-truth sets differ in size.
The Hungarian algorithm (Kuhn-Munkres algorithm), implemented via
\texttt{scipy.optimize.linear\_sum\_assignment}, solves the bipartite matching
problem exactly in $O(n^3)$ time: it finds the unique one-to-one pairing between
$R_{\text{gt}}$ and $R_{\text{pred}}$ that \emph{maximises} the total sum of
$CA$ scores, providing the globally optimal assignment.

\paragraph{Construction of the cost matrix.}
The $n_{\text{gt}} \times n_{\text{pred}}$ matrix
$M[i,j] = CA(r^{\text{gt}}_i,\, r^{\text{pred}}_j)$
is computed for all pairs using Equation~\ref{eq:content_agreement}.
Because \texttt{linear\_sum\_assignment} minimises a cost function, the
algorithm is applied to the \emph{negative} of this matrix ($-M$), converting
the maximisation problem to an equivalent minimisation.
The resulting assignment $\mathcal{A}$ is the globally optimal one-to-one
pairing.

\paragraph{True-positive threshold and F1 computation.}
A matched pair $(r^{\text{gt}}_i,\, r^{\text{pred}}_j) \in \mathcal{A}$ is
counted as a \emph{true positive} if and only if
$CA(r^{\text{gt}}_i,\, r^{\text{pred}}_j) \geq 0.6$.
The threshold of $0.6$ was selected empirically to separate pairs sharing
essential operational semantics (correct sensor, constraint type, and
approximately correct numeric values) from pairs sharing only superficial
structure (same rule class and station but different sensor or inverted
threshold values).
Pairs below the threshold are treated as unmatched, contributing to either
false positives or false negatives.
Let $TP$ be the number of qualifying matched pairs:

$$P_{\text{content}} = \frac{TP}{n_{\text{pred}}}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
R_{\text{content}} = \frac{TP}{n_{\text{gt}}}$$

$$F1_{\text{content}} = \frac{2\, P_{\text{content}}\, R_{\text{content}}}
                             {P_{\text{content}} + R_{\text{content}}}$$

This formulation directly rewards pairs that capture the essential operational
semantics while penalising spurious extractions.
Using a hard TP threshold rather than summing continuous scores avoids a
well-known inflation effect where many low-quality near-matches accumulate
to produce a misleadingly high F1.

% § 3.10  SBERT singleton caching, CUDA/MPS auto-detection, empty-list edge cases.
\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:eval_impl}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SBERT singleton and batch encoding.}
      The Sentence-Transformer model is loaded once as a module-level singleton
      and reused for all evaluations in a session.
      At the start of each SOP's evaluation, all unique \texttt{condition} and
      \texttt{action} text strings from both the ground-truth and predicted rule
      sets are collected and batch-encoded in a single forward pass.
      The resulting embedding vectors are stored in a dictionary keyed by text
      string and reused across all $n_{\text{gt}} \times n_{\text{pred}}$ pair
      comparisons, ensuring each unique string is encoded exactly once per
      evaluation.
      This is critical for the multi-run stability study, where the same strings
      can appear in multiple runs and batch reuse reduces encoding time
      substantially.

\item \textbf{Hardware acceleration.}
      The SBERT model automatically detects and utilises CUDA (NVIDIA GPU) or
      MPS (Apple Silicon GPU) when available, falling back to CPU otherwise.

\item \textbf{Edge cases.}
      When either the ground-truth or the prediction list is empty,
      F1\textsubscript{content} is defined as $0.0$ and both precision and recall
      are set to $0.0$, avoiding division-by-zero errors and correctly penalising
      systems that produce no rules.

\item \textbf{Non-empty field guard.}
      Only fields that are non-empty (i.e., not an empty string, not
      \texttt{None}, not the string \texttt{"nan"}) in the ground-truth rule
      contribute to the $CA$ denominator.
      If a ground-truth rule's only non-empty scored field is \texttt{condition},
      the denominator equals $w_{\text{condition}}(c)$ and the score collapses
      to the SBERT similarity of the condition texts alone.
\end{itemize}

% § 3.11  Ablation study design
\subsection{Ablation Study Design}
\label{sec:ablation_design}

To quantify each pipeline component's individual contribution, the ablation
study follows the \emph{single-variable isolation} principle: each experimental
condition differs from the full pipeline by exactly one modification, and all
other nodes, prompts, model assignments, seed, and temperature are held constant.
This clean one-factor-at-a-time design ensures that observed performance
differences can be attributed to the modified component rather than to confounded
interactions.

Five conditions are evaluated (described in detail in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation_configs}):

\begin{description}
    \item[\texttt{ext\_multi\_agent} (full).] The reference configuration with
          all seven nodes active (Coordinator, three Extractors, Merger, Validator,
          Judge, Normalizer).
    \item[\texttt{abl\_noJudge}.] The Quality Gap Evaluator and the entire retry
          loop are removed; the Validator connects directly to the Normalizer.
          \emph{Scientific question:} how much does the iterative judge-and-retry
          mechanism improve extraction quality?
    \item[\texttt{abl\_noValidator}.] The Validator is replaced by a passthrough
          that applies no deterministic filter and no LLM gap-filling.
          \emph{Scientific question:} what is the combined contribution of
          structural rule checking, grounding-based gap recovery, and
          sensor-identifier normalisation?
    \item[\texttt{abl\_noCM}.] The LLM-based document-type classifier is replaced
          by a three-pattern keyword/regex classifier.
          \emph{Scientific question:} is learned document-type classification
          necessary, or can simple keyword heuristics route documents with
          equivalent precision?
    \item[\texttt{baseline\_b0} (deterministic).] The entire LLM pipeline is
          replaced by a hand-crafted regex parser with hard-coded knowledge of
          the four production stations and their sensors.
          \emph{Scientific question:} what is the performance ceiling of domain
          knowledge alone without any language understanding?
\end{description}

Each variant is implemented as a separate \texttt{StateGraph} object
instantiated by calling \texttt{build\_graph(ablation)}, where the tag causes
exactly one node or routing edge to be substituted or removed.
This clean separation prevents any shared mutable state between conditions.

% § 3.12  Multi-run stability assessment methodology
\subsection{Multi-Run Stability Assessment Methodology}
\label{sec:stability_design}

A single deterministic evaluation (seed~42, $T=0$) establishes the nominal
performance of the pipeline, but it does not characterise reliability under
natural LLM non-determinism.
For industrial deployment, stability is as important as peak performance: a
pipeline whose outputs vary substantially across re-runs would require expensive
human review after every document update triggers a re-extraction.

The following methodology is applied to characterise stability.

\paragraph{Run protocol.}
The full multi-agent pipeline is executed $K = 20$ independent times at
temperature $T = 0.5$ without a fixed seed.
Temperature $0.5$ introduces mild but genuine stochasticity: the model no longer
greedily follows the single most probable token path, but the distribution is
not so flat as to produce random-walk outputs.
This setting reflects typical production conditions, where temperature is set
above zero to prevent degenerate repetition.
Twenty runs provide a sufficient sample for stable mean and standard deviation
estimates; a 95\% confidence interval is computed as
$\text{CI}_{95} = 1.96 \cdot \sigma / \sqrt{K}$.

\paragraph{F1 aggregation.}
For each of the $K$ runs, F1\textsubscript{content} and all associated counts
are computed against the iMAKS ground truth using the evaluation framework
described above.
The aggregate statistics summarise expected performance and variance.

\paragraph{Inter-Agent Agreement Score (IAAS).}
Beyond per-run F1, pairwise consistency between runs is measured using the
Inter-Agent Agreement Score.
For each ordered pair of runs $(i,j)$, the globally optimal bipartite matching
between the two runs' rule sets $R_i$ and $R_j$ is computed using the same
content-agreement function and Hungarian algorithm as the evaluation framework.
The IAAS for the pair is:
\begin{equation}
\text{IAAS}(i,j) = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{A}_{ij}}
                         CA(r^i_a,\, r^j_b)}
                        {\max(|R_i|, |R_j|)}
\label{eq:iaas}
\end{equation}
The denominator normalises by the larger rule set, penalising runs that extract
many spurious extra rules even if all their matched rules are semantically close.
The global IAAS is the mean over all $\binom{K}{2} = 190$ pairwise scores.
Unlike F1, which compares each run against a fixed ground truth, IAAS measures
internal consistency regardless of correctness, providing an independent
characterisation of output stability.

\paragraph{Rule clustering and Fleiss~$\kappa$.}
All rules from all $K$ runs are pooled and clustered using a greedy
nearest-centroid algorithm with SBERT cosine similarity.
Each rule is represented as a concatenated five-field string
(\texttt{class~|~station~|~sensor~|~condition~|~action});
a new cluster is created if no existing cluster centroid has cosine
similarity $\geq 0.90$ to the incoming rule.
After clustering, a binary presence matrix
$M \in \{0,1\}^{N_c \times K}$ is constructed (entry $M_{c,r} = 1$ if
cluster~$c$ appears in run~$r$) and Fleiss~$\kappa$ is computed over this
matrix, treating clusters as ``items'' and runs as ``raters''.
This quantifies inter-run reliability beyond chance agreement using a
well-established inter-rater agreement statistic.

\paragraph{Stability labels and the two-tier deployment strategy.}
Each cluster is labelled based on its empirical frequency
$f_c = \sum_r M_{c,r} / K$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Consensus} ($f_c \geq 0.70$): the rule concept appears in at
          least 70\% of runs and is safe for automatic loading into the
          Knowledge Graph without human review.
    \item \emph{Hallucination proxy} ($f_c < 0.30$): the rule concept appears
          in fewer than 30\% of runs and is likely an LLM artefact rather than
          a genuine operational constraint.
    \item \emph{Borderline} ($0.30 \leq f_c < 0.70$): extracted inconsistently;
          queued for human review.
\end{itemize}
The thresholds of 0.70 and 0.30 are symmetric around 0.50, distinguishing a
clear majority ($>$~two-thirds prevalence) from a clear minority
($<$~one-third prevalence) with an explicitly ambiguous middle range.
The consensus set forms the automatically deployable subset of the pipeline's
output; this two-tier strategy operationalises uncertainty quantification for
LLM-based extraction in a practically deployable form.

% § 3.13  Why F1_content is the primary metric; why the normalizer's few-shot advantage does NOT affect it.
\subsection{Metric Validity and the Strict-F1 Caveat}
\label{sec:metric_validity}

The two-tier evaluation framework is designed so that F1\textsubscript{content}
is the primary, operationally meaningful metric and F1\textsubscript{strict} is
a secondary, name-matching baseline.
This distinction matters because the iMAKS benchmark assigns ground-truth
\texttt{ruleId} values using annotator conventions (e.g., \texttt{RULE-ST01-01}
for the first rule at station~1) that are not derivable from the SOP source text
alone; many SOPs do not label rules at all, and those that do use different
naming schemes.
An extraction system is therefore penalised on F1\textsubscript{strict} for
producing a semantically correct rule under a plausible but non-matching
identifier, which is not a meaningful penalty for operational quality.

A further caveat applies specifically to the multi-agent pipeline evaluated in
this thesis.
The \texttt{IdentifierCanonicalizer} node (Section~\ref{sec:normalizer}) is
shown up to 20 ground-truth \texttt{ruleId} examples as few-shot demonstrations
before it assigns identifiers to the extracted rules.
This gives the normalizer knowledge of the annotation convention used in the test
set, which can modestly increase the probability that its assigned identifiers
match the ground-truth strings.

\textbf{Critically, this does not affect F1\textsubscript{content}.}
The \texttt{content\_agreement} function (Equation~\ref{eq:content_agreement})
iterates over the active scored fields for the rule's class as defined in
Table~\ref{tab:class_weights}: the twelve fields
\texttt{class}, \texttt{station}, \texttt{sensor}, \texttt{sensorType},
\texttt{severity}, \texttt{unit}, \texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi},
\texttt{warnLo}, \texttt{critLo}, \texttt{condition}, and \texttt{action},
subject to the class-specific subset shown in the table (fields marked with a
dash are not iterated for that class).
The \texttt{ruleId} field is not in this set and contributes nothing to the
content score.
The Hungarian matching that determines true positives, false positives, and
false negatives is based entirely on these twelve content fields.

All performance claims, ablation comparisons, and cross-system comparisons in
this thesis use F1\textsubscript{content} as the decisive criterion.
F1\textsubscript{strict} values are included in the appendix tables for
completeness and to allow comparison with other works that report identifier
match, but they should be interpreted with the above caveat: they reflect both
extraction quality \emph{and} naming convention alignment, and the multi-agent
pipeline has an advantage in naming convention alignment that single-agent
baselines do not.

\section{Rule Extraction Grid Search Methodology}
\label{sec:grid_search}

Before introducing the collaborative multi-agent architecture, it is necessary to
establish a robust baseline for extracting operational rules from technical
documentation. To this end, a comprehensive grid search methodology was developed
to evaluate the performance of various LLMs and prompting paradigms on industrial
SOPs, using the F1\textsubscript{content} metric defined in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

% § 4.1  Models (4B–20B), 8,000-char input cap, 16,384-token context, T=0, seed=42, exponential backoff.
\subsection{System Architecture and Experimental Parameters}
The grid search is orchestrated through a highly resilient, single-agent execution
pipeline. The system evaluates an array of models spanning parameter sizes from 4\,B
to 20\,B, specifically \texttt{mistral-3:14b}, \texttt{gemma3:12b},
\texttt{mistral-3:8b}, \texttt{qwen/qwen3-4b-2507}, and \texttt{gpt-oss:20b}.
To accommodate the dense technical nature of the documentation, the text input from
each SOP document is capped at 8\,000 characters.

The LLM connection parameters are configured to maximise stability during
long-running batch operations. The context window is explicitly set to 16\,384
tokens to ensure sufficient capacity for both the input text and the structured JSON
output. A strict timeout of 600 seconds (10 minutes) is enforced per API call, and
network resilience is handled via an exponential backoff mechanism that allows up to
three retries, starting with a 15-second base delay that doubles upon each failure.

For the majority of evaluated paradigms, the model temperature is set to $0$ and a
fixed random seed (default~42) is supplied to enforce deterministic, reproducible
outputs. The exception is the self-consistency paradigm, which utilises a
temperature of $0.3$ across three independent runs to generate a diverse set of
reasoning paths.

A further implementation detail concerns the two Mistral-family models evaluated
in the grid search (\texttt{mistral-3:14b}, \texttt{mistral-3:8b}): their chat
templates do not support a dedicated system role. For these models, the system
message is automatically merged into the leading user turn at call time, preserving
full prompt semantics without requiring paradigm-specific adjustments.

% § 4.2  Four complexity levels: L0 direct (naive, few-shot, graph-informed), L1 CoT,
%         L2 iterative/ensemble (self-consistency, reflexion, reflexion-guided), L3 ReAct (react_abox).
\subsection{Prompting Paradigms and Complexity Levels}
To systematically assess how LLMs interpret industrial logic, the grid search
categorises prompting strategies into four distinct complexity levels:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Level~0 (Direct Extraction):} Includes the \texttt{naive} approach,
          which enforces a strict JSON schema without additional constraints.
          The \texttt{few\_shot\_static} paradigm enhances this by providing two
          concrete examples one \texttt{OperationalRule} and one
          \texttt{ThresholdRule} illustrating the required output format and numeric
          field mapping. Finally, the \texttt{graph\_informed} strategy introduces
          mandatory constraints, forcing the LLM to map its outputs to specific sensor
          naming conventions (e.g., \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_TMP}).
    \item \textbf{Level~1 (Reasoning Before Action):} Introduces intermediate
          cognitive steps before JSON generation. The \texttt{cot\_basic} paradigm
          requires standard step-by-step reasoning, while \texttt{cot\_structured}
          enforces a strict five-step analytical process: (1)~enumerate all stations
          and zones, (2)~identify the sensors and measurement types per station,
          (3)~extract threshold conditions with their numeric values,
          (4)~identify causal dependencies between stations, and
          (5)~identify access-control, occupancy, and maintenance rules.
          The \texttt{pre\_act} paradigm requires the model to summarise the
          document's structure, the categories of rules present, and any
          cross-section dependencies before producing the extraction.
    \item \textbf{Level~2 (Iterative Refinement and Ensemble Voting):} Incorporates
          multi-turn logic and ensemble strategies. The \texttt{self\_consistency}
          paradigm runs three independent extractions at temperature $0.3$ and then
          aggregates the results through a score-based voting mechanism
          (Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}). Both \texttt{reflexion} and
          \texttt{reflexion\_guided} use the \texttt{graph\_informed} system prompt
          as their conversational base, inheriting its mandatory sensor-naming
          constraints, whereas \texttt{self\_consistency} uses the simpler
          \texttt{naive} base without those constraints.
          The \texttt{reflexion} strategy utilises a two-turn loop in which the model
          critiques its own initial output for missing rules, imprecise or incorrect
          numeric conditions, wrong sensor and station names, and duplicate or
          poorly-structured entries.
          The \texttt{reflexion\_guided} paradigm extends this to three turns,
          adding a rigid domain-specific checklist in the final turn that covers
          six categories: (1)~completeness across all stations, (2)~cross-station
          dependencies, (3)~temporal anomaly patterns (SPIKE, DRIFT, STUCK,
          OUT\_OF\_RANGE, CORRELATED), (4)~predictive and corrective maintenance
          protocols (MAINT-01 to MAINT-08), (5)~access-control and zone-occupancy
          rules, and (6)~correctness of all numeric threshold values.
    \item \textbf{Level~3 (Tool Use / ReAct):} The \texttt{react\_abox} paradigm
          allows the model to actively verify sensor names against a localised
          Knowledge Graph (ABox). The LLM can halt its generation to emit a
          \texttt{VERIFY: <sensor\_name>} command. The system evaluates this request
          using fuzzy token-overlap matching and returns either a \texttt{SENSOR\_OK}
          confirmation or a \texttt{SENSOR\_NOT\_FOUND} warning with up to three
          syntactically similar suggestions. The loop is bounded to a maximum of
          five turns; if this limit is reached without a final JSON response, the
          last assistant message is parsed directly.
\end{itemize}

% § 4.3  self_consistency: 3 runs at T=0.3, pairwise agreement matrix, score_based voting, threshold 0.55.
\subsection{Ensemble Voting and Content Agreement}
\label{sec:ensemble}
The \texttt{self\_consistency} paradigm (Level~2) relies on an ensemble voting
mechanism to aggregate the results of three independent LLM calls. To determine
whether two independently generated rules describe the same operational constraint,
a \texttt{\_content\_agreement} algorithm calculates a weighted similarity score
(from 0.0 to 1.0).

Categorical fields (\texttt{class}, \texttt{station}, \texttt{sensor},
\texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity}, \texttt{unit}) are evaluated using exact
string matching after normalisation, while text fields (conditions and actions) are
evaluated using a Jaccard token-overlap approximation. Numeric fields within
\texttt{ThresholdRule} instances are subjected to a tolerance-based similarity
calculation to prevent minor rounding differences from invalidating a match. Field weights are non-uniform: among categorical fields, location identifiers
(\texttt{station}, \texttt{sensor}) carry weight~2.0, rule class
(\texttt{class}) carries weight~1.5, and the remaining categorical fields
(\texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity}, \texttt{unit}) each carry weight~1.0.
Primary numeric bounds (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{critLo}) carry weight~3.0,
secondary bounds (\texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo}) carry weight~1.5, and text
fields carry weight~0.5 for \texttt{ThresholdRule} entries (where numerics are
the primary signal) or~2.0 for all other rule classes.

The system implements a \texttt{score\_based} voting strategy by default. Rather
than requiring rules to share an identical \texttt{ruleId}, this strategy builds a
pairwise agreement matrix across all rules generated in the ensemble. If the
similarity score meets or exceeds the predefined threshold of $0.55$
(i.e.\ $\geq 0.55$), the rules are clustered via greedy merging in descending
agreement order. A rule is only retained
for the final output if its cluster spans at least 2 out of 3 independent runs.
For retained \texttt{ThresholdRule} clusters, a secondary majority vote is applied
across the threshold-related fields (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{critLo},
\texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo}) and the measurement unit (\texttt{unit}) to
resolve minor threshold discrepancies and produce a single stabilised value.

% § 4.4  JSON registry (partial → completed), heartbeat thread, 4-strategy cascading JSON parser.
\subsection{System Resilience and Output Parsing}
Given the tendency of LLMs to deviate from strict schemas, especially under high
token loads, the system includes extensive fallback mechanisms. Experiment state is
preserved via a JSON registry that marks runs as \texttt{partial} upon initiation
and \texttt{completed} only upon successful disk write, allowing interrupted grid
searches to resume seamlessly. Furthermore, a dedicated heartbeat thread
continuously logs timestamps to prevent silent hangs during prolonged API timeouts.

To extract valid data from malformed LLM responses, a cascading four-strategy JSON
parser is deployed. If standard parsing fails, the system attempts to repair
truncated JSON strings by balancing unclosed brackets. If the output contains
Markdown artifacts, regex extraction is used to isolate the data blocks. As a final
fallback, the parser iterates through the corrupted output to salvage any
individually well-formed rule objects.

\section{Multi-Agent Extraction Pipeline Architecture}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Building on the grid-search findings, a staged extraction pipeline was designed
that combines specialised LLM agents under a graph-based orchestration framework.
The system treats rule extraction as a directed acyclic computation where each
stage contributes a specific quality-improving transformation. Implemented with
\texttt{LangGraph}, the pipeline dispatches LLM calls through local \texttt{Ollama}
or \texttt{LM Studio} endpoints, maintaining full data sovereignty with no cloud
dependency.

The motivation for a multi-agent architecture rather than a single monolithic LLM
prompt stems directly from the grid-search analysis. Three key observations drove the
design.  First, the iMAKS SOP corpus is structurally heterogeneous: some files are
pure narrative prose, others are dense sensor-threshold tables, and others encode
occupancy or access-control matrices. A single prompt optimised for prose extraction
systematically under-performs on tabular layouts, and vice versa.  Second, no single
model achieved both high precision \emph{and} high recall across all document types:
\texttt{gemma3:12b} showed the strongest precision on narrative text, whereas
\texttt{mistral-3:8b} offered the best precision-recall balance on structured
table rows. Third, error modes compound across stages, hallucinated rules that
pass extraction unchecked cannot easily be corrected downstream without risking valid
rules. Separating responsibilities into discrete, verifiable stages allows each
failure mode to be addressed by a targeted mechanism (deterministic merger,
LLM-based validator, judge with retry) rather than relying on a single prompt to
handle all concerns simultaneously.

The resulting pipeline is a directed acyclic graph of seven specialised nodes
(plus one conditional re-entry loop) that sequentially classify, extract, deduplicate,
validate, and canonicalise rules. Every LLM-facing node uses a purpose-built
system prompt and the model best suited to its task. Deterministic steps are
preferred wherever the logic can be expressed as an algorithm, reserving LLM
reasoning for tasks that genuinely require semantic understanding.

\subsection{Shared State Management in LangGraph}
\label{sec:langgraph_state}

Each node in the LangGraph graph receives the shared \texttt{PipelineState} and
returns a partial update containing only the fields it modifies.  The state is
declared as a Python \texttt{TypedDict} (\texttt{PipelineState}) so the schema
is explicit and type-checked.

The most significant design decision is the treatment of \texttt{extracted\_rules}.
Because the three extractor nodes run in parallel, their outputs must be
\emph{concatenated} rather than replaced.  This is achieved by annotating the
field with \texttt{operator.add} as the merge reducer:

\begin{verbatim}
extracted_rules: Annotated[list[dict], operator.add]
\end{verbatim}

LangGraph then appends each extractor's output to the accumulating list rather
than overwriting it, so the Merger receives the combined candidate set from all
three extractors.

The key state fields and their roles are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{sop\_texts} SOP filename $\to$ truncated text; input to the
          Coordinator and all extractors.
    \item \texttt{doc\_types} SOP filename $\to$ document type (\textit{narrative},
          \textit{tabular}, \textit{matrix}, \textit{mixed}); drives the extractor
          fan-out and Judge checklist selection.
    \item \texttt{extracted\_rules} concatenated rule candidates from all extractor
          nodes (sum-reducer field described above).
    \item \texttt{merged\_rules} deduplicated output of the Consensus Merger.
    \item \texttt{validated\_rules} cleaned, gap-filled output of the Validator;
          input to the Judge and Normalizer.
    \item \texttt{normalized\_rules} final rule set with canonical \texttt{ruleId}
          values; written to the output CSV.
    \item \texttt{retry\_files} filename $\to$ document type for files the Judge
          flagged as structurally failed; populated only when a retry is triggered.
    \item \texttt{retry\_count} retry cycle counter; enforces the one-cycle cap.
    \item \texttt{ablation} optional tag (\texttt{"no\_cm"}, \texttt{"no\_validator"},
          \texttt{"no\_judge"}) that causes \texttt{build\_graph()} to omit or replace
          the corresponding node.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Assignments and Node Configuration}
\label{sec:agent_config}

Each node in the graph is assigned a specific model and a dedicated system prompt.
Model assignments are \emph{not} arbitrary: every assignment was derived from the
grid-search evaluation on the iMAKS dataset (Section~\ref{sec:grid_search}) and
reflects the performance profile best suited to each node's task.  The assignments
are centralised in a \texttt{DEFAULTS} dictionary in the implementation so that
a single change point is available for experiment reconfiguration; each key is also
exposed as a command-line argument, allowing per-node model overrides without
modifying the source code.

The core principle guiding assignments is \emph{task-role matching}: tasks that
require careful semantic reasoning under potential ambiguity are assigned larger,
higher-precision models, while tasks that amount to structural classification or
pattern-matching over a fixed schema are assigned smaller, faster models.
Assigning a heavyweight model everywhere would increase latency unnecessarily;
assigning a lightweight model to a semantically demanding node would risk precision
drops that propagate downstream.

\subsection{Stage Descriptions}
\label{sec:stages}

This subsection walks through each pipeline node in execution order, explaining
what it receives as input, what computation it performs, what it returns as output,
and why it was designed the way it was.  The goal is to provide enough detail that
each node's behaviour can be understood without reading the implementation.

% § 5.4.1  coordinator_node: LLM reads first 8,000 chars → JSON {doc_type, structural_reasoning};
%           classifies as narrative / tabular / matrix / mixed.
\subsubsection{Document Type Classifier}
\label{sec:classifier}

\paragraph{What it does.}

For each SOP, the \texttt{qwen/qwen3-4b-2507} model is shown the first
8\,000 characters and asked to produce a JSON object with two keys:
\texttt{doc\_type} and \texttt{structural\_reasoning}.  The system prompt
supplies precise semantic definitions for each of the four categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{tabular}}, the dominant layout is a sensor threshold
          table.  The clearest indicator is the presence of column headers such as
          \texttt{CRIT\_LO}, \texttt{WARN\_LO}, \texttt{CRIT\_HI}, \texttt{WARN\_HI}
          alongside rows of numeric values.  These files map cleanly to the
          \texttt{ThresholdRule} schema.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{matrix}}, the document presents an access-control or
          occupancy grid, where rows represent zones or roles and columns represent
          permissions, capacity limits, or acknowledgment deadlines.  These files map
          primarily to the \texttt{AccessRule} schema.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{mixed}}, the dominant layout is structured table rows
          where each row encodes a maintenance procedure or correlated-fault pattern
          (trigger condition, required action, and priority in separate columns).
          The key distinction from \texttt{narrative} is that the rules are table
          data, not labeled prose sentences.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{narrative}}, the default for all remaining documents:
          plain procedural or descriptive text without structured tables or grids.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What it returns.}
The node writes the completed \texttt{doc\_types} dictionary into the pipeline
state.  Each key is a filename and each value is one of the four category strings.
This mapping is subsequently used by the conditional fan-out function
(\texttt{dispatch\_to\_extractors}) to route each SOP to exactly one of the three
specialised extractor nodes.  The same mapping is also used later by the Judge to
select the appropriate completeness checklist for each document type.


% § 5.4.2  Three parallel extractors: A (gemma3:12b, narrative/mixed, all classes + PCM),
%           B (mistral-3:8b, tabular, ThresholdRule only), C (gemma3:12b, matrix, AccessRule only).
\subsubsection{Specialised Extractors}
\label{sec:miners}

After classification, the Coordinator's fan-out routes each SOP file to exactly
one extractor based on its document type: Extractor~A receives \texttt{narrative}
and \texttt{mixed} files, Extractor~B receives \texttt{tabular} files, and
Extractor~C receives \texttt{matrix} files.  The three nodes run concurrently and
append their results to \texttt{extracted\_rules} via the sum reducer.

\paragraph{The Physical Constraint Mapping (PCM) discipline.}
All three extractors are governed by the same grounding discipline, referred to as
the \emph{Physical Constraint Mapping} (PCM) principles.  PCM is a set of five
prompt-level instructions that collectively prevent the most common hallucination
patterns observed in the grid-search baseline runs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PCM-1 (verbatim numeric copying)}, threshold values must be
          copied character-for-character from the source text.  The model is
          explicitly prohibited from rounding, normalising, or converting units.
    \item \textbf{PCM-2 (no unit invention)}, the \texttt{unit} field must be
          taken from the source; if no unit is stated, the field is left empty.
          The model must not infer a unit from physical context (e.g., assuming
          \texttt{°C} because the field is labelled ``temperature'').
    \item \textbf{PCM-3 (actionable conditions)}, the \texttt{condition} field must
          express the exact physical state that triggers the rule (e.g., sensor
          exceeds value at station), not a vague paraphrase.  The numeric boundary
          and location must be included when stated in the source.
    \item \textbf{PCM-4 (strict null discipline)}, if a field value is implied,
          inferred, or absent from the source text, the field must be left as an
          empty string.  The model must not fill \texttt{sensor}, \texttt{sensorType},
          \texttt{unit}, \texttt{station}, or any threshold field with a plausible
          guess.
    \item \textbf{PCM-5 (no cross-row or cross-sentence inference)}, each rule must
          be grounded solely in the row, sentence, or paragraph that introduces it.
          Values must not be borrowed from neighbouring rows, column headers, or
          earlier paragraphs to fill gaps in the current rule.
\end{itemize}

These five constraints were not part of the grid-search baseline prompts; they were
added in response to hallucination patterns observed during grid-search analysis and
represent one of the principal prompt engineering contributions of the pipeline.

\paragraph{Extractor A, narrative and mixed documents.}
Extractor A (\texttt{gemma3:12b}) handles \texttt{narrative} and \texttt{mixed}
SOPs.  Its system prompt covers all four rule classes
(\texttt{OperationalRule}, \texttt{ThresholdRule}, \texttt{MaintenanceRule},
\texttt{AccessRule}) because prose-based SOPs can contain rules of any type.
The prompt includes a \emph{multi-severity splitting} instruction: if a single
sensor has both a WARNING tier and a CRITICAL tier of the same constraint
(e.g., ``warn QC at 28\,°C; initiate shutdown at 32\,°C''), the model must
produce \emph{two separate rules}, one with \texttt{severity = WARNING} and one
with \texttt{severity = CRITICAL}, rather than collapsing them into a single rule.
This is necessary because downstream evaluation scores each rule independently,
and collapsing severity tiers would cause both to be counted as false negatives.
The prompt also includes several few-shot examples drawn from the iMAKS corpus
and instructs the model to produce an optional \texttt{"reasoning"} key (alongside
the \texttt{"rules"} list), which provides a chain-of-thought trace for
debugging without affecting the extracted rules themselves.

\paragraph{Extractor B, tabular documents.}
Extractor B (\texttt{mistral-3:8b}) handles \texttt{tabular} documents.
Its extraction task is narrower and more mechanical than Extractor A's: the
source document is a sensor-threshold table whose columns map directly to the
four threshold fields (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo},
\texttt{critLo}).  The system prompt therefore restricts the extractor to the
\texttt{ThresholdRule} class only, provides explicit column-to-field mapping
instructions, and applies PCM-1 through PCM-5 to ensure that cell values are
copied verbatim and no unit suffixes are appended to the numeric threshold fields.
The prompt also instructs the model to preserve partial thresholds: if only
critical limits are defined for a sensor (no warning band), the model produces a
\texttt{ThresholdRule} with only \texttt{critHi}/\texttt{critLo} populated and
the warning fields left empty, rather than omitting the rule entirely.

\paragraph{Extractor C, matrix documents.}
Extractor C (\texttt{gemma3:12b}) handles \texttt{matrix} documents.  It focuses
exclusively on \texttt{AccessRule} entries, which encode occupancy limits
(maximum number of personnel in a zone), acknowledgment deadlines (response time
required after an alarm), and explicitly labelled access or permission rules.
The system prompt instructs the model to enumerate one rule per non-trivial cell
value in the matrix, applying PCM to keep cell values verbatim.  The instruction
to avoid cross-cell inference (PCM-3 applied to matrix context) is particularly
important here: matrix layouts can confuse models into pairing a row label with
the wrong column value when rows and columns both carry semantically similar names.

\paragraph{Retry prompt augmentation.}
When the Judge triggers a re-extraction cycle (described in
Section~\ref{sec:judge}), the \texttt{PreRetry} node sets an \texttt{is\_retry}
flag in the per-file sub-state.  Each extractor checks this flag and, when it is
set, appends an exhaustiveness instruction to its prompt: the model is told
explicitly that a previous extraction pass missed rules and is asked to be more
thorough, paying special attention to any rule that is explicitly named or
labelled in the source text.  The semantic fields of the re-extracted rules are
otherwise produced by the same prompt and model as the original pass.

\subsubsection{Consensus Merger}
\label{sec:merger}

\paragraph{What it receives.}
The \texttt{ConsensusMerger} node receives the complete \texttt{extracted\_rules}
list, which at this point is the concatenated output of all extractor nodes.
Because Extractors A, B, and C may each have processed overlapping document
types in a retry scenario, and because a single SOP can exhibit both narrative and
tabular sections (document type \texttt{mixed}), the same underlying rule may appear
as multiple slightly-different candidate dictionaries in this list.  The Merger's
responsibility is to produce a single coherent, deduplicated rule set without
discarding any rule that represents a genuinely distinct constraint.

\paragraph{Grouping by physical identity key.}
The Merger first groups all candidates by a four-component identity key:

\[
  k = (\texttt{class},\ \texttt{station},\ \texttt{sensorType},\ \texttt{condition\_prefix})
\]

The \texttt{condition\_prefix} component is included only when both
\texttt{station} and \texttt{sensorType} are empty strings (a common situation
for \texttt{AccessRule} and \texttt{MaintenanceRule} entries, which often lack a
physical sensor location).  Without this guard, two completely different
\texttt{AccessRule} entries, for example, one about zone capacity and one about
acknowledgment deadlines, could fall into the same key group and be incorrectly
collapsed together.  The prefix (the first 40 characters of the
\texttt{condition} field, lowercased) is used as a coarse proxy for rule identity
in these cases.

\paragraph{Four deterministic resolution branches.}
Within each group, four resolution branches execute in sequence.  A rule leaves
the sequence as soon as it is handled by a branch; later branches only see
candidates that the earlier branches did not resolve.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Branch~A (Singleton pass-through.)}
          If only one candidate exists in the group, it is passed through
          unchanged.  No comparison or merging logic is needed.  This is the most
          common case: many rules have a unique physical identity key and therefore
          appear in a singleton group.

    \item \textbf{Branch~B (Complementary threshold merge.)}
          This branch handles a specific duplication pattern that arises frequently
          in tabular documents: two extractors independently parse the same threshold
          table row but each captures only one half of the threshold band.  Extractor A
          (narrative/mixed) might extract a rule with only the critical limits
          (\texttt{critHi}/\texttt{critLo} populated, warning fields empty), while
          Extractor B (tabular) extracts a rule with only the warning limits
          (\texttt{warnHi}/\texttt{warnLo} populated, critical fields empty).
          Both rules refer to the same physical sensor and the same condition, but
          neither is complete on its own.

          The branch fires when exactly two \texttt{ThresholdRule} candidates exist
          in the group and they form a complementary pair: one carries only critical
          fields and the other carries only warning fields.  In this case, the
          critical-carrying rule is taken as the base and the warning values from
          the other candidate are merged into it.  The merged rule's severity is set
          to \texttt{CRITICAL} (because it now contains both tiers, and the most
          severe tier determines the rule's overall severity label).  Both candidates
          are replaced by this single merged rule and the group is marked resolved.

    \item \textbf{Branch~B2 (Threshold semantic deduplication.)}
          This branch targets a second threshold-specific pattern: two or more
          \texttt{ThresholdRule} candidates share the same physical identity key and
          also share \emph{identical numeric threshold values} (after stripping any
          unit suffix that might have been appended by one extractor but not another).
          This happens when the same threshold table row is processed by both the
          tabular extractor and a retry pass, producing two semantically identical
          rules that differ only in superficial string formatting.

          When such duplicate pairs are detected, the group is collapsed to the
          \emph{most-populated} candidate, the one with the greatest number of
          non-empty fields, which is most likely to be the more complete extraction.
          The other candidates in the group are discarded.

    \item \textbf{Branch~C (Content-agreement collapse.)}
          For any candidates that remain after Branches B and B2, a pairwise content
          agreement score is computed over a six-field normalised signature:
          \texttt{class}, \texttt{station}, \texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity},
          \texttt{condition}, and \texttt{action}.  Each field is lowercased, stripped
          of punctuation and extra whitespace, and the score is the fraction of
          mutually non-empty field pairs whose normalised values match (only fields
          that are non-empty in \emph{both} candidates contribute to the denominator).

          If the score meets or exceeds the threshold of $0.75$, the pair is
          considered near-duplicate and is collapsed to the richer candidate.  However, an
          important guard prevents this collapse when the two candidates have
          \emph{different} \texttt{action} fields after normalisation: differing
          actions on the same sensor signal an intentional severity-tier split
          (e.g., a WARNING tier that logs the event and notifies QC vs.\ a CRITICAL
          tier that initiates an emergency shutdown).  These must be preserved as
          two distinct rules; collapsing them would erase the more urgent instruction.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Handling unresolved conflicts.}
Any candidates that remain after all four branches are kept as-is and forwarded to
the Validator and Judge.  LLM adjudication was deliberately excluded from the Merger:
without access to the source SOP text, a model cannot reliably distinguish genuinely
distinct rules from near-duplicate ones, and early experiments showed it introduced
false negatives by collapsing rules that only appeared similar.  The Validator and
Judge have access to the full source text and are better positioned to resolve
ambiguous cases.

%           physics ordering); Pass 2 = per-file LLM gap-filling (additive only, never removes).
\subsubsection{Validator (Pre-Judge Scrubber)}
\label{sec:validator}

\paragraph{What it receives.}
The Validator receives \texttt{merged\_rules} and \texttt{sop\_texts}.  It runs
before the Judge so the Judge receives a structurally clean rule set and can focus
on semantic gaps.

\paragraph{Pass 1, deterministic post-filter.}
The first pass applies a \emph{deterministic post-filter} (implemented as
\texttt{\_deterministic\_post\_filter}) that removes any rule failing any of the
following structural checks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Invalid class.}  The \texttt{class} field must be one of
          \texttt{OperationalRule}, \texttt{ThresholdRule}, \texttt{MaintenanceRule},
          or \texttt{AccessRule}.  Common misspellings and capitalisation variants
          are normalised before checking; unrecognisable values cause the rule to
          be discarded.
    \item \textbf{Missing threshold numeric.}  A \texttt{ThresholdRule} with no
          numeric value in any of \texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo},
          \texttt{critLo} is discarded.
    \item \textbf{Empty condition.}  Rules with a blank \texttt{condition} field
          are discarded.
    \item \textbf{Physically impossible threshold ordering.}  The filter enforces
          the full ordering $\texttt{critLo} \leq \texttt{warnLo} \leq \texttt{warnHi}
          \leq \texttt{critHi}$ for any populated pair.  Violations indicate a
          column-swap error and cause the rule to be removed.
    \item \textbf{Content deduplication.}  Remaining rules are deduplicated by a
          six-field normalised signature (\texttt{class}, \texttt{station},
          \texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity}, \texttt{condition}, \texttt{action});
          the most-populated rule in each duplicate group is kept.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pass 2, additive LLM gap-filling.}
After the deterministic filter, the Validator performs a per-file LLM scan using
\texttt{mistral-3:14b}.  For each source SOP file, the model receives the SOP
text and the current set of extracted rules for that file and is asked to identify
any rule that is \emph{explicitly labelled} in the source text (e.g., a sentence
beginning with ``Rule~3:'' or a numbered item in a procedure list) but has no
corresponding entry in the extracted set.  If such a gap is found, the model
produces the missing rule in the standard JSON schema and it is appended to the
rule set.

This pass is \emph{strictly additive}: the prompt explicitly forbids the model
from removing or modifying any existing rule.  Incorrect rules are left for the
Judge, which has access to the full source text and explicit quality criteria.

After the LLM gap-filling step, the deterministic filter is applied a second
time to eliminate any structurally invalid rules that the LLM may have
introduced during gap-filling, ensuring the output \texttt{validated\_rules}
list satisfies all structural invariants.

\subsubsection{Quality Gap Evaluator (Judge)}
\label{sec:judge}

\paragraph{What it receives.}
The \texttt{QualityGapEvaluator} node receives the \texttt{validated\_rules} list
and the \texttt{sop\_texts} and \texttt{doc\_types} dictionaries.  It has access to
the original SOP text, a capability that distinguishes it from the Merger, which
operates only on the extracted rule dictionaries.

\paragraph{Prompt assembly.}
The Judge's system prompt is not static: it is assembled dynamically for each run
by combining a \emph{base judge prompt} with one or more \emph{document-type-specific
completeness checklists}.  The base prompt defines the four gap categories and the
expected output schema.  The checklists enumerate the rule classes that should be
present for each document type, for example, the tabular checklist specifies that
every sensor row in the threshold table must produce at least one
\texttt{ThresholdRule} entry, while the narrative checklist covers operational and
maintenance rule expectations.  For \texttt{mixed} documents, both the narrative
checklist and the maintenance checklist are appended, because mixed SOPs combine
prose procedures with embedded maintenance tables.

\paragraph{Gap categories.}
The Judge evaluates the validated rule set against four categories of quality
deficiency:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{S1 (Missing rule classes.)}
          The Judge checks whether the rule set contains at least one rule of each
          class expected for the document type.  For a tabular SOP, the absence of
          any \texttt{ThresholdRule} is an S1 violation.  For a matrix SOP, the
          absence of any \texttt{AccessRule} is an S1 violation.  For a narrative
          SOP, missing \texttt{OperationalRule} entries are flagged.

    \item \textbf{S2 (Missing labelled rules.)}
          The Judge reads the source SOP text and checks whether any rule that is
          explicitly identified in the text (e.g., by a numbered label, a named
          procedure, or a clearly stated constraint) has no corresponding entry
          in \texttt{validated\_rules}.  This is the most semantically demanding
          of the four checks: the model must cross-reference the rule set against
          the source text rather than applying a purely structural criterion.

    \item \textbf{S3 (Quality issues.)}
          The Judge inspects individual rules for internal quality problems:
          incomplete mandatory fields, numeric threshold values that appear to be
          hallucinated (i.e., not present anywhere in the source text), physically
          impossible orderings that survived the Validator's filter, or
          \texttt{condition} descriptions that are implausibly vague or generic.

    \item \textbf{S4 (Identifier conflicts.)}
          The Judge checks for \texttt{ruleId} collisions: two rules sharing the
          same \texttt{ruleId} and \texttt{severity} are flagged as conflicting.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Deterministic pre-check.}
In addition to the LLM's evaluation, the Judge node performs a deterministic
threshold-order pre-check identical to the one in the Validator's first pass.
This redundant check is included because a rule with an impossible ordering may
have been introduced by the Validator's LLM gap-filling step after the first
deterministic filter ran, and the Judge is the last stage before normalisation
where such violations can be caught without requiring a full re-run.

\paragraph{Output format.}
The Judge outputs a JSON object with four fields:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{pass}, a boolean indicating whether the rule set is
          considered complete and free of critical gaps.
    \item \texttt{gaps}, a list of gap category codes (S1, S2, S3, S4)
          for each detected violation.
    \item \texttt{suggestions}, a list of concrete, human-readable descriptions
          of what is missing or incorrect, intended for observability and debugging.
    \item \texttt{deficient\_files}, the list of SOP filenames for which the
          Judge detected violations.  This list is recorded in the pipeline log
          for observability but is \emph{not} used directly to determine whether
          a retry is triggered (see below).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Retry dispatch logic.}
The decision to trigger a re-extraction cycle is \emph{not} made by the Judge's
LLM output.  Instead, it is made by a separate deterministic routing function
(\texttt{\_judge\_routing}) that inspects \texttt{validated\_rules} directly.
A file is queued for re-extraction only when it exhibits a \emph{complete
structural failure}: zero rules of the expected primary class are present in the
current rule set for that file.  The specific failure conditions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A \texttt{tabular} SOP with no \texttt{ThresholdRule} entries.
    \item A \texttt{matrix} SOP with no \texttt{AccessRule} entries.
    \item A \texttt{narrative} or \texttt{mixed} SOP with no \texttt{OperationalRule}
          or \texttt{MaintenanceRule} entries.
    \item Any SOP with zero rules of any class.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Partial} gaps detected by the LLM (e.g., a narrative SOP that has
\texttt{OperationalRule} entries but is suspected of missing one specific rule)
are \emph{not} escalated to a retry.  Instead, they are addressed by the
Validator's additive gap-filling mechanism, which operates on a per-file basis
and can insert missing rules without re-running the full merge.  The reason for
this distinction is that a full re-extraction and re-merge over an already-populated
rule set risks over-deduplication: if the merged set already contains correct rules,
passing them through the Merger a second time alongside new extractions may cause
the Merger's agreement collapse (Branch C) to discard valid rules as near-duplicates.

\paragraph{PreRetry node.}
When a structural failure is detected and the retry counter has not yet reached
its cap (one cycle), the routing function directs execution to the
\texttt{PreRetry} node.  This node increments \texttt{retry\_count},
populates the \texttt{retry\_files} dictionary with the filenames of the
deficient SOPs and their document types, and emits \texttt{Send} objects to
dispatch each deficient file to its appropriate extractor with the
\texttt{is\_retry = True} flag set.  The extractor uses this flag to append the
exhaustiveness instruction described in Section~\ref{sec:miners}.  The
re-extracted rules enter the same Merge $\to$ Validate $\to$ Judge sequence as
the original extraction; if the retry itself produces a structural failure, the
routing function observes that the retry cap has been reached and proceeds
directly to normalisation without further re-extraction.

\subsubsection{Identifier Canonicalizer (Normalizer)}
\label{sec:normalizer}

\paragraph{What it receives.}
The \texttt{IdentifierCanonicalizer} node receives \texttt{validated\_rules},
the cleaned, gap-filled, judge-approved rule set.  At this stage, the semantic
content of every rule (class, station, sensor, condition, action, thresholds) is
considered final.  The Normalizer's sole responsibility is to assign a
human-readable, structured \texttt{ruleId} to each rule.

\paragraph{Why ruleId assignment is a separate step.}
Extractor nodes produce placeholder identifiers (e.g.\ \texttt{R01}, \texttt{R02})
that carry no naming-convention information and cannot be matched against
ground-truth entries in the strict evaluation metric.  Decoupling identifier
assignment from extraction also prevents naming errors from influencing the
semantic fields: if the extractor were responsible for both tasks, a malformed
identifier could corrupt the rule object or reduce the number of rules produced.

\paragraph{Few-shot prompt and naming convention.}
The \texttt{qwen/qwen3-4b-2507} model is given a few-shot prompt constructed by
the \texttt{\_load\_gt\_few\_shots()} function, which loads up to 20 ground-truth
\texttt{ruleId}/rule pairs from \texttt{ground\_truth.csv}.  These examples
demonstrate the iMAKS naming schema:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{ThresholdRule} identifiers follow the pattern
          \texttt{RULE-THR-\{STATION\}-\{SENSOR\}-\{SEVERITY\}}, e.g.,
          \texttt{RULE-THR-ST01-TMP-CRIT} for the critical temperature threshold
          at filling station ST01.
    \item \texttt{OperationalRule} identifiers follow
          \texttt{RULE-\{STATION\}-\{NN\}}, where \texttt{NN} is a two-digit
          sequence number, e.g., \texttt{RULE-ST01-01}.
    \item \texttt{MaintenanceRule} identifiers follow
          \texttt{MAINT-\{NN\}}, e.g., \texttt{MAINT-01}.
    \item \texttt{AccessRule} identifiers follow one of three sub-patterns
          depending on the access type: \texttt{RULE-OCC-\{ZONE\}} for zone
          occupancy limits, \texttt{RULE-ACK-\{ROLE\}-\{SEV\}} for per-role
          acknowledgment deadlines (e.g., \texttt{RULE-ACK-OP-WARN}), and
          \texttt{RULE-ACCESS-\{NN\}} for labeled prose access rules.
\end{itemize}

The model is asked to read the validated rules and produce a JSON list in which
each entry contains only two fields: the original rule's extracted content
(used to identify the rule) and the new canonical \texttt{ruleId}.

\paragraph{Surgical field replacement.}
Only the \texttt{ruleId} field from the LLM's output is accepted.  All other
fields, class, station, sensorType, condition, action, threshold values, unit,
severity, and source metadata, are taken verbatim from the corresponding
validated rule, not from the LLM's output.  This surgical approach means that
any formatting error or hallucination in the LLM's response can affect at most
the \texttt{ruleId} of a single rule and cannot corrupt semantic content.

If the number of rules returned by the LLM does not match the number of validated
rules (an indicator that the LLM dropped or duplicated rules), the node falls back
to the original placeholder identifiers for the entire batch rather than attempting
a partial match that could misalign identifiers and rule content.

\paragraph{Metric validity and the strict-F1 caveat.}
Because the Normalizer is shown up to 20 ground-truth identifiers drawn from the
\emph{same} benchmark being evaluated, the resulting \texttt{ruleId} values may
more closely match the annotation conventions than would be achievable in a blind
deployment, giving F1\textsubscript{strict} a modest artificial advantage.

\textbf{This does not affect F1\textsubscript{content}}, which is the primary
reported metric throughout this thesis.  The \texttt{content\_agreement} function
(Equation~\ref{eq:content_agreement}) scores the \texttt{class}, \texttt{station},
\texttt{sensor}, \texttt{condition}, \texttt{action}, and threshold fields; it does
not include \texttt{ruleId} in its computation at all.  All ablation comparisons
and the cross-system comparison in Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results} use
F1\textsubscript{content} as the decisive criterion.
F1\textsubscript{strict} figures are reported for completeness but should be
interpreted with this caveat in mind.
\textbf{The Knowledge-Graph validation is likewise unaffected.}
Detection in the raw-data validation (Section~\ref{sec:neo4j}) is driven by
which sensor a rule governs (the \texttt{:GOVERNS} edge derived from the
rule's \texttt{sensor} field) and by the rule's extracted numeric
parameters, never by its \texttt{ruleId}.
Even if the Normalizer assigned an incorrect identifier to a rule, that
rule would still analyze the same sensor with the same thresholds, leaving
the validation results structurally independent of the naming demonstration.

\subsection{Pipeline Topology and Execution Flow}
\label{sec:topology}

\paragraph{Graph construction.}
The complete computation graph is assembled by the \texttt{build\_graph()} function,
which takes an optional \texttt{ablation} tag as its only argument.  The function
instantiates a LangGraph \texttt{StateGraph} over the \texttt{PipelineState}
schema, registers each node, and wires the edges.  The graph is compiled once by
calling \texttt{.compile()}, producing an executable object that can process an
entire SOP corpus in a single invocation.  The ablation tag, if present, causes
\texttt{build\_graph()} to omit or bypass the corresponding node (e.g., when
\texttt{ablation = "no\_cm"}, the LLM Coordinator is replaced by
\texttt{coordinator\_node\_no\_cm}, a keyword/regex classifier that assigns
document types without any LLM call).

\paragraph{Execution flow.}
The directed execution flow for the full pipeline is:

\begin{verbatim}
START → Coordinator → [conditional fan-out]
          ├─ Extractor A (narrative / mixed files)
          ├─ Extractor B (tabular files)
          └─ Extractor C (matrix files)
                 └─ [fan-in: sum reducer on extracted_rules]
        → ConsensusMerger → Validator → QualityGapEvaluator (Judge)
              → [conditional routing]
                    ├─ (structural failure + retry budget remaining)
                    │      → PreRetry → [fan-out to relevant extractor(s)]
                    │             → ConsensusMerger → Validator → Judge → …
                    └─ (pass or retry budget exhausted)
                           → IdentifierCanonicalizer (Normalizer) → Save → END
\end{verbatim}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_full} illustrates the compiled graph topology.  Solid
edges represent unconditional data flow.  Dashed edges represent the two
conditional routing points: the Coordinator's fan-out (which routes each SOP to
exactly one extractor based on its document type) and the Judge's routing decision
(which either sends execution to \texttt{PreRetry} for a retry cycle or to the
Normalizer to finalise the results).

\paragraph{Conditional fan-out from Coordinator.}
LangGraph's \texttt{Send}-based conditional edges are used to implement the
Coordinator's fan-out.  After the Coordinator fills the \texttt{doc\_types}
dictionary, the \texttt{dispatch\_to\_extractors} function is called as a
conditional edge function.  It iterates over \texttt{doc\_types} and emits one
\texttt{Send} object per SOP file, targeting the node name corresponding to the
file's document type.  LangGraph schedules all emitted \texttt{Send} calls
concurrently; the three extractor nodes therefore process their respective files
in parallel, with their outputs merged into \texttt{extracted\_rules} via the
\texttt{operator.add} reducer.

\paragraph{Conditional routing after the Judge.}
The \texttt{\_judge\_routing} function examines the current \texttt{validated\_rules}
and \texttt{retry\_count} to decide the next node.  If at least one SOP file
exhibits a complete structural failure (zero rules of the expected primary class)
and the retry counter is below the cap, execution is directed to \texttt{pre\_retry},
which populates \texttt{retry\_files} and emits \texttt{Send} objects for another
extraction round.  The retry outputs re-enter the graph at the Merger node and
proceed through the full Validate $\to$ Judge sequence again.  If no structural
failure is detected, or if the retry cap has been reached, execution proceeds
unconditionally to the Normalizer.

\subsection{Error Resilience Strategy}
\label{sec:error_resilience}

Running large language models locally on consumer hardware introduces failure modes
that do not appear in cloud API settings: GPU out-of-memory crashes, inference
server overloads, network timeouts on the loopback interface, and occasional
partial responses where the model terminates generation before closing the JSON
array.  The pipeline addresses each of these through a combination of
exponential-backoff retries, graceful empty-list handling, and a cascading JSON
parser.

\paragraph{Exponential-backoff retry loop.}
Every LLM invocation in the pipeline is made through the shared \texttt{llm\_call}
function, which wraps the API call in a retry loop with up to \texttt{MAX\_RETRIES}
(3) attempts.  On the first failure, the function waits \texttt{RETRY\_BASE\_DELAY}
(15\,s) before retrying.  On each subsequent failure, the delay doubles (so the
sequence is 15\,s, 30\,s).  If the third attempt also fails, the original exception
is re-raised.  The doubling schedule prevents hammering an overloaded inference
server while still attempting recovery from transient issues such as a momentary
GPU stall.  If the exception propagates out of \texttt{llm\_call}, it terminates the
current LangGraph node; LangGraph marks the node as failed and halts the pipeline
run with an informative traceback.

\paragraph{Empty-list fallback.}
If an extractor produces no rules, the Merger returns an empty list.  All
downstream nodes (Validator, Judge) handle empty inputs gracefully: the Judge's
structural failure check will detect the complete failure and trigger a retry if
the budget allows.

\paragraph{Cascading JSON parser.}
The LLM output in every node is parsed by the \texttt{parse\_rules} function,
which applies four recovery strategies in sequence:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Direct parse.}  The raw string is passed to
          \texttt{json.loads()} after stripping any chain-of-thought
          \texttt{<think>}\ldots\texttt{</think>} blocks that some models emit.
          If the result is a list, it is returned directly.  If the result is a
          dictionary, the function looks for a \texttt{"rules"} key and returns
          its value, printing any \texttt{"reasoning"} value for debug inspection.
    \item \textbf{Bracket repair.}  If direct parsing fails, the function attempts
          to rebalance truncated output by appending the appropriate number of
          closing brackets and braces.  Models that run out of token budget
          sometimes terminate mid-array, leaving a structurally incomplete but
          otherwise correct JSON fragment.  After rebalancing, \texttt{json.loads()}
          is attempted again.
    \item \textbf{Fence and pattern scan.}  If bracket repair also fails, the
          function searches for a Markdown code fence
          (\texttt{```json \ldots ```}) and attempts to parse its content.
          It also tries to locate an outer JSON object or array anywhere in the
          response using a regular-expression scan.
    \item \textbf{Rule-object salvage.}  As a last resort, the function scans
          the raw string for individual JSON objects that contain a \texttt{ruleId}
          key and collects whatever valid rule dictionaries it can find.  This
          recovers partial outputs where the array wrapper is corrupted but the
          individual rule objects are syntactically correct.
\end{enumerate}

If none of the four strategies produces a non-empty list, \texttt{parse\_rules}
returns an empty list rather than raising an exception, allowing the pipeline to
continue with whatever other rules were extracted from other files.

  & What is the performance ceiling for a purely deterministic, domain-specific

All components not listed as modified, including the extractor prompts, the

% § 6.2  Each variant is a clean independent StateGraph; ablation tag swaps one node at compile time.
\subsection{Implementation of Ablation Variants}
\label{sec:ablation_impl}

Each ablation variant is built as an independent \texttt{StateGraph} by the
function \texttt{build\_graph(ablation)}. Passing the ablation tag at compile
time rather than introducing runtime branches inside shared nodes ensures
every condition is a clean, independently auditable pipeline and avoids
accidentally sharing mutable state between conditions.

% ¶  coordinator_node_no_cm: 3-pattern keyword regex replaces LLM call; patterns for tabular / matrix / mixed.
\paragraph{No LLM Coordinator (\texttt{abl\_noCM}).}
The \texttt{coordinator\_node} is replaced by \texttt{coordinator\_node\_no\_cm},
a keyword/regex classifier that requires no LLM call.
Three ordered patterns are tested against the first 8\,000 characters of each SOP:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the pattern \texttt{CRIT\_LO|WARN\_LO|WARN\_HI|CRIT\_HI} triggers
          \texttt{tabular} (threshold table);
    \item a pattern matching zone/area/sector names with authorisation keywords
          triggers \texttt{matrix};
    \item a pattern matching maintenance identifiers or drift/cross-sensor keywords
          triggers \texttt{mixed}.
\end{itemize}
Any document not matching the first three patterns defaults to \texttt{narrative}.
This ablation isolates the value of having an LLM read the full structural layout
of a document before deciding which extraction prompt to apply.

% ¶  validate_node_noop: merged rules forwarded unchanged, no deterministic filter, no LLM gap-fill.
\paragraph{No Validator (\texttt{abl\_noValidator}).}
The \texttt{validate\_node} is replaced by \texttt{validate\_node\_noop}, a
passthrough that forwards the merged rule set to the judge completely unchanged.
Three operations are bypassed simultaneously:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Deterministic post-filter}: class validation, threshold hygiene
          (e.g.\ ensuring \texttt{critLo} $<$ \texttt{warnLo} $<$ \texttt{warnHi}
          $<$ \texttt{critHi}), and physics ordering checks.
    \item \textbf{LLM-based gap-filling}: a grounding pass that re-reads the source
          SOP to recover rules the extractor missed and fills in incomplete field
          values.
    \item \textbf{Sensor-identifier normalisation}: resolution of abbreviated node
          references to their canonical Knowledge-Graph names (e.g.\
          \texttt{ST01\_FLW} $\to$ \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_FLW}).  This is directly
          visible in the raw output: every SOP-002 rule in the \texttt{abl\_noValidator}
          CSV carries the shortened sensor identifier that the extractor emitted,
          whereas the full pipeline promotes each to the full KG node name.
\end{enumerate}
Even obviously malformed rules, wrong class names, empty condition fields,
threshold values with unit suffixes attached, are forwarded without correction.
The ablation therefore measures the combined contribution of all three operations
in a single condition.

% ¶  build_graph("no_judge"): unconditional edge validate → normalize; no gap report, no retry loop.
\paragraph{No Judge (\texttt{abl\_noJudge}).}
The \texttt{QualityGapEvaluator} node and the associated retry loop are removed
by connecting the Validate node directly to the Normalizer with an unconditional
edge.  No per-file gap report is produced, no structural failure detection is
performed, and no re-extraction cycle is triggered regardless of how many rules
were found.

% ¶  baseline.py: regex parser, knowledge limited to the 24 KG-seed nodes (ST01–ST04 only);
%     4 dedicated parse functions (SOP-001/002/003/004); no LLM; lower-bound reference.
\paragraph{Deterministic Baseline (\texttt{baseline\_b0}).}
A hand-crafted regex parser whose station-sensor inventory is derived from
and hard-coded to match the 24~nodes of the KG seed file
(\texttt{nodes\_factory.csv}): the four production stations
(\texttt{ST01}--\texttt{ST04}) and their three sensors each.
The five auxiliary sensor stations (\texttt{SRV01}, \texttt{WRH01}, \texttt{CHM01},
\texttt{RND01}, \texttt{CAF01}) do not appear in the seed and are therefore
structurally unreachable; all threshold and maintenance rules for those stations
represent entities that must be \emph{discovered} from the SOP text, a capability
the regex parser lacks by design.
It processes the four SOP document types with dedicated parsing functions, with no
LLM calls.  Its output serves as a lower-bound reference for what pre-existing
domain knowledge alone can achieve without any language understanding.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{sec:ablation_interpretation}

\textbf{Full pipeline superiority.}
The complete multi-agent pipeline achieves
F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.909, outperforming the best single-agent few-shot
baseline (\texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.728) by 0.181 points
  a relative improvement of approximately 25\%.
Its recall of~0.872 substantially exceeds every single-agent approach, and it
simultaneously maintains very high precision~(0.949), producing only 4~false
positives out of 79~extracted rules.
The 75~true positive content matches represent 87.2\% of the 86~ground-truth rules,
leaving just 11~rules unrecovered.

\textbf{Impact of the Quality Gap Evaluator.}
Removing the judge loop (\texttt{abl\_noJudge}) drops
F1\textsubscript{content} from 0.909 to 0.885, a loss of 0.024 points.
Notably, the noJudge run produces exactly the same number of predicted rules
(79) and the same strict TP count (58) as the full pipeline, but achieves
only 73~content TPs compared to~75 for the full run.
This means the judge+retry cycle does not substantially change \emph{which rules}
are found (the Validator already recovers most), but it does refine the quality of
a small number of rules whose field values are close to but not quite over the
$0.6$ content-agreement threshold.
In practice, the judge fires when a file produces zero rules of its expected
class; the retry re-extracts the full document with an exhaustive flag, and
the subsequent merge favours the better-worded version of any near-threshold
rule over the original.  A concrete illustration from the raw CSVs: \texttt{abl\_noJudge}
conditions read ``\textit{Flow drops below 105~L/min at ST01\_FILLING}'', while
after the judge+retry cycle the full pipeline normalises the same condition to
``\textit{Flow $\leq$ 105~L/min at ST01\_FILLING}'', a semantically equivalent
but more precise phrasing that crosses the $0.6$ content-agreement threshold in
the Hungarian assignment.

\textbf{Importance of the Validator.}
Without the Validator (\texttt{abl\_noValidator}),
F1\textsubscript{content} drops to 0.894, a loss of 0.015 points from the full
pipeline.
Counter-intuitively, the noValidator run produces \emph{fewer} predictions
(75) than the full pipeline (79), not more.  This is because the Validator's
dominant role here is the LLM \emph{gap-filling} pass: it actively adds rules
that the extractors never emitted by re-reading the source SOP against the
current rule set and recovering omissions.  Those 4 additional rules in the full
pipeline include 3~that are true positives and 1~false positive.
Without gap-filling, the pipeline emits only what the extractors found directly
(75~rules), and without the deterministic filter some rules that should have been
removed survive, yet the net prediction count is still lower because gap-filling
added more than the filter removed.
Precision therefore rises slightly (0.960 vs.\ 0.949): with fewer total predictions,
and specifically without the 1~spurious gap-filled rule, raw FP count falls to~3.
However, recall drops to 0.837 (from 0.872), recovering only 72~of 86 GT rules
instead of 75, because those 3 gap-filled TPs are absent.
Additionally, the sensor-identifier normalisation step is also bypassed: the raw
\texttt{abl\_noValidator} CSV shows abbreviated names such as \texttt{ST01\_FLW}
in place of the canonical \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_FLW}, which lowers the strict
TP count from 58 (full) to 54 and explains why the strict F1 penalty
(0.703$\to$0.671) is proportionally larger than the content F1 penalty
(0.909$\to$0.894).
The net trade is clear: the Validator contributes 3~additional content-true-positives
at the cost of 1~content-false-positive, a favourable exchange given that each
recovered rule is a safety-critical operational constraint that would otherwise be
absent from the Knowledge Graph.

\textbf{The LLM Coordinator vs.\ keyword heuristics.}
Replacing the LLM-based document classifier with the three-pattern keyword
classifier (\texttt{abl\_noCM}) reduces F1\textsubscript{content} to 0.872, a
loss of 0.037 points and the largest single-component drop in the study.
Interestingly, the recall is \emph{identical} to the full pipeline (both achieve
TP~=~75, FN~=~11): the keyword classifier routes all documents to the correct
extractor often enough that it does not miss any GT rules that the LLM classifier
finds.
The difference is entirely in precision: \texttt{abl\_noCM} extracts 86~rules
and incurs 11~FPs, while the full pipeline extracts only 79~rules with 4~FPs.
This reveals the LLM Coordinator's true contribution: it does not improve
\emph{what} the pipeline finds, but it prevents routing a document to the wrong
extractor prompt, which would cause that extractor to generate spurious rules
for the wrong rule class.  For example, a mixed-type document routed to the
narrative extractor might produce \texttt{OperationalRule} entries for rows
that should have been classified as \texttt{MaintenanceRule}, plausible-looking
rules that score just below the 0.6 content-agreement threshold and inflate FP.

\textbf{Deterministic baseline.}
The hand-crafted regex parser (\texttt{baseline\_b0}) achieves a perfect
\emph{strict} precision of~1.0 (strict~FP~=~0): every emitted rule identifier is
explicitly hard-coded from the known station-sensor inventory, so no spurious rules
can be generated.
Note that the table reports the \emph{content} metric, under which the baseline
still incurs 11~FPs (P\textsubscript{c}~=~0.804) because some extracted rules
match structurally but diverge in condition wording from the ground truth.
Its F1\textsubscript{content} of~0.634, driven by recall~0.523,
establishes the performance ceiling of pure domain knowledge without language
understanding.
Recovering just 45~of 86~GT rules, it misses 41~rules mostly the operationally
nuanced conditions, access constraints, and cross-sensor maintenance patterns that
require reading and interpreting sentence-level semantics.
Any change to the SOP layout or the addition of a new sensor would require
manual re-engineering of the regex rules, underscoring the brittleness that
motivates the LLM-based pipeline.

\textbf{Cross-system comparison.}
All four ablated LLM-based configurations (F1\textsubscript{content} 0.872--0.909)
substantially outperform the best single-agent approach (0.728).
Even the weakest ablation, \texttt{abl\_noCM} at 0.872, exceeds \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}
by 0.144 points nearly 20\% relative improvement despite using only a keyword
classifier to route documents.
This confirms that the multi-stage architecture itself (specialised extractors +
validator + judge) is the primary driver of quality, not any individual model or
any single pipeline stage.

\textbf{Overall conclusion.}
The ablation study demonstrates that every retained component contributes measurably
to the final extraction quality:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{LLM Coordinator} reduces false positives by ensuring each
          document is processed with the semantically appropriate extraction prompt.
    \item The \textbf{Validator} adds rules via LLM gap-filling (recovering rules
          present in the source text that the extractors missed), removes malformed
          candidates through the deterministic post-filter, and normalises sensor
          identifiers to canonical KG node names.
    \item The \textbf{Judge+Retry loop} refines rule quality at the margin by
          triggering a focused re-extraction of documents where the initial pass
          produced structurally incomplete rules.
    \item The \textbf{deterministic Consensus Merger} consolidates parallel extractor
          outputs without introducing LLM-induced merging errors, forwarding genuine
          ambiguities to nodes with grounding access.
\end{itemize}
Together, these stages enable the pipeline to recover 87.2\% of the ground-truth
operational rules with 94.9\% precision, a result that is unattainable by any
single LLM regardless of its prompting strategy.

\section{Multi-Run Stability and Inter-Agent Agreement}
\label{sec:multirun}

A single deterministic run (seed~42, temperature~0) establishes the nominal
performance of the pipeline, but it does not answer whether the extracted rule
set is \emph{stable}, i.e., whether re-running the pipeline under natural
LLM non-determinism produces substantially different outputs.
This is an important practical question for industrial deployment: a pipeline
whose output varies significantly across runs would require expensive human
review of every re-extraction.

To characterise this stability, the multi-run evaluation harness runs the complete multi-agent pipeline $K$
independent times with a temperature of $0.5$ and without a fixed seed, and
then measures agreement across runs using several complementary metrics.

% § 7.1  Patched llm_call omits seed and extra_body.seed; T=0.5; 20 runs; same model assignments as ablation.
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:multirun_setup}

Each run uses a monkey-patched version of the LLM call function.
The original \texttt{llm\_call} in the pipeline module passes
\texttt{seed=42} as a top-level API parameter and also embeds it inside
\texttt{extra\_body=\{"options": \{"seed": 42, \ldots\}\}} for Ollama-compatible
backends.
Before each pipeline invocation, the multi-run evaluation harness replaces
\texttt{\_pipe.llm\_call} and \texttt{\_pipe.save\_node} at runtime with
patched versions: the patched \texttt{llm\_call} omits both seed fields,
so the inference server draws a genuinely independent sample from the model's
distribution at every step; the patched \texttt{save\_node} redirects output to
a per-run file \texttt{ext\_multi\_agent\_run\{i:02d\}.csv} in the
\texttt{multi\_run/} subdirectory.
The default configuration executes \textbf{20 independent runs at temperature~0.5}
(i.e.\ \texttt{MULTI\_RUN\_TEMPERATURE}~=~0.5, \texttt{--runs 20}), trading a
modest increase in stochasticity against a robust sample size for the statistical
estimates below.
The same model assignments as the ablation study are used
(Table~\ref{tab:node_roles}).
Existing run files are detected and skipped automatically, so interrupted
experiments can be resumed; the \texttt{--skip-runs} flag bypasses pipeline
execution entirely and recomputes all statistics from the already-saved CSVs.

% § 7.2  compute_f1_stats: per-run F1 via run_evaluation(); aggregate mean/std/CI95 (z=1.96/√K).
\subsection{F1 Aggregation Across Runs}
\label{sec:multirun_f1}

For each run CSV, the evaluation framework computes
F1\textsubscript{strict}, F1\textsubscript{content}, and all associated
precision, recall, TP, FP, FN counts.
A summary row is computed by taking the mean and standard deviation of each
metric across all $K$ runs, and a 95\% confidence interval is derived using
the standard formula $\text{CI}_{95} = 1.96 \cdot \sigma / \sqrt{K}$.
This allows claims such as
``the pipeline achieves F1\textsubscript{content}~=~$\mu \pm \sigma$'' to be
made with statistical grounding, rather than relying on a single seed.

% § 7.3  IAAS: pairwise semantic agreement across all K(K-1)/2 run pairs via content_agreement + Hungarian.
\subsection{Inter-Agent Agreement Score (IAAS)}
\label{sec:iaas}

Beyond aggregate F1, the script computes the \emph{Inter-Agent Agreement Score}
(IAAS), a pairwise measure of how much two runs of the pipeline agree on the
semantic content of extracted rules.
Unlike F1, which compares each run to a fixed ground truth, IAAS compares
pairs of \emph{pipeline outputs} to one another, measuring internal consistency
regardless of whether either run is correct.

% § 7.3.1  5-field text (class|station|sensor|condition|action) → SBERT all-MiniLM-L6-v2;
%           shared embedding cache built once for all rules across all runs.
\subsubsection{Rule Embedding and Content Agreement}

Two distinct SBERT encoding steps are used, each serving a different downstream
computation.

\paragraph{Content agreement (pairwise IAAS).}
The \texttt{content\_agreement} function imported from
the rule evaluation module computes a class-dependent weighted
average over all active fields of a rule pair.
For each non-empty field $f$ of the reference rule, a per-field score
$s_f \in [0,1]$ is assigned: SBERT cosine similarity for text fields
(\texttt{condition}, \texttt{action}); exact string match for categorical fields
(\texttt{class}, \texttt{station}, \texttt{sensor}, etc.); and a proportional
tolerance score for numeric threshold fields (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{critLo},
etc.).
These scores are combined with class-dependent weights $w_f$ from
\texttt{FIELD\_WEIGHTS}: for instance, \texttt{OperationalRule} assigns
$w_{\text{condition}}=4.0$ and $w_{\text{action}}=2.0$, while
\texttt{ThresholdRule} places greatest weight on numeric threshold fields
($w_{\text{critHi/Lo}}=3.0$ each) and lower weights on text fields ($0.5$).
To avoid redundant SBERT inference, \texttt{\_embed\_all\_rules} pre-encodes all
\texttt{condition} and \texttt{action} texts from every run in a single batch
using the \texttt{sentence-transformers}/\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model, storing
the results in a shared dictionary cache keyed by text string.
This cache is passed to every subsequent \texttt{content\_agreement} call,
ensuring each unique text is encoded exactly once across all $K(K{-}1)/2$
pairwise comparisons.

\paragraph{Rule-level embedding (clustering).}
For clustering, each rule is represented as a single concatenated string:
\texttt{class}~|~\texttt{station}~|~\texttt{sensor}~|~\texttt{condition}~|~\texttt{action},
where empty fields are omitted.
All unique such strings across all runs are encoded jointly in one SBERT batch
(duplicates removed via \texttt{dict.fromkeys} before encoding), and the
resulting per-text embeddings are stored in a lookup dictionary used during the
greedy clustering pass.
This encoding is independent of the pairwise cache above.

% § 7.3.2  _pairwise_iaas: Hungarian on CA cost matrix; score / max(|Ri|,|Rj|) penalises cardinality drift.
\subsubsection{Pairwise IAAS Computation}

For each ordered pair of runs $(i, j)$, a cost matrix
$C \in \mathbb{R}^{|R_i| \times |R_j|}$ is constructed where
$C_{ab} = \texttt{content\_agreement}(r^i_a,\, r^j_b)$, i.e.\ the semantic
agreement score (as defined above) between rule $a$ from run $i$ and rule $b$
from run $j$.
The Hungarian algorithm (\texttt{scipy.optimize.linear\_sum\_assignment}) then
finds the one-to-one assignment that \emph{maximises} the total agreement,
yielding the optimal bipartite matching between the two rule sets.
The IAAS score for the pair is:
\begin{equation}
\text{IAAS}(i,j) = \frac{\sum_{(a,b) \in \text{matching}} CA(r^i_a, r^j_b)}
                        {\max(|R_i|, |R_j|)}
\label{eq:iaas_pairwise}
\end{equation}
where $R_i$ and $R_j$ are the rule sets of runs $i$ and $j$,
$CA(\cdot,\cdot)$ denotes the content agreement score (class-dependent weighted
average over all active rule fields, as described in Section~\ref{sec:iaas}),
and unmatched rules contribute~0 to the numerator.
Dividing by $\max(|R_i|, |R_j|)$ rather than the size of either set
individually penalises cardinality differences: a run that extracts many
spurious extra rules receives a lower IAAS even if all its matched rules are
semantically close, because the denominator grows with the larger set.
The global IAAS reported in Table~\ref{tab:iaas_results} is the mean over all
$\binom{K}{2} = K(K{-}1)/2$ pairwise scores.

% § 7.3.3  _cluster_rules: greedy SBERT cosine clustering (threshold=0.90, centroid updated as mean);
%           binary presence matrix → Fleiss κ; hallucination <30%, consensus ≥70% of runs.
\subsubsection{Rule Clustering, Fleiss~$\kappa$, and Consensus}

\paragraph{Greedy clustering.}
All rules from all $K$ runs are pooled and clustered greedily using the
five-field rule text embeddings computed in the previous step.
Each rule is processed in order: it is assigned to the existing cluster whose
current centroid embedding has the highest cosine similarity to the rule's
embedding, provided that similarity is at least
\texttt{SIMILARITY\_THRESHOLD}~=~0.90; if no such cluster exists, a new
singleton cluster is created.
After each assignment, the cluster centroid is updated to the mean of all
member embeddings (computed by stacking and averaging the member tensors).
Each resulting cluster represents a single \emph{conceptual rule}, a
semantically distinct operational constraint, that may have been extracted
in some runs but not others.

\paragraph{Binary presence matrix and Fleiss~$\kappa$.}
A binary presence matrix $M \in \{0,1\}^{N_{\text{clusters}} \times K}$ is
constructed, where $M_{c,r} = 1$ if at least one member of cluster~$c$ came
from run~$r$, and~0 otherwise.
In the Fleiss~$\kappa$ framework, the \emph{items} are the $N_{\text{clusters}}$
unique rule concepts, the \emph{raters} are the $K$~pipeline runs, and the
\emph{binary rating} for each item--rater pair is presence or absence of that
concept in that run.
Fleiss~$\kappa$ is computed over this matrix to quantify inter-run reliability
beyond chance: $\kappa = 0$ means agreement no better than random, $\kappa = 1$
means perfect agreement, and negative values indicate systematic disagreement.

\paragraph{Stability labels and consensus representative.}
Each cluster is assigned a stability label based on the fraction of runs in
which it appears ($\text{run\_frac}_c = M_{c,\cdot}\mathbf{1} / K$):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Hallucination proxy} if
          $\text{run\_frac}_c < \texttt{HALLUCINATION\_FRAC} = 0.30$:
          the rule concept appears in fewer than 30\% of runs, suggesting it
          is an artefact of LLM randomness rather than a genuine operational
          constraint.
    \item \emph{Consensus rule} if
          $\text{run\_frac}_c \geq \texttt{CONSENSUS\_FRAC} = 0.70$:
          the rule concept appears in at least 70\% of runs, confirming
          stable, reproducible extraction.
    \item \emph{Borderline} otherwise (30--70\%): the concept is extracted
          inconsistently and warrants human review.
\end{itemize}
For each consensus cluster, the most representative rule, the cluster member
with the highest mean content agreement to all other members in the same
cluster, is selected as the canonical extraction and written to
\texttt{step3\_results/consensus\_rules.csv}, augmented with a
\texttt{run\_frac} column recording the empirical stability fraction.
This file constitutes the most deployment-trustworthy subset of the
pipeline's output.

% § 7.4  Four output artefacts: per-run CSVs, multi_run_stats.csv, iaas_report.json, consensus_rules.csv.
\subsection{Outputs}
\label{sec:multirun_outputs}

Running the multi-run evaluation harness (with defaults: 20 runs,
temperature~0.5) produces four output artefacts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{step2\_results/multi\_run/ext\_multi\_agent\_run\{i:02d\}.csv}
           , one extraction CSV per run, containing the normalised rule fields
            output by the multi-agent pipeline for that run.
    \item \texttt{step3\_results/multi\_run\_stats.csv}, per-run F1 metrics
          (strict and content F1, precision, recall, TP/FP/FN, and total predicted
          count) plus an \texttt{AGGREGATE} summary row with mean, standard
          deviation, and 95\%~CI for every metric.
    \item \texttt{step3\_results/iaas\_report.json}, IAAS mean/std, Fleiss~$\kappa$,
          hallucination rate, consensus rate, cluster count, per-pair IAAS scores,
          and the threshold constants used.
    \item \texttt{step3\_results/consensus\_rules.csv}, the consensus rule set
          (rules appearing in $\geq 70\%$ of runs), one row per cluster, with
          an additional \texttt{run\_frac} column recording each rule's stability
          fraction across the $K$ runs.
\end{itemize}
If the per-run CSVs already exist (e.g.\ after a partial run), passing
\texttt{--skip-runs} bypasses the pipeline execution step entirely and
recomputes all statistics directly from the existing files.

% § 7.5  20-run F1 table + IAAS table: mean F1_content=0.864±0.023 (CI±0.010),
%         IAAS=0.942±0.027, Fleiss κ=0.596, 68/91 consensus (74.7%), 20/91 hallucination (22%).

% § 7.6  Two-tier deployment strategy: auto-load 68 consensus rules; queue 23 non-consensus for human review.
\subsection{Significance of Stability Analysis for Industrial Deployment}
\label{sec:multirun_significance}

The multi-run evaluation addresses a gap that pure benchmark F1 cannot close.
An F1\textsubscript{content} of 0.909 on a single seed (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results})
proves that the pipeline can achieve high quality; the measured IAAS~=~0.942~$\pm$~0.027
proves that it does so \emph{reliably}.
In an industrial Digital Twin context where SOP re-extraction may be triggered
by document updates, the consensus rate of 74.7\% (68 of 91 unique rule concepts)
means that operators can trust that the core Knowledge Graph the rules that appear
in $\geq$70\% of runs will remain stable between extraction cycles, reducing the
burden of manual post-verification.
The measured hallucination-proxy rate of 22.0\% (20 of 91 unique rule concepts)
identifies the fraction of extracted rule concepts that are artefacts of LLM
randomness rather than genuine operational constraints: these 20 concepts appeared
in fewer than 30\% of runs and cannot be trusted to survive re-extraction.
A further 3 borderline concepts (30--70\% prevalence) are extracted inconsistently
and also require review.
In total, the 23 non-consensus rule concepts (20 hallucination proxies + 3
borderline) warrant a filtered deployment strategy: the 68 consensus rules (written
to \texttt{step3\_results/consensus\_rules.csv} with their empirical
\texttt{run\_frac} values) are immediately deployable, while all 23 non-consensus
rules should be queued for human review before loading into a production graph.
This two-tier deployment strategy, auto-load consensus, review non-consensus,
is directly enabled by the IAAS framework and represents a practical operationalisation
of uncertainty quantification for LLM-based knowledge extraction systems.

% § 8  Knowledge Graph Construction and Phase-2 Validation
\section{Knowledge Graph Construction and Phase-2 Validation}
\label{sec:neo4j}

Layer~4 answers one practical question: \emph{are the rules that the pipeline
extracted actually useful?} Two sub-questions guide the evaluation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Q1:Anomaly detection.} Can the threshold rules stored in
        the graph fire on the real sensor data at the right time:i.e.\ inside
        the labelled anomaly windows?
  \item \textbf{Q2:Maintenance coverage.} Does the graph contain a
        maintenance rule for every documented maintenance action?
\end{enumerate}
Layer~4 runs two scripts in sequence: \texttt{step4\_populate.py} builds the
knowledge graph in Neo4j, and \texttt{step5\_match.py} evaluates whether those
rules work.

% ── § 8.1  Building the graph ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Building the Knowledge Graph (\texttt{step4\_populate.py})}
\label{sec:step4}

\paragraph{What is the input?}
The only input is \texttt{layers/step3\_results/consensus\_rules.csv}:the
68~consensus rules produced by Layer~3's multi-run aggregation.
No ground-truth file is touched at this stage.
The graph reflects exactly what the LLM pipeline extracted on its own.

\paragraph{Graph schema.}
Three kinds of nodes are created, and three relationship types connect them.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Station node}:one node per physical location, e.g.\
        \texttt{ST02\_SEALING}.
        Property: \texttt{stationId}.

  \item \textbf{Sensor node}:one node per sensor, e.g.\
        \texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_TMP}.
        Properties: \texttt{sensorId}, \texttt{sensorType} (e.g.\ \texttt{TMP},
        \texttt{PRS}, \texttt{VIB}), \texttt{unit}.

  \item \textbf{Rule node}:one node per extracted rule.
        Properties: \texttt{ruleId}, \texttt{class}
        (\texttt{ThresholdRule} or \texttt{MaintenanceRule}),
        \texttt{condition}, \texttt{action}, \texttt{severity},
        four numeric threshold fields
        (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo}, \texttt{critLo}),
        \texttt{unit}, and \texttt{sourceFile}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{(Station)-[:HAS\_SENSOR]->(Sensor)} links each station to its
        installed sensors.
  \item \textbf{(Rule)-[:GOVERNS]->(Sensor)} expresses which sensor a rule
        monitors.  Only rules that carry this edge are evaluated in Step~5;
        rules without a sensor (e.g.\ access-control rules) are stored as
        isolated \texttt{Rule} nodes and ignored in the evaluation.
  \item \textbf{(Rule)-[:APPLIES\_TO]->(Station)} links a rule to its station.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What the script does.}
Starting from the 68~consensus rules, the script:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Clears any existing graph (unless \texttt{--no-clear} is passed).
  \item Creates 9~\texttt{Station} nodes from the distinct station names in the
        rules.
  \item Creates 22~\texttt{Sensor} nodes, setting \texttt{sensorType} and
        \texttt{unit} from the first rule that provides them; adds a
        \texttt{HAS\_SENSOR} edge from the parent station.
  \item Creates 68~\texttt{Rule} nodes, writing all extracted properties
        including the four threshold fields as floats.
        For each rule that has a sensor, adds a \texttt{GOVERNS} edge; for
        each rule that has a station, adds an \texttt{APPLIES\_TO} edge.
\end{enumerate}
After loading, the graph contains \textbf{99 nodes} and \textbf{three relationship
types}, all derived purely from LLM-extracted knowledge.

% ── § 8.2  Rule usability evaluation ─────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Rule Usability Evaluation (\texttt{step5\_match.py})}
\label{sec:step5}

\texttt{step5\_match.py} evaluates whether the rules in the graph can do
real work.  The evaluation is split into two phases so that the detection step
is completely independent of the answer key.

\paragraph{Graph fields read from Neo4j.}
Step~5 issues a single Cypher query
(\texttt{MATCH (r:Rule)-[:GOVERNS]->(s:Sensor)}, with an optional
\texttt{MATCH (st:Station)-[:HAS\_SENSOR]->(s)}) and reads the following
fields.  Table~\ref{tab:graph_fields} shows exactly which field is used for
what purpose.

\begin{table}[H]

\label{tab:graph_fields}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{Source node} & \textbf{How reached} & \textbf{Used for} \\
\midrule

\paragraph{Role of \texttt{ruleId} in the matching pipeline.}
Because rule extraction by LLMs is imperfect, the extracted \texttt{ruleId}
strings will often differ from any ground-truth identifier.
This does \emph{not} affect detection, because \texttt{ruleId} is
\textbf{never used to find candidate rules}.

The lookup chain in \texttt{step5\_match.py} works as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]

  \item \textbf{GT event $\to$ sensor.}
    The ground-truth event in \texttt{nodes.csv} is linked via a
    \texttt{triggers} edge in \texttt{edges.csv} to a \texttt{Sensor}
    node.  The system reads \textbf{only} the sensor identifier
    (e.g.\ \texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_TMP}) from that edge — no ruleId is
    consulted.

  \item \textbf{Sensor $\to$ candidate rules.}
    The system looks up \emph{all} \texttt{ThresholdRule} nodes in the
    graph that have a \texttt{GOVERNS} edge pointing to that sensor:
    \verb|rules_by_sensor.get(gt_sensor, [])| (line~280).
    Any rule with the correct \texttt{sensor} field will appear here,
    regardless of what its \texttt{ruleId} string says.

  \item \textbf{Candidate rules $\to$ violations.}
    Phase~1 pre-computed violations keyed by
    \texttt{(sensor\_id, extracted\_ruleId)}.  Phase~2 looks up that
    same key (line~299), so the extracted \texttt{ruleId} is used
    \emph{internally} as a dictionary key but is never compared against
    any ground-truth identifier.

  \item \textbf{Best rule selection.}
    Among all candidates, the rule that (a)~fired inside the GT window
    and (b)~matched the most structural fields
    (\texttt{station}, \texttt{sensorType}) is reported in the output.
    \texttt{ruleId} plays no role in this ranking.

\end{enumerate}

\noindent
\textbf{What can actually break detection.}
A wrong \texttt{ruleId} is harmless.
The only two fields that determine whether a rule is found and fires are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{\texttt{sensor}} — if the LLM extracts the wrong sensor
    name, \texttt{step4\_populate.py} creates a \texttt{GOVERNS} edge to
    the wrong \texttt{Sensor} node, and the rule will never appear as a
    candidate for the correct event (status \textsc{no\_rule}).
  \item \textbf{threshold values} (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi},
    \texttt{warnLo}, \texttt{critLo}) if the extracted thresholds are
    wrong, the Phase-1 mask may not fire within the anomaly window
    (status \textsc{covered\_no\_fire}).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Phase~1:Autonomous threshold scan (no ground truth)}

The first phase runs every \texttt{ThresholdRule} that has a
\texttt{GOVERNS} edge over the complete sensor timeseries
(\texttt{timeseries\_raw.csv}: 211\,200 rows, 30-second readings, $\approx$3.3~days,
22~sensors).  For each rule the script applies the threshold bounds that were
extracted from the source documents:

\[
  \text{violation} =
    \bigl(\text{value} > \mathtt{critHi}\bigr)
    \;\lor\; \bigl(\text{value} > \mathtt{warnHi}\bigr)
    \;\lor\; \bigl(\text{value} < \mathtt{warnLo}\bigr)
    \;\lor\; \bigl(\text{value} < \mathtt{critLo}\bigr)
\]

Only threshold fields that were actually extracted (non-null) participate in
the check.  Every timestamp at which the condition is true is recorded.
The result is a dictionary:
\[
  \textit{violations} = \bigl\{\,
    (\textit{sensor\_id},\; \textit{rule\_id}) \;\mapsto\; [\,t_1, t_2, \ldots\,]
  \,\bigr\}
\]
Ground-truth information is \emph{not used} at this stage.  The rules scan the
full timeseries autonomously.

\subsubsection{Phase~2:Ground-truth window matching}

The second phase checks whether the autonomous violations from Phase~1 happened
to coincide with the labelled anomaly events.  For each of the 14~ground-truth
\texttt{AnomalyEvent} records in \texttt{nodes.csv}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Find the trigger sensor.} The \texttt{edges.csv} file contains
        edges of type \texttt{triggers} that link each sensor to the event it
        caused.  This sensor is the ``expected'' sensor for the event.

  \item \textbf{Find candidate rules.} All \texttt{ThresholdRule} nodes in
        the graph that have a \texttt{GOVERNS} edge to that sensor are
        collected.  If none exist the event is marked \textbf{NO\_RULE}.

  \item \textbf{Intersect with the GT window.} For each candidate rule, the
        Phase-1 violation timestamps are intersected with the labelled window
        $[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$.  The rule \textbf{fires} for
        this event if at least one violation falls inside the window.

  \item \textbf{Compare rule fields with timeseries metadata.}
        Three fields are compared:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \texttt{station\_match}: does the rule's station equal the
                sensor's station in the timeseries file?
          \item \texttt{sensor\_match}: does the rule's sensor equal the
                timeseries sensor id?  (Always true by construction.)
          \item \texttt{sensorType\_match}: does the rule's sensor type equal
                the timeseries sensor type?
        \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Pick the best rule.} If multiple candidates exist, the
        script prefers a rule that fired; among equally-firing rules it picks
        the one with the most matching fields.
\end{enumerate}

Each event is assigned one of four statuses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{FIRES}:at least one Phase-1 violation fell inside the
        labelled window.
  \item \textbf{COVERED\_NO\_FIRE}:a rule exists for the sensor, but no
        violation was detected inside the window.
  \item \textbf{NO\_RULE}:the graph contains no rule governing the trigger
        sensor.
  \item \textbf{CORRELATED\_SKIP}:the event is a multi-sensor correlation
        anomaly that cannot be detected by a single-sensor threshold rule; it
        is excluded from the denominator.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Q2:Maintenance structural check}

For the eight \texttt{Maintenance} nodes the evaluation is structural, not
temporal.  The script looks up the trigger sensor from \texttt{edges.csv} and
then checks whether the graph contains a \texttt{MaintenanceRule} with a
\texttt{GOVERNS} edge to that sensor.  If found, the same three-field
comparison is performed.  No timeseries scan is run here: maintenance actions
are triggered by persistent patterns such as drift or stuck sensors, which
require dedicated detectors beyond the scope of this evaluation.

\subsubsection{Q1:Anomaly Detection}

Table~\ref{tab:anomaly_results} shows the per-event results.
Of the 14~ground-truth anomaly events, one (GT-0009, a correlated anomaly) is
excluded by design, leaving \textbf{13~evaluated events}.
The LLM-extracted threshold rules fired inside the correct ground-truth window
for \textbf{11 out of 13} events.
All 13~sensors were covered by at least one rule:the pipeline produced a
rule for every relevant sensor, so the \textbf{NO\_RULE} status did not occur.

The two events where no violation fired are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GT-0004 (STUCK / WARNING, \texttt{ST03\_LABELLING\_TEN})}.
        The sensor was frozen at a constant value \emph{within} the normal
        operating range.  A threshold rule cannot detect this: if the reading
        never crosses a bound, no violation fires.
        This is a known structural limitation of threshold-based monitoring,
        not a failure of the extraction pipeline.

  \item \textbf{GT-0011 (OUT\_OF\_RANGE / CRITICAL,
        \texttt{CHM01\_CHEMICALSTORAGE\_TMP})}.
        The ground truth defines two thresholds for this sensor:
        \texttt{warnHi=20.0°C} and \texttt{critHi=25.0°C}.
        The LLM extracted only \texttt{critHi=25.0°C}, omitting
        \texttt{warnHi=20.0°C}.
        The \textsc{out\_of\_range} anomaly type is defined as a sustained
        \emph{warning-level} violation (temperature above the warning bound
        for 20 or more consecutive readings), not a critical one.
        The sensor readings during the labelled window exceeded 20°C but never
        reached 25°C, so the incomplete rule produces zero violations.
        This is a partial extraction failure: had \texttt{warnHi=20.0°C} been
        captured in the graph, the rule would have fired.
\end{itemize}

    This count is produced entirely autonomously in Phase~1.

  \item \textbf{Violations inside GT window.}\ \
    Of the Phase-1 violation timestamps above, this column counts only
    those that also fall inside the labelled anomaly window
    $[t_\text{start},\, t_\text{end}]$ taken from \texttt{nodes.csv}.
    This intersection is computed in Phase~2 purely for evaluation;
    the threshold scan itself used no ground-truth information.

  \item \textbf{Status.}\ \
    \textsc{fires}: at least one violation timestamp falls inside the GT
    window, so the rule would have raised an alert during the event.
    \textsc{cov.\ no fire} : a rule exists for the sensor, but no reading
    crossed a threshold inside the window (the rule is present but did not
    trigger for this event type).
    \textsc{corr.\ skip} : the event (GT-0009) requires correlating
    multiple sensors simultaneously; single-sensor threshold rules cannot
    detect it by design, so it is excluded from the 13-event denominator.

\end{itemize}

\medskip
\textbf{Why GT-0003 and GT-0014 both show 9\,500 — an extraction quality artefact.}
Both events involve the same sensor (\texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_VIB}).
The ground-truth thresholds for \texttt{RULE-ST04-01} are
\texttt{critHi}=0.35, \textbf{\texttt{warnHi}=0.20},
\texttt{warnLo}=0.02, \texttt{critLo}=0.01.
The LLM extracted \texttt{critHi}=0.35 correctly but placed
\textbf{\texttt{warnLo}=0.20} instead of \texttt{warnHi}=0.20,
and missed \texttt{warnLo}=0.02 and \texttt{critLo}=0.01 entirely.
Because the sensor's typical operating vibration is
$\approx$0.12\,mm/s, the incorrect mask
\texttt{value}~$<$~0.20 fires on 9\,498 of 9\,600 readings;
the correct mask \texttt{value}~$>$~\texttt{warnHi}=0.20 would fire
on only $\approx$102.
The 9\,500 count is therefore an artefact of the threshold field
being placed on the wrong side, not a meaningful violation density.
The correctly extracted rule \texttt{RULE-THR-ST04-PACK-VIB-CRIT}
(all four thresholds matching ground truth) is also present in the
graph and also fires for both events, but \texttt{RULE-ST04-01} is
selected first because both rules fire with equal structural field
scores.
The 11/13 detection rate is therefore unaffected by this error.
Because Phase-1 is computed once per (sensor, rule) pair, both events
draw from this same pool of 9\,500 timestamps; they differ only in
how many fall inside their respective event windows: 81 for GT-0003
(a 90-minute drift window) and 3 for GT-0014 (a 1-minute spike window).

\medskip
\textbf{How DRIFT and SPIKE anomaly types are detected by threshold rules.}
Threshold rules do not detect temporal patterns such as gradual drift or
sudden spikes.  They detect only whether an instantaneous sensor reading
crosses a stored bound.  DRIFT and SPIKE events fire in this evaluation
because the anomalous values were large enough to go \emph{out of bounds}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item GT-0001 (SPIKE, \texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_TMP}): temperature spiked
    from a baseline of $\approx$185\,°C to 212\,°C during the event
    window, crossing \texttt{critHi}=195\,°C on 5~readings.
  \item GT-0002 (DRIFT, \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_PRS}): pressure drifted
    gradually from $\approx$3.8\,bar up to 4.43\,bar over 45~minutes,
    crossing \texttt{warnHi}=4.2\,bar on 30 of the 92 window readings.
  \item GT-0012 (DRIFT, \texttt{WRH01\_WAREHOUSE\_TMP}): temperature
    drifted from $\approx$4\,°C to 10.5\,°C over 3~hours, crossing
    \texttt{warnHi}=8\,°C on all 120 later readings in the window.
\end{itemize}
By contrast, GT-0004 (STUCK, \texttt{ST03\_LABELLING\_TEN}) produces
zero violations because the sensor freezes at a constant value that
remains within the normal operating range; no threshold is ever crossed.
This is the fundamental limitation of single-value threshold detection:
it catches out-of-bounds consequences of drift and spike events, but
cannot detect a stuck sensor or a drift that stays within bounds.
\end{minipage}

\subsubsection{Summary}

The Layer~4 evaluation shows that the rules extracted autonomously by the LLM
pipeline are largely usable for real-world monitoring:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Q1:} 11 out of 13 evaluated anomaly events were detected by
        threshold rules firing inside the correct time window.  The two missed
        events have clear, explainable causes: one is a sensor stuck within
        normal bounds (a structural limitation of threshold rules, not of the
        extraction pipeline), and one is a partial extraction failure where
        the LLM captured the critical bound but omitted the warning bound that
        the anomaly actually crossed.

  \item \textbf{Q2:} 8 out of 8 maintenance actions were covered by a matching
        rule, with all field properties correctly populated.
\end{itemize}

These results confirm that the knowledge graph built from LLM-extracted rules
is operationally applicable: it would fire the correct alert for 11 of the 13
detectable anomaly scenarios and would recommend the correct maintenance action
in all 8 documented cases.

% ── § 8.4  Limitations ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:layer4_limitations}

The evaluation has four structural limitations that bound the interpretation
of the results.

\paragraph{Threshold-based rules cannot detect stuck sensors.}
GT-0004 (\textsc{stuck} / WARNING, \texttt{ST03\_LABELLING\_TEN}) will always
produce a COVERED\_NO\_FIRE result regardless of how accurately the pipeline
extracts the rule.
A \textsc{stuck} anomaly means the sensor output is frozen at a constant value
that lies \emph{within} the normal operating range, so it never crosses any
threshold bound.
Detecting stuck sensors requires a separate mechanism, such as checking the
standard deviation of recent readings.
This is a limitation of the evaluation method, not of the knowledge graph.

\paragraph{Partial threshold extraction causes missed detections.}
GT-0011 (\textsc{out\_of\_range} / CRITICAL, \texttt{CHM01\_CHEMICALSTORAGE\_TMP})
missed because the LLM extracted only \texttt{critHi=25.0°C} and omitted
\texttt{warnHi=20.0°C}.
The \textsc{out\_of\_range} anomaly type is defined as a sustained
warning-level violation; the sensor exceeded 20°C but not 25°C during the
event window, so the incomplete rule produced zero violations.
This is a genuine extraction failure: it reflects a case where the LLM
captured the critical bound but missed the warning bound from the same source
document.
Had the full rule been extracted, the rule would have fired.

\paragraph{GT-0009 is out of scope for single-sensor threshold rules
(primary evaluation discriminator).}
GT-0009 is the only \textsc{correlated} event in the dataset and represents
the central scientific challenge of the iMAKS benchmark.
It encodes a speed reduction at \texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING} caused by a current
drift at \texttt{ST02\_SEALING} through shared electrical coupling, with a
90-minute temporal lag between cause and effect.

This causal triple cannot be extracted from any single source document.
Correct extraction requires explicit three-way evidence fusion:
(1)~a temporal co-occurrence pattern in the timeseries, specifically a
\texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_CUR} DRIFT followed within 90~minutes by a
\texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_SPD} reduction;
(2)~\texttt{RULE-ST02-04} in SOP-001 \S2.2, which asserts the causal
correlation between these two sensors through shared electrical coupling;
(3)~the correlated fault procedure in SOP-003 \S2, which names the
ST02$\to$ST04 propagation pattern and specifies the correct response.
No single source is individually sufficient; each provides one piece of
the causal argument.

Because no single \texttt{ThresholdRule} in the graph can capture a
cross-station causal relationship, GT-0009 is excluded from the
denominator of Q1 by design.
It is evaluated as a separate binary question: does the pipeline produce
a rule that explicitly encodes the ST02$\to$ST04 causal edge?
A pipeline that correctly extracts this edge has demonstrated
architecture-level multi-source fusion capability, which is a qualitatively
different result from correctly extracting a per-sensor threshold.
The current pipeline does not produce this edge; this is therefore an
acknowledged open limitation of the system.

\paragraph{Threshold rules are pattern-blind: DRIFT and SPIKE are not distinguished.}
A threshold rule asks only one question at each reading:
\emph{does the current value cross a stored bound?}
It has no memory of previous readings and cannot detect whether a violation
was caused by a sudden spike, a gradual drift, or any other temporal pattern.
DRIFT and SPIKE events in the evaluation fire for the same mechanical reason:
the anomalous value happened to exceed a threshold at that instant.
For example, GT-0002 (\textsc{drift}, \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_PRS}) is
detected because the pressure drifted to 4.43\,bar and crossed
\texttt{warnHi}=4.2\,bar; GT-0001 (\textsc{spike},
\texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_TMP}) is detected because the temperature jumped to
212\,°C and crossed \texttt{critHi}=195\,°C.
In both cases the rule fires for the same reason — a threshold crossing —
and would equally fire if the causes were reversed.
Pattern-aware detection (e.g.\ rate-of-change checks for drift, or
short-window variance for spikes) would require rule types beyond the
current \texttt{ThresholdRule} schema.

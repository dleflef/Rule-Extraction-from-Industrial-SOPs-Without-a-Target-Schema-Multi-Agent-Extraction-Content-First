\title{\textbf{Agentic Knowledge Graph Construction for Digital Twins:\\
A Multi-Agent LLM Pipeline for Industrial SOP Rule Extraction}}

\author{Dleflef}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Industrial Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) encode safety-critical operational
rules across heterogeneous formats narrative procedures, numeric threshold tables,
and role-zone permission matrices that resist uniform extraction strategies.
Standard F1 metrics further conflate naming accuracy with content quality: in the
industrial Multi-Agent Knowledge extraction Synthetic dataset (iMAKS) benchmark,
many identifiers are annotated with conventions absent from the source text,
making exact-match metrics unreliable for evaluating operational correctness.

We adopt \emph{F1\textsubscript{content}}, a content-first metric that uses
Hungarian optimal assignment and SBERT similarity to evaluate operational correctness
independently of naming conventions.
A comprehensive single-agent grid search over five LLMs (4\,B to 20\,B parameters)
and ten prompting paradigms on the iMAKS corpus establishes a best single-agent
performance of F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.728
(\texttt{gpt-oss-20b} with few-shot static prompting).

Building on these baselines, we design a multi-agent pipeline in which a
Document Type Coordinator routes each SOP to one of three specialised extractors.
The pipeline adds a deterministic Consensus Merger for deduplication, a Validator
for gap-filling and scrubbing, an iterative Quality Gap Evaluator with re-extraction
loops, and an Identifier Canonicalizer.
An ablation study shows that the full pipeline achieves
F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.909, surpassing the best single-agent baseline by
0.181 points (relative improvement of $\approx$25\%).
Removing the iterative judge loop drops F1\textsubscript{content} to 0.885;
removing the Validator decreases it to 0.894.
These results demonstrate that collaborative multi-stage refinement specifically
the combination of type-aware extraction, additive gap-filling, and iterative
quality verification is the primary driver of extraction quality on
structurally heterogeneous industrial corpora.

We note that the Identifier Canonicalizer uses 20 ground-truth ruleId examples as
naming demonstrations; this may modestly inflate F1\textsubscript{strict} but does
not affect F1\textsubscript{content}, which is the primary reported metric, since
the content agreement function does not score ruleId at all.

The best extraction run is subsequently integrated into a Neo4j Knowledge Graph,
where the automatically extracted rules are overlaid on top of a hand-crafted
industrial ABox comprising 115 nodes and 341 edges representing the factory's
physical structure, sensors, anomaly events, maintenance procedures, and personnel
access rights.
A phase-2 graph coverage evaluation shows that the pipeline's extracted rules cover
14/14 anomaly events and 8/8 maintenance procedures, for an overall coverage of
100\% (well above the 70\% design threshold).
A multi-run stability study over 20 independent pipeline runs at temperature~0.5
yields a mean F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.864~$\pm$~0.023 (95\%\,CI~$\pm$0.010),
an Inter-Agent Agreement Score (IAAS)~=~0.942~$\pm$~0.027, and a Fleiss~$\kappa$~=~0.596,
confirming that 74.7\% of extracted rule concepts are stable across stochastic runs.
A qualitative inspection of the \texttt{:GOVERNS} edges reveals residual quality
issues: temporal drift thresholds from SOP-003 maintenance conditions are sometimes
conflated with static alarm limits in \texttt{ThresholdSpec} nodes, and isolated
unit-dimension mismatches exist at the semantic edge level indicating that
field-level quality validation beyond the structural coverage metric is warranted.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% § 1.1  Motivates Digital Twin + KG adoption; bottlenecks in high-quality KG construction.
\subsection{Context and Motivation}
In recent years, there has been a growing adoption of Digital Twins in complex
industrial domains. Within these environments, the Knowledge Graph (KG) is
increasingly recognised as the cognitive backbone of Digital Twins, though its
implementation is often hindered by the limitations of the current state of the
art. Industrial facilities generate knowledge across various simultaneous streams,
ranging from continuous numerical measurements produced by sensors to Standard
Operating Procedures (SOPs) that encode alarm thresholds and maintenance rules in
natural language.

Despite the potential of KGs to unify this data, there are significant bottlenecks
in high-quality KG construction, particularly concerning costs, scalability, and
semantic maintenance. Relying on manual extraction or centralised processing
pipelines is no longer viable for modern facilities. Consequently, there is a
pressing need for intelligent automation through multi-agent systems capable of
processing heterogeneous technical documentation and resolving inter-document
tensions.

% § 1.2  Missing agent-specialisation frameworks, conflict-resolution studies, temporal reasoning in KGs.
\subsection{Literature Gaps}
A review of current KG construction methodologies reveals several critical gaps.
Primarily, there is an absence of systematic architectures for agent specialisation,
such as frameworks that clearly separate the roles of entity extraction, relation
validation, and ontology alignment agents. Furthermore, there are no comprehensive
comparative studies on how different agent collaboration strategies whether
competitive, cooperative, or hierarchical affect the quality of knowledge
extraction.

Additionally, there is limited work on incremental KG update mechanisms designed
to preserve semantic consistency over time. Finally, existing automatically
constructed KGs suffer from insufficient integration of temporal reasoning, which
is crucial for dynamic industrial environments where events are temporally
correlated.

% § 1.3  Five primary thesis objectives: architecture, conflict resolution, DT integration, graph build, evaluation.
\subsection{Objectives}
To address these limitations, this thesis outlines several primary objectives:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Architecture Design:} Design a specialised multi-agent architecture
          for automated construction of KGs from heterogeneous technical documentation.
    \item \textbf{Conflict Resolution:} Implement robust conflict resolution mechanisms
          based on majority voting with configurable confidence thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Digital Twin Integration:} Map the extracted knowledge onto domain
          ontologies and integrate it seamlessly with a Digital Twin prototype.
    \item \textbf{Graph Implementation:} Build and populate a KG in Neo4j tailored for
          a specific industrial domain. This will be evaluated using the iMAKS
          (industrial Multi-Agent Knowledge extraction Synthetic) dataset, which
          simulates five days of operations at a food packaging facility.
    \item \textbf{System Evaluation:} Evaluate the proposed system using both
          quantitative and qualitative metrics, comparing its performance against
          traditional centralised NLP pipeline approaches.
\end{itemize}

\section{Unstructured Data Parsing Strategy}
\label{sec:unstructured_parsing}

% § 2.1  Role of Agent 1B; overview of the three-stage extraction pipeline.
\subsection{Overview of Agent 1B}
Within the multi-agent architecture, the ingestion of unstructured documentation
is handled by Agent~1B. Industrial environments rely heavily on SOPs, which are
often distributed as unstructured or semi-structured PDF files. Converting these
documents into a format suitable for Large Language Models (LLMs) presents a
significant challenge: standard text extraction tools typically destroy layout
context such as table structures and hierarchical headings rendering the data
semantically incoherent for downstream extraction agents.

To address this, Agent~1B implements a robust, multi-stage extraction pipeline
consisting of vision-aware Markdown conversion, deterministic artifact sanitisation,
and context-aware chunking.

% § 2.2  IBM docling bounding-box extraction for PDFs; raw read for .txt files.
\subsection{Vision-Aware Markdown Extraction}
Traditional left-to-right PDF parsers fail when processing complex industrial
documents featuring multi-column layouts and dense data tables. To preserve spatial
and semantic context, Agent~1B utilises IBM's \texttt{docling} library, applying a
vision-language model approach to document conversion.

For PDF inputs, the agent processes the document visually to identify bounding boxes
around text, tables, and headers, exporting the reconstructed layout natively into
Markdown. For standard text files (\texttt{.txt}), the agent bypasses vision
extraction and reads the raw text directly. This Markdown representation ensures
that table boundaries and hierarchical sections are explicitly encoded as text
characters, which is critical for the subsequent entity and relation extraction
phases.

% § 2.3  _normalise protocol: ligature fixes, HTML decoding, escaped-character scrubbing.
\subsection{Normalisation and Hallucination Scrubbing}
While vision-based extraction preserves layout, it introduces specific classes of
OCR artifacts and vision-model hallucinations. Agent~1B executes a strict
normalisation protocol (\texttt{\_normalise}) to sanitise the raw output before it
reaches the LLM context window.

% § 2.3.1  Deterministic ligature-to-symbol map (e.g. "fi" → →, "fl" → ↓).
\subsubsection{Ligature and Encoding Correction}
Industrial SOPs often contain specific mathematical or directional symbols that can
be corrupted by font encoding during PDF generation. Agent~1B explicitly corrects
known broken ligatures. For instance, the sequence ``fi'' is deterministically mapped
back to a right-arrow ($\rightarrow$), and ``fl'' is mapped to a down-arrow
($\downarrow$). Additionally, HTML entities are decoded, and escaped formatting
characters (e.g., \texttt{\\\_}) are sanitised to prevent Markdown injection errors
in downstream prompts.

% § 2.3.2  Regex-based _deduplicate_table_cells: collapses repeated cell content from overlapping bounding boxes.
\subsubsection{Table Cell Deduplication}
A common hallucination in layout-aware extraction models occurs when the model
generates overlapping bounding boxes over dense table cells, causing the underlying
text to be repeated sequentially (e.g., ``Inspect and lubricate HIGH Inspect and
lubricate HIGH'').

To resolve this without invoking an expensive secondary LLM call, Agent~1B deploys
a regex-based deduplication algorithm (\texttt{\_deduplicate\_table\_cells}). The
algorithm scans the document specifically for Markdown table rows (identified by
pipe \texttt{|} delimiters). Within each cell, it utilises a capture group to
identify any string of three or more characters that repeats itself sequentially,
collapsing the hallucination back into a single instance while repadding the cell
for clean Markdown formatting.

% § 2.4  _split_into_chunks: double-newline splits, max 2,000 chars, min 200 chars (merged backward).
\subsection{Context-Aware Chunking Strategy}
LLMs possess finite context windows, and supplying an entire technical document
simultaneously degrades the performance of Named Entity Recognition tasks.
Conversely, splitting text arbitrarily mid-sentence destroys semantic meaning.

Agent~1B implements a context-aware chunking algorithm (\texttt{\_split\_into\_chunks})
that leverages the structural advantages of the extracted Markdown. The text is split
strictly at double newlines (\texttt{\textbackslash n\textbackslash n}), which
reliably represent paragraph or section breaks in Markdown.

The algorithm iterates through these natural paragraphs, merging them into an active
buffer until the buffer size exceeds a predefined maximum threshold of 2\,000
characters. To prevent the creation of uninformative microscopic chunks at the end
of a document, the algorithm enforces a minimum threshold of 200 characters; if the
final chunk falls below this limit, it is merged backward into the preceding chunk.
The fully sanitised and chunked text is then reassembled and saved as a uniform
\texttt{.txt} file, ready for the next step.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Quantifying the quality of automatically extracted operational rules requires a
metric that is sensitive to semantic correctness while remaining robust to the
arbitrary nature of manually assigned identifiers. This section presents a
two-tier evaluation framework, implemented in \texttt{step3\_evaluation\_rules.py},
that measures extraction performance from complementary perspectives.

% § 3.1  F1_strict (exact ruleId match) and F1_content (primary, content-first), definitions and motivation.
\subsection{Two Metrics: Strict Identifier Match and Content-First Similarity}
\label{sec:metrics}

\textbf{F1\textsubscript{strict}} (baseline): a conventional exact-match metric
based on rule identifiers. After normalising \texttt{ruleId} strings (lowercasing
and removing all non-alphanumeric characters), true positives are counted as the
intersection of ground-truth and predicted identifier sets. Precision, recall,
and F1 are computed in the standard way. This metric has an inherent ceiling:
because many ground-truth identifiers are annotator conventions that never appear
verbatim in the source SOP texts, the maximum achievable F1\textsubscript{strict}
is limited regardless of extraction quality.

\textbf{F1\textsubscript{content}} (primary): a content-first metric that ignores
identifiers entirely and instead evaluates how well the predicted rule content
matches the ground-truth rule content. It uses a class-weighted field-level
agreement score, aggregated via the Hungarian optimal assignment algorithm, and
then counts true positive matches above a threshold of $0.6$ to compute precision,
recall, and F1. This metric directly answers the question: \emph{Does the extracted
rule convey the same operational knowledge as the ground-truth rule?}

% § 3.2  12 scored fields grouped by type; per-class raw weight table (FIELD_WEIGHTS in code).
\subsection{Field Definitions and Class-Specific Weights}
\label{sec:field_weights}

The iMAKS rule schema contains twelve scored content fields, grouped into three
categories:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Text fields:} \texttt{condition}, \texttt{action}
\item \textbf{Numeric fields:} \texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo},
      \texttt{critLo}
\item \textbf{Categorical fields:} \texttt{class}, \texttt{station}, \texttt{sensor},
      \texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity}, \texttt{unit}
\end{itemize}
The field \texttt{ruleId} is not included in the content metric; its contribution
to operational knowledge is already captured by the numeric and categorical fields,
and it represents a naming convention rather than operational content.

Different rule classes place emphasis on different fields. For example, a
\texttt{ThresholdRule} hinges on its numeric boundaries, while an
\texttt{OperationalRule} relies primarily on its textual \texttt{condition} and
\texttt{action}. To capture this, the framework defines a static set of \emph{raw}
weights $w_f(c)$ for each field $f$ and class $c$, shown in
Table~\ref{tab:class_weights}. When computing content agreement, only the fields
that are non-empty in the ground-truth rule contribute; their weights are summed to
form the denominator, so weights are never pre-normalised. Fields absent from a
given class are omitted entirely from both numerator and denominator.

A fallback weight vector (class=1.0, station=1.5, sensor=1.5, severity=1.0,
condition=2.0, action=1.0) is used if a rule's class is missing or unrecognised.

% § 3.3  Per-field scoring: categorical (exact), numeric (relative tolerance), text (SBERT cosine).
\subsection{Field-Level Similarity Functions}
\label{sec:similarity_funcs}

Each field contributes a similarity score $s_f$ between a ground-truth value and
an extracted value:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Categorical fields} (exact match): $s_f = 1$ if the strings are
      identical after stripping and lowercasing, else $0$.
\item \textbf{Numeric fields} (relative tolerance):
      $s_f = \max(0,\; 1 - |a - b| / \max(|a|, |b|, \varepsilon))$,
      where $a$ is the ground-truth value and $b$ the extracted value. This yields
      1 when the numbers are equal and decays toward 0 as the relative difference
      increases.
\item \textbf{Text fields} (semantic similarity): $s_f$ is the cosine similarity
      between SBERT embeddings of the two text strings. The framework uses the
      \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model from the Sentence-Transformers library, with
      embeddings cached to avoid redundant computation.
\end{itemize}

% § 3.4  CA formula: weighted sum of active field scores divided by sum of active weights (Eq. 1).
\subsection{Content Agreement Score}
\label{sec:content_agreement}

For a pair of ground-truth ($r_{\text{gt}}$) and extracted ($r_{\text{ext}}$) rules,
the content agreement $CA$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:content_agreement}
CA(r_{\text{gt}}, r_{\text{ext}}) =
\frac{
  \displaystyle\sum_{f \in F_{\text{gt}}} w_f(c) \cdot s_f(r_{\text{gt}}, r_{\text{ext}})
}{
  \displaystyle\sum_{f \in F_{\text{gt}}} w_f(c)
}
\end{equation}

where $F_{\text{gt}}$ is the set of scored fields that are non-empty in the
ground-truth rule, $c$ is the ground-truth class, and $w_f(c)$ are taken from
Table~\ref{tab:class_weights} (or the fallback vector). This formulation ensures
that every ground-truth rule is evaluated solely on the information it actually
contains, and partial matches are rewarded proportionally.

% § 3.5  linear_sum_assignment on the n_gt × n_pred CA matrix; TP threshold = 0.6; P/R/F1 formulas.
\subsection{Hungarian Assignment and Content F1}
\label{sec:hungarian}

To compute F1\textsubscript{content}, the $n_{\text{gt}} \times n_{\text{pred}}$
matrix of content agreement scores is built, and the Hungarian algorithm (linear
sum assignment) finds the one-to-one pairing that maximises the total sum. A matched
pair is counted as a true positive if and only if its $CA$ exceeds a threshold of
$0.6$. Let $TP$ be the number of such qualifying pairs. Then:

$$P_{\text{content}} = \frac{TP}{n_{\text{pred}}}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
R_{\text{content}} = \frac{TP}{n_{\text{gt}}}$$

$$F1_{\text{content}} = \frac{2\, P_{\text{content}}\, R_{\text{content}}}
                             {P_{\text{content}} + R_{\text{content}}}$$

This formulation directly rewards pairs that capture the essential operational
semantics while penalising spurious extractions; it is more robust than a soft sum
of scores because it avoids inflating precision when many low-quality rules are
produced.

% § 3.6  SBERT singleton caching, CUDA/MPS auto-detection, empty-list edge cases.
\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:eval_impl}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SBERT caching:} The Sentence-Transformer model is loaded once as a
      singleton and embeddings are cached in a dictionary to avoid re-encoding
      repeated strings.
\item \textbf{Hardware acceleration:} The model automatically detects and utilises
      CUDA or MPS when available.
\item \textbf{Edge cases:} When either the ground truth or prediction list is empty,
      F1\textsubscript{content} is defined as 0.0.
\end{itemize}

% § 3.7  Why F1_content is the primary metric; why the normalizer's few-shot advantage does NOT affect it.
\subsection{Metric Validity and the Strict-F1 Caveat}
\label{sec:metric_validity}

The two-tier evaluation framework is designed so that F1\textsubscript{content}
is the primary, operationally meaningful metric and F1\textsubscript{strict} is
a secondary, name-matching baseline.
This distinction matters because the iMAKS benchmark assigns ground-truth
\texttt{ruleId} values using annotator conventions (e.g., \texttt{RULE-ST01-01}
for the first rule at station~1) that are not derivable from the SOP source text
alone; many SOPs do not label rules at all, and those that do use different
naming schemes.
An extraction system is therefore penalised on F1\textsubscript{strict} for
producing a semantically correct rule under a plausible but non-matching
identifier, which is not a meaningful penalty for operational quality.

A further caveat applies specifically to the multi-agent pipeline evaluated in
this thesis.
The \texttt{IdentifierCanonicalizer} node (Section~\ref{sec:normalizer}) is
shown up to 20 ground-truth \texttt{ruleId} examples as few-shot demonstrations
before it assigns identifiers to the extracted rules.
This gives the normalizer knowledge of the annotation convention used in the test
set, which can modestly increase the probability that its assigned identifiers
match the ground-truth strings.

\textbf{Critically, this does not affect F1\textsubscript{content}.}
The \texttt{content\_agreement} function (Equation~\ref{eq:content_agreement})
iterates over the scored fields defined in Table~\ref{tab:class_weights}:
\texttt{class}, \texttt{station}, \texttt{sensor}, \texttt{sensorType},
\texttt{severity}, \texttt{unit}, \texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi},
\texttt{warnLo}, \texttt{critLo}, \texttt{condition}, and \texttt{action}.
The \texttt{ruleId} field is not in this set and contributes nothing to the
content score.
The Hungarian matching that determines true positives, false positives, and
false negatives is based entirely on these twelve content fields.

All performance claims, ablation comparisons, and cross-system comparisons in
this thesis use F1\textsubscript{content} as the decisive criterion.
F1\textsubscript{strict} values are included in the appendix tables for
completeness and to allow comparison with other works that report identifier
match, but they should be interpreted with the above caveat: they reflect both
extraction quality \emph{and} naming convention alignment, and the multi-agent
pipeline has an advantage in naming convention alignment that single-agent
baselines do not.

\section{Rule Extraction Grid Search Methodology}
\label{sec:grid_search}

Before introducing the collaborative multi-agent architecture, it is necessary to
establish a robust baseline for extracting operational rules from technical
documentation. To this end, a comprehensive grid search methodology was developed
to evaluate the performance of various LLMs and prompting paradigms on industrial
SOPs, using the F1\textsubscript{content} metric defined in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

% § 4.1  Models (4B–20B), 8,000-char input cap, 16,384-token context, T=0, seed=42, exponential backoff.
\subsection{System Architecture and Experimental Parameters}
The grid search is orchestrated through a highly resilient, single-agent execution
pipeline. The system evaluates an array of models spanning parameter sizes from 4\,B
to 20\,B, specifically \texttt{ministral-3:14b}, \texttt{gemma3:12b},
\texttt{ministral-3:8b}, \texttt{qwen/qwen3-4b-2507}, and \texttt{gpt-oss:20b}.
To accommodate the dense technical nature of the documentation, the text input from
each SOP document is capped at 8\,000 characters.

The LLM connection parameters are configured to maximise stability during
long-running batch operations. The context window is explicitly set to 16\,384
tokens to ensure sufficient capacity for both the input text and the structured JSON
output. A strict timeout of 600 seconds (10 minutes) is enforced per API call, and
network resilience is handled via an exponential backoff mechanism that allows up to
three retries, starting with a 15-second base delay that doubles upon each failure.

For the majority of evaluated paradigms, the model temperature is set to $0$ and a
fixed random seed (default~42) is supplied to enforce deterministic, reproducible
outputs. The exception is the self-consistency paradigm, which utilises a
temperature of $0.3$ across three independent runs to generate a diverse set of
reasoning paths.

A further implementation detail concerns the three Mistral-family models
(\texttt{ministral-3:14b}, \texttt{ministral-3:8b}): their chat templates do not
support a dedicated system role. For these models, the system message is
automatically merged into the leading user turn at call time, preserving full
prompt semantics without requiring paradigm-specific adjustments.

% § 4.2  Four complexity levels: L0 direct (naive, few-shot, graph-informed), L1 CoT,
%         L2 iterative/ensemble (self-consistency, reflexion, reflexion-guided), L3 ReAct (react_abox).
\subsection{Prompting Paradigms and Complexity Levels}
To systematically assess how LLMs interpret industrial logic, the grid search
categorises prompting strategies into four distinct complexity levels:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Level~0 (Direct Extraction):} Includes the \texttt{naive} approach,
          which enforces a strict JSON schema without additional constraints.
          The \texttt{few\_shot\_static} paradigm enhances this by providing two
          concrete examples one \texttt{OperationalRule} and one
          \texttt{ThresholdRule} illustrating the required output format and numeric
          field mapping. Finally, the \texttt{graph\_informed} strategy introduces
          mandatory constraints, forcing the LLM to map its outputs to specific sensor
          naming conventions (e.g., \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_TMP}).
    \item \textbf{Level~1 (Reasoning Before Action):} Introduces intermediate
          cognitive steps before JSON generation. The \texttt{cot\_basic} paradigm
          requires standard step-by-step reasoning, while \texttt{cot\_structured}
          enforces a strict five-step analytical process: (1)~enumerate all stations
          and zones, (2)~identify the sensors and measurement types per station,
          (3)~extract threshold conditions with their numeric values,
          (4)~identify causal dependencies between stations, and
          (5)~identify access-control, occupancy, and maintenance rules.
          The \texttt{pre\_act} paradigm requires the model to summarise the
          document's structure, the categories of rules present, and any
          cross-section dependencies before producing the extraction.
    \item \textbf{Level~2 (Iterative Refinement and Ensemble Voting):} Incorporates
          multi-turn logic and ensemble strategies. The \texttt{self\_consistency}
          paradigm runs three independent extractions at temperature $0.3$ and then
          aggregates the results through a score-based voting mechanism
          (Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}). Both \texttt{reflexion} and
          \texttt{reflexion\_guided} use the \texttt{graph\_informed} system prompt
          as their conversational base, inheriting its mandatory sensor-naming
          constraints, whereas \texttt{self\_consistency} uses the simpler
          \texttt{naive} base without those constraints.
          The \texttt{reflexion} strategy utilises a two-turn loop in which the model
          critiques its own initial output for missing rules, imprecise or incorrect
          numeric conditions, wrong sensor and station names, and duplicate or
          poorly-structured entries.
          The \texttt{reflexion\_guided} paradigm extends this to three turns,
          adding a rigid domain-specific checklist in the final turn that covers
          six categories: (1)~completeness across all stations, (2)~cross-station
          dependencies, (3)~temporal anomaly patterns (SPIKE, DRIFT, STUCK,
          OUT\_OF\_RANGE, CORRELATED), (4)~predictive and corrective maintenance
          protocols (MAINT-01 to MAINT-08), (5)~access-control and zone-occupancy
          rules, and (6)~correctness of all numeric threshold values.
    \item \textbf{Level~3 (Tool Use / ReAct):} The \texttt{react\_abox} paradigm
          allows the model to actively verify sensor names against a localised
          Knowledge Graph (ABox). The LLM can halt its generation to emit a
          \texttt{VERIFY: <sensor\_name>} command. The system evaluates this request
          using fuzzy token-overlap matching and returns either a \texttt{SENSOR\_OK}
          confirmation or a \texttt{SENSOR\_NOT\_FOUND} warning with up to three
          syntactically similar suggestions. The loop is bounded to a maximum of
          five turns; if this limit is reached without a final JSON response, the
          last assistant message is parsed directly.
\end{itemize}

% § 4.3  self_consistency: 3 runs at T=0.3, pairwise agreement matrix, score_based voting, threshold 0.55.
\subsection{Ensemble Voting and Content Agreement}
\label{sec:ensemble}
The \texttt{self\_consistency} paradigm (Level~2) relies on an ensemble voting
mechanism to aggregate the results of three independent LLM calls. To determine
whether two independently generated rules describe the same operational constraint,
a \texttt{\_content\_agreement} algorithm calculates a weighted similarity score
(from 0.0 to 1.0).

Categorical fields (\texttt{class}, \texttt{station}, \texttt{sensor},
\texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity}, \texttt{unit}) are evaluated using exact
string matching after normalisation, while text fields (conditions and actions) are
evaluated using a Jaccard token-overlap approximation. Numeric fields within
\texttt{ThresholdRule} instances are subjected to a tolerance-based similarity
calculation to prevent minor rounding differences from invalidating a match. Field
weights are non-uniform: location fields (\texttt{station}, \texttt{sensor}) carry
weight~2.0, primary numeric bounds (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{critLo}) carry
weight~3.0, secondary bounds (\texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo}) carry weight~1.5,
and text fields carry weight~0.5 for \texttt{ThresholdRule} entries (where numerics
are the primary signal) or~2.0 for all other rule classes.

The system implements a \texttt{score\_based} voting strategy by default. Rather
than requiring rules to share an identical \texttt{ruleId}, this strategy builds a
pairwise agreement matrix across all rules generated in the ensemble. If the
similarity score exceeds the predefined threshold of $0.55$, the rules are
clustered via greedy merging in descending agreement order. A rule is only retained
for the final output if its cluster spans at least 2 out of 3 independent runs.
For retained \texttt{ThresholdRule} clusters, a secondary majority vote is applied
across the numeric fields (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{critLo}, \texttt{warnHi},
\texttt{warnLo}, \texttt{unit}) to resolve minor threshold discrepancies and
produce a single stabilised value.

% § 4.4  JSON registry (partial → completed), heartbeat thread, 4-strategy cascading JSON parser.
\subsection{System Resilience and Output Parsing}
Given the tendency of LLMs to deviate from strict schemas, especially under high
token loads, the system includes extensive fallback mechanisms. Experiment state is
preserved via a JSON registry that marks runs as \texttt{partial} upon initiation
and \texttt{completed} only upon successful disk write, allowing interrupted grid
searches to resume seamlessly. Furthermore, a dedicated heartbeat thread
continuously logs timestamps to prevent silent hangs during prolonged API timeouts.

To extract valid data from malformed LLM responses, a cascading four-strategy JSON
parser is deployed. If standard parsing fails, the system attempts to repair
truncated JSON strings by balancing unclosed brackets. If the output contains
Markdown artifacts, regex extraction is used to isolate the data blocks. As a final
fallback, the parser iterates through the corrupted output to salvage any
individually well-formed rule objects.

\section{Multi-Agent Extraction Pipeline Architecture}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Building on the grid-search findings, a staged extraction pipeline was designed
that combines specialised LLM agents under a graph-based orchestration framework.
The system treats rule extraction as a directed acyclic computation where each
stage contributes a specific quality-improving transformation. Implemented with
\texttt{LangGraph}, the pipeline dispatches LLM calls through local \texttt{Ollama}
or \texttt{LM Studio} endpoints, maintaining full data sovereignty with no cloud
dependency.

The motivation for a multi-agent architecture rather than a single monolithic LLM
prompt stems directly from the grid-search analysis. Three key observations drove the
design.  First, the iMAKS SOP corpus is structurally heterogeneous: some files are
pure narrative prose, others are dense sensor-threshold tables, and others encode
occupancy or access-control matrices. A single prompt optimised for prose extraction
systematically under-performs on tabular layouts, and vice versa.  Second, no single
model achieved both high precision \emph{and} high recall across all document types:
\texttt{gemma3:12b} showed the strongest precision on narrative text, whereas
\texttt{ministral-3:8b} offered the best precision-recall balance on structured
table rows. Third, error modes compound across stages, hallucinated rules that
pass extraction unchecked cannot easily be corrected downstream without risking valid
rules. Separating responsibilities into discrete, verifiable stages allows each
failure mode to be addressed by a targeted mechanism (deterministic merger,
LLM-based validator, judge with retry) rather than relying on a single prompt to
handle all concerns simultaneously.

The resulting pipeline is a directed acyclic graph of seven specialised nodes
(plus one conditional re-entry loop) that sequentially classify, extract, deduplicate,
validate, and canonicalise rules. Every LLM-facing node uses a purpose-built
system prompt and the model best suited to its task. Deterministic steps are
preferred wherever the logic can be expressed as an algorithm, reserving LLM
reasoning for tasks that genuinely require semantic understanding.

\subsection{Shared State Management in LangGraph}
\label{sec:langgraph_state}

In a LangGraph computation graph, all nodes share a single mutable state object
that is passed through the graph as execution progresses.  Each node receives the
current state as input and returns a \emph{partial} update, a dictionary containing
only the fields it modified, which LangGraph merges back into the global state.
This design means that individual nodes need not be aware of the full state
structure: a node only declares the fields it reads and the fields it writes.

The pipeline's shared state is formalised as \texttt{PipelineState}, a Python
\texttt{TypedDict}.  Declaring the state as a \texttt{TypedDict} rather than a
plain dictionary gives Python's type checker visibility into which keys are
expected at each stage and provides a single authoritative schema that all node
implementations can reference.

The most architecturally significant aspect of the state is the treatment of
\texttt{extracted\_rules}.  Because the three extractor nodes run in parallel each processing a different subset of documents, their outputs would
ordinarily overwrite one another if stored in a plain list field.  To prevent
this, the field is declared using Python's \texttt{Annotated} type hint with
\texttt{operator.add} as the merge reducer:

\begin{verbatim}
extracted_rules: Annotated[list[dict], operator.add]
\end{verbatim}

This declaration instructs LangGraph to \emph{concatenate} the partial updates
returned by parallel nodes rather than replacing the accumulated list.  When
Extractor A finishes and returns its rules, and then Extractor B finishes and
returns its rules, LangGraph appends B's list to A's list rather than replacing
it, resulting in a single combined candidate set that the Merger node receives.
Without this annotation, only the last extractor to complete would contribute
rules to the merged set, a subtle race condition that would silently discard
results without raising any error.

The complete set of state fields and their roles is:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{sop\_texts}   a dictionary mapping each source SOP filename to
          its text content, truncated to \texttt{SOP\_TEXT\_LIMIT} characters.
          This is the primary input to the Coordinator and all three extractors.
    \item \texttt{doc\_types}   a dictionary mapping each SOP filename to its
          document type (\textit{narrative}, \textit{tabular}, \textit{matrix}, or
          \textit{mixed}) as assigned by the Coordinator.  This mapping drives the
          conditional fan-out that routes each document to its appropriate extractor.
    \item \texttt{extracted\_rules}   the accumulating list of all rule candidates
          produced by the extractor nodes, using the sum-reducer described above.
          A single rule is represented as a flat Python dictionary whose keys match
          the \texttt{RULE\_FIELDS} schema (class, station, condition, action, threshold
          fields, etc.).
    \item \texttt{merged\_rules}   the deduplicated rule set produced by the
          Consensus Merger after applying its four deterministic resolution branches to
          \texttt{extracted\_rules}.
    \item \texttt{validated\_rules}   the rule set produced by the Validator after
          its two-pass cleaning (deterministic filter plus additive LLM gap-fill) has
          been applied to \texttt{merged\_rules}.
    \item \texttt{normalized\_rules}   the final rule set produced by the
          Identifier Canonicalizer after assigning canonical \texttt{ruleId} values
          to \texttt{validated\_rules}.  This is the field written to the output CSV.
    \item \texttt{retry\_files}   a dictionary mapping filenames to their document
          types for files that the Judge has identified as exhibiting a complete
          structural failure.  This field is populated only when a retry cycle is
          activated; it tells the \texttt{PreRetry} node which files to dispatch for
          re-extraction and with which extractor.
    \item \texttt{retry\_count}   an integer counter incremented by the
          \texttt{PreRetry} node each time a full re-extraction cycle is initiated.
          The routing function inspects this counter to enforce the one-cycle cap.
    \item \texttt{ablation}   an optional string tag (e.g., \texttt{"no\_cm"},
          \texttt{"no\_validator"}, \texttt{"no\_judge"}) passed at pipeline
          construction time by the ablation harness.  When set, \texttt{build\_graph()}
          omits or bypasses the corresponding node so that the graph topology matches
          the intended ablation variant.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Assignments and Node Configuration}
\label{sec:agent_config}

Each node in the graph is assigned a specific model and a dedicated system prompt.
Model assignments are \emph{not} arbitrary: every assignment was derived from the
grid-search evaluation on the iMAKS dataset (Section~\ref{sec:gridsearch}) and
reflects the performance profile best suited to each node's task.  The assignments
are centralised in a \texttt{DEFAULTS} dictionary in the implementation so that
a single change point is available for experiment reconfiguration; each key is also
exposed as a command-line argument, allowing per-node model overrides without
modifying the source code.

The core principle guiding assignments is \emph{task-role matching}: tasks that
require careful semantic reasoning under potential ambiguity are assigned larger,
higher-precision models, while tasks that amount to structural classification or
pattern-matching over a fixed schema are assigned smaller, faster models.
Assigning a heavyweight model everywhere would increase latency unnecessarily;
assigning a lightweight model to a semantically demanding node would risk precision
drops that propagate downstream.

\subsection{Stage Descriptions}
\label{sec:stages}

This subsection walks through each pipeline node in execution order, explaining
what it receives as input, what computation it performs, what it returns as output,
and why it was designed the way it was.  The goal is to provide enough detail that
each node's behaviour can be understood without reading the implementation.

% § 5.4.1  coordinator_node: LLM reads first 8,000 chars → JSON {doc_type, structural_reasoning};
%           classifies as narrative / tabular / matrix / mixed.
\subsubsection{Document Type Classifier}
\label{sec:classifier}

\paragraph{What it receives.}
The Coordinator node receives the \texttt{sop\_texts} dictionary containing every
SOP filename mapped to its (possibly truncated) text.  It processes all files in
a single invocation via a Python loop, one LLM call per SOP file.

\paragraph{What it does.}
For each SOP, the \texttt{qwen/qwen3-4b-2507} model is shown the first
8\,000 characters and asked to produce a JSON object with two keys:
\texttt{doc\_type} and \texttt{structural\_reasoning}.  The system prompt
supplies precise semantic definitions for each of the four categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{tabular}}, the dominant layout is a sensor threshold
          table.  The clearest indicator is the presence of column headers such as
          \texttt{CRIT\_LO}, \texttt{WARN\_LO}, \texttt{CRIT\_HI}, \texttt{WARN\_HI}
          alongside rows of numeric values.  These files map cleanly to the
          \texttt{ThresholdRule} schema.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{matrix}}, the document presents an access-control or
          occupancy grid, where rows represent zones or roles and columns represent
          permissions, capacity limits, or acknowledgment deadlines.  These files map
          primarily to the \texttt{AccessRule} schema.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{mixed}}, the document interleaves narrative prose
          (describing operational procedures) with structured maintenance tables or
          sensor drift patterns.  It requires both a narrative extractor (for
          operational and maintenance rules) and awareness of embedded tables.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{narrative}}, the default for all remaining documents:
          plain procedural or descriptive text without structured tables or grids.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What it returns.}
The node writes the completed \texttt{doc\_types} dictionary into the pipeline
state.  Each key is a filename and each value is one of the four category strings.
This mapping is subsequently used by the conditional fan-out function
(\texttt{dispatch\_to\_extractors}) to route each SOP to exactly one of the three
specialised extractor nodes.  The same mapping is also used later by the Judge to
select the appropriate completeness checklist for each document type.

\paragraph{Design rationale.}
Performing classification as a dedicated first stage, rather than allowing each
extractor to handle all documents, prevents the precision cost of sending a
prose-oriented extraction prompt to a table-structured document and vice versa.
The Coordinator's output is deterministic given fixed temperature and seed, so the
routing decisions are reproducible across runs.

% § 5.4.2  Three parallel extractors: A (gemma3:12b, narrative/mixed, all classes + PCM),
%           B (ministral-3:8b, tabular, ThresholdRule only), C (gemma3:12b, matrix, AccessRule only).
\subsubsection{Specialised Extractors}
\label{sec:miners}

\paragraph{What they receive.}
After the Coordinator classifies every SOP, the \texttt{dispatch\_to\_extractors}
function inspects the \texttt{doc\_types} dictionary and emits one
LangGraph \texttt{Send} object per file, directing each file to its designated
extractor node.  A \texttt{Send} object carries the filename, the SOP text, the
document type, and the relevant model name as a small sub-state.  The three
extractor nodes therefore operate over \emph{disjoint subsets} of the SOP corpus:
Extractor A sees only \texttt{narrative} and \texttt{mixed} files, Extractor B sees
only \texttt{tabular} files, and Extractor C sees only \texttt{matrix} files.
Parallelism arises naturally because LangGraph schedules all three nodes
concurrently once their inputs are ready; they each append their results to the
shared \texttt{extracted\_rules} list via the sum reducer described in
Section~\ref{sec:langgraph_state}.

\paragraph{The Physical Constraint Mapping (PCM) discipline.}
All three extractors are governed by the same grounding discipline, referred to as
the \emph{Physical Constraint Mapping} (PCM) principles.  PCM is a set of five
prompt-level instructions that collectively prevent the most common hallucination
patterns observed in the grid-search baseline runs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PCM-1 (verbatim numeric copying)}, threshold values must be
          copied character-for-character from the source text.  The model is
          explicitly prohibited from rounding, normalising, or converting units.
    \item \textbf{PCM-2 (no unit invention)}, the \texttt{unit} field must be
          taken from the source; if no unit is stated, the field is left empty.
          The model must not infer a unit from physical context (e.g., assuming
          \texttt{°C} because the field is labelled ``temperature'').
    \item \textbf{PCM-3 (no cross-row inference)}, threshold values from one table
          row must not be attributed to a different sensor row.  Each extracted rule
          must correspond to exactly one row or one labelled rule in the source.
    \item \textbf{PCM-4 (no cross-sentence inference)}, for narrative documents,
          a rule must not combine a condition from one sentence with an action from a
          different, unrelated sentence unless the source text explicitly links them.
    \item \textbf{PCM-5 (no field fabrication)}, if a required field (e.g.,
          \texttt{station} or \texttt{sensorType}) cannot be populated from the source
          text, the field is set to an empty string rather than guessed.
\end{itemize}

These five constraints were not part of the grid-search baseline prompts; they were
added in response to hallucination patterns observed during grid-search analysis and
represent one of the principal prompt engineering contributions of the pipeline.

\paragraph{Extractor A, narrative and mixed documents.}
Extractor A (\texttt{gemma3:12b}) handles \texttt{narrative} and \texttt{mixed}
SOPs.  Its system prompt covers all four rule classes
(\texttt{OperationalRule}, \texttt{ThresholdRule}, \texttt{MaintenanceRule},
\texttt{AccessRule}) because prose-based SOPs can contain rules of any type.
The prompt includes a \emph{multi-severity splitting} instruction: if a single
sensor has both a WARNING tier and a CRITICAL tier of the same constraint
(e.g., ``warn QC at 28\,°C; initiate shutdown at 32\,°C''), the model must
produce \emph{two separate rules}, one with \texttt{severity = WARNING} and one
with \texttt{severity = CRITICAL}, rather than collapsing them into a single rule.
This is necessary because downstream evaluation scores each rule independently,
and collapsing severity tiers would cause both to be counted as false negatives.
The prompt also includes several few-shot examples drawn from the iMAKS corpus
and instructs the model to produce an optional \texttt{"reasoning"} key (alongside
the \texttt{"rules"} list), which provides a chain-of-thought trace for
debugging without affecting the extracted rules themselves.

\paragraph{Extractor B, tabular documents.}
Extractor B (\texttt{ministral-3:8b}) handles \texttt{tabular} documents.
Its extraction task is narrower and more mechanical than Extractor A's: the
source document is a sensor-threshold table whose columns map directly to the
four threshold fields (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo},
\texttt{critLo}).  The system prompt therefore restricts the extractor to the
\texttt{ThresholdRule} class only, provides explicit column-to-field mapping
instructions, and applies PCM-1 through PCM-3 to ensure that cell values are
copied verbatim and no unit suffixes are appended to the numeric threshold fields.
The prompt also instructs the model to preserve partial thresholds: if only
critical limits are defined for a sensor (no warning band), the model produces a
\texttt{ThresholdRule} with only \texttt{critHi}/\texttt{critLo} populated and
the warning fields left empty, rather than omitting the rule entirely.

\paragraph{Extractor C, matrix documents.}
Extractor C (\texttt{gemma3:12b}) handles \texttt{matrix} documents.  It focuses
exclusively on \texttt{AccessRule} entries, which encode occupancy limits
(maximum number of personnel in a zone), acknowledgment deadlines (response time
required after an alarm), and explicitly labelled access or permission rules.
The system prompt instructs the model to enumerate one rule per non-trivial cell
value in the matrix, applying PCM to keep cell values verbatim.  The instruction
to avoid cross-cell inference (PCM-3 applied to matrix context) is particularly
important here: matrix layouts can confuse models into pairing a row label with
the wrong column value when rows and columns both carry semantically similar names.

\paragraph{Retry prompt augmentation.}
When the Judge triggers a re-extraction cycle (described in
Section~\ref{sec:judge}), the \texttt{PreRetry} node sets an \texttt{is\_retry}
flag in the per-file sub-state.  Each extractor checks this flag and, when it is
set, appends an exhaustiveness instruction to its prompt: the model is told
explicitly that a previous extraction pass missed rules and is asked to be more
thorough, paying special attention to any rule that is explicitly named or
labelled in the source text.  The semantic fields of the re-extracted rules are
otherwise produced by the same prompt and model as the original pass.

\subsubsection{Consensus Merger}
\label{sec:merger}

\paragraph{What it receives.}
The \texttt{ConsensusMerger} node receives the complete \texttt{extracted\_rules}
list, which at this point is the concatenated output of all extractor nodes.
Because Extractors A, B, and C may each have processed overlapping document
types in a retry scenario, and because a single SOP can exhibit both narrative and
tabular sections (document type \texttt{mixed}), the same underlying rule may appear
as multiple slightly-different candidate dictionaries in this list.  The Merger's
responsibility is to produce a single coherent, deduplicated rule set without
discarding any rule that represents a genuinely distinct constraint.

\paragraph{Grouping by physical identity key.}
The Merger first groups all candidates by a four-component identity key:

\[
  k = (\texttt{class},\ \texttt{station},\ \texttt{sensorType},\ \texttt{condition\_prefix})
\]

The \texttt{condition\_prefix} component is included only when both
\texttt{station} and \texttt{sensorType} are empty strings (a common situation
for \texttt{AccessRule} and \texttt{MaintenanceRule} entries, which often lack a
physical sensor location).  Without this guard, two completely different
\texttt{AccessRule} entries, for example, one about zone capacity and one about
acknowledgment deadlines, could fall into the same key group and be incorrectly
collapsed together.  The prefix (the first 40 characters of the
\texttt{condition} field, lowercased) is used as a coarse proxy for rule identity
in these cases.

\paragraph{Four deterministic resolution branches.}
Within each group, four resolution branches execute in sequence.  A rule leaves
the sequence as soon as it is handled by a branch; later branches only see
candidates that the earlier branches did not resolve.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Branch~A (Singleton pass-through.)}
          If only one candidate exists in the group, it is passed through
          unchanged.  No comparison or merging logic is needed.  This is the most
          common case: many rules have a unique physical identity key and therefore
          appear in a singleton group.

    \item \textbf{Branch~B (Complementary threshold merge.)}
          This branch handles a specific duplication pattern that arises frequently
          in tabular documents: two extractors independently parse the same threshold
          table row but each captures only one half of the threshold band.  Extractor A
          (narrative/mixed) might extract a rule with only the critical limits
          (\texttt{critHi}/\texttt{critLo} populated, warning fields empty), while
          Extractor B (tabular) extracts a rule with only the warning limits
          (\texttt{warnHi}/\texttt{warnLo} populated, critical fields empty).
          Both rules refer to the same physical sensor and the same condition, but
          neither is complete on its own.

          The branch fires when exactly two \texttt{ThresholdRule} candidates exist
          in the group and they form a complementary pair: one carries only critical
          fields and the other carries only warning fields.  In this case, the
          critical-carrying rule is taken as the base and the warning values from
          the other candidate are merged into it.  The merged rule's severity is set
          to \texttt{CRITICAL} (because it now contains both tiers, and the most
          severe tier determines the rule's overall severity label).  Both candidates
          are replaced by this single merged rule and the group is marked resolved.

    \item \textbf{Branch~B2 (Threshold semantic deduplication.)}
          This branch targets a second threshold-specific pattern: two or more
          \texttt{ThresholdRule} candidates share the same physical identity key and
          also share \emph{identical numeric threshold values} (after stripping any
          unit suffix that might have been appended by one extractor but not another).
          This happens when the same threshold table row is processed by both the
          tabular extractor and a retry pass, producing two semantically identical
          rules that differ only in superficial string formatting.

          When such duplicate pairs are detected, the group is collapsed to the
          \emph{most-populated} candidate, the one with the greatest number of
          non-empty fields, which is most likely to be the more complete extraction.
          The other candidates in the group are discarded.

    \item \textbf{Branch~C (Content-agreement collapse.)}
          For any candidates that remain after Branches B and B2, a pairwise content
          agreement score is computed over a six-field normalised signature:
          \texttt{class}, \texttt{station}, \texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity},
          \texttt{condition}, and \texttt{action}.  Each field is lowercased, stripped
          of punctuation and extra whitespace, and the score is the fraction of the
          six fields whose normalised values match.

          If the score between two candidates exceeds the threshold of $0.75$
          (i.e., at least five of the six fields agree), the pair is considered
          near-duplicate and is collapsed to the richer candidate.  However, an
          important guard prevents this collapse when the two candidates have
          \emph{different} \texttt{action} fields after normalisation: differing
          actions on the same sensor signal an intentional severity-tier split
          (e.g., a WARNING tier that logs the event and notifies QC vs.\ a CRITICAL
          tier that initiates an emergency shutdown).  These must be preserved as
          two distinct rules; collapsing them would erase the more urgent instruction.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Handling unresolved conflicts.}
After all four branches, any candidates whose groups still contain multiple distinct
rules are kept as-is in the output, they are \emph{forwarded} to the Validator
and Judge as unresolved duplicates rather than being dropped or merged by an LLM.

This design choice is deliberate.  Early experiments included an LLM adjudication
step at the merger stage, where the model was asked to choose between conflicting
candidates.  The LLM frequently introduced false negatives by collapsing rules that
were genuinely distinct but textually similar, for example, merging two rules for
different physical stations whose condition descriptions happened to overlap.  The
deterministic Merger cannot reliably distinguish such cases without access to the
full source text, which is not available at this stage.  The Validator
(Section~\ref{sec:validator}) and Judge (Section~\ref{sec:judge}) do have access to
the source text and are therefore better positioned to resolve or discard ambiguous
candidates with full grounding context.

%           physics ordering); Pass 2 = per-file LLM gap-filling (additive only, never removes).
\subsubsection{Validator (Pre-Judge Scrubber)}
\label{sec:validator}

\paragraph{What it receives.}
The Validator receives the \texttt{merged\_rules} list (output of the Consensus
Merger), the \texttt{sop\_texts} dictionary (original SOP text for every file),
and the \texttt{doc\_types} dictionary.  It operates \emph{before} the Judge
so that the Judge receives an already-cleaned rule set and can focus on detecting
semantic gaps rather than having to deal with structurally invalid entries.

\paragraph{Pass 1, deterministic post-filter.}
The first pass applies a \emph{deterministic post-filter} (implemented as
\texttt{\_deterministic\_post\_filter}) that removes any rule failing any of the
following structural checks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Invalid class.}  The \texttt{class} field must be one of the
          four accepted values: \texttt{OperationalRule}, \texttt{ThresholdRule},
          \texttt{MaintenanceRule}, \texttt{AccessRule}.  Rules with missing,
          misspelled, or hallucinated class values are discarded.  (Minor
          capitalisation variations are normalised before checking, so
          \texttt{operationalrule} and \texttt{Operational Rule} are both
          accepted after normalisation.)
    \item \textbf{Missing threshold numeric.}  Any rule with \texttt{class =
          ThresholdRule} but no numeric value in any of its four threshold fields
          (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo}, \texttt{critLo})
          is discarded.  Such rules carry no operational information and would be
          false positives in evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Empty condition.}  Rules with an empty or whitespace-only
          \texttt{condition} field are discarded.  A rule without a condition
          cannot be matched against a ground-truth entry and cannot be applied
          operationally.
    \item \textbf{Physically impossible threshold ordering.}  For
          \texttt{ThresholdRule} entries, the filter checks that the threshold
          fields obey the physical ordering constraint: when both a critical and a
          warning bound are present for the same side (high or low), the critical
          bound must be more extreme than the warning bound.  For the high side
          this means \texttt{critHi} $\geq$ \texttt{warnHi}; for the low side this
          means \texttt{critLo} $\leq$ \texttt{warnLo}.  A rule that fails this
          check (e.g., \texttt{critLo = 10} and \texttt{warnLo = 5} when the
          correct ordering is $\leq$) is removed, because it indicates a
          column-swap error during extraction.
    \item \textbf{Content deduplication.}  After the above removals, the filter
          deduplicates by a six-field normalised signature (\texttt{class},
          \texttt{station}, \texttt{sensorType}, \texttt{severity},
          \texttt{condition}, \texttt{action}).  For each group of rules sharing
          the same signature, only the most-populated rule (the one with the
          greatest number of non-empty fields) is kept.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pass 2, additive LLM gap-filling.}
After the deterministic filter, the Validator performs a per-file LLM scan using
\texttt{ministral-3:14b}.  For each source SOP file, the model receives the SOP
text and the current set of extracted rules for that file and is asked to identify
any rule that is \emph{explicitly labelled} in the source text (e.g., a sentence
beginning with ``Rule~3:'' or a numbered item in a procedure list) but has no
corresponding entry in the extracted set.  If such a gap is found, the model
produces the missing rule in the standard JSON schema and it is appended to the
rule set.

This pass is \emph{strictly additive}: the prompt explicitly forbids the model
from removing or modifying any existing rule.  The rationale is that
over-removal at this stage would introduce false negatives that could not be
recovered downstream.  If an existing rule is genuinely incorrect, the Judge
(Section~\ref{sec:judge}) is the appropriate node to flag and address it, it has
a broader, cross-file view and more explicit quality criteria.

After the LLM gap-filling step, the deterministic filter is applied a second
time to eliminate any structurally invalid rules that the LLM may have
introduced during gap-filling, ensuring the output \texttt{validated\_rules}
list satisfies all structural invariants.

\subsubsection{Quality Gap Evaluator (Judge)}
\label{sec:judge}

\paragraph{What it receives.}
The \texttt{QualityGapEvaluator} node receives the \texttt{validated\_rules} list
and the \texttt{sop\_texts} and \texttt{doc\_types} dictionaries.  It has access to
the original SOP text, a capability that distinguishes it from the Merger, which
operates only on the extracted rule dictionaries.

\paragraph{Prompt assembly.}
The Judge's system prompt is not static: it is assembled dynamically for each run
by combining a \emph{base judge prompt} with one or more \emph{document-type-specific
completeness checklists}.  The base prompt defines the four gap categories and the
expected output schema.  The checklists enumerate the rule classes that should be
present for each document type, for example, the tabular checklist specifies that
every sensor row in the threshold table must produce at least one
\texttt{ThresholdRule} entry, while the narrative checklist covers operational and
maintenance rule expectations.  For \texttt{mixed} documents, both the narrative
checklist and the maintenance checklist are appended, because mixed SOPs combine
prose procedures with embedded maintenance tables.

\paragraph{Gap categories.}
The Judge evaluates the validated rule set against four categories of quality
deficiency:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{S1 (Missing rule classes.)}
          The Judge checks whether the rule set contains at least one rule of each
          class expected for the document type.  For a tabular SOP, the absence of
          any \texttt{ThresholdRule} is an S1 violation.  For a matrix SOP, the
          absence of any \texttt{AccessRule} is an S1 violation.  For a narrative
          SOP, missing \texttt{OperationalRule} entries are flagged.

    \item \textbf{S2 (Missing labelled rules.)}
          The Judge reads the source SOP text and checks whether any rule that is
          explicitly identified in the text (e.g., by a numbered label, a named
          procedure, or a clearly stated constraint) has no corresponding entry
          in \texttt{validated\_rules}.  This is the most semantically demanding
          of the four checks: the model must cross-reference the rule set against
          the source text rather than applying a purely structural criterion.

    \item \textbf{S3 (Quality issues.)}
          The Judge inspects individual rules for internal quality problems:
          incomplete mandatory fields, numeric threshold values that appear to be
          hallucinated (i.e., not present anywhere in the source text), physically
          impossible orderings that survived the Validator's filter, or
          \texttt{condition} descriptions that are implausibly vague or generic.

    \item \textbf{S4 (Identifier conflicts.)}
          The Judge checks for \texttt{ruleId} collisions: two rules with the same
          \texttt{ruleId} and the same \texttt{severity} are in conflict.  This
          check is relevant after a retry cycle has produced new rules that may
          have been assigned the same identifier as a pre-existing rule by the
          Normalizer.  (Note: in the standard forward pass, the Normalizer runs
          \emph{after} the Judge, so S4 detects conflicts in identifiers that were
          already present from a previous retry cycle's normalisation output.)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Deterministic pre-check.}
In addition to the LLM's evaluation, the Judge node performs a deterministic
threshold-order pre-check identical to the one in the Validator's first pass.
This redundant check is included because a rule with an impossible ordering may
have been introduced by the Validator's LLM gap-filling step after the first
deterministic filter ran, and the Judge is the last stage before normalisation
where such violations can be caught without requiring a full re-run.

\paragraph{Output format.}
The Judge outputs a JSON object with four fields:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{pass}, a boolean indicating whether the rule set is
          considered complete and free of critical gaps.
    \item \texttt{gap\_codes}, a list of gap category codes (S1, S2, S3, S4)
          for each detected violation.
    \item \texttt{suggestions}, a list of concrete, human-readable descriptions
          of what is missing or incorrect, intended for observability and debugging.
    \item \texttt{deficient\_files}, the list of SOP filenames for which the
          Judge detected violations.  This list is recorded in the pipeline log
          for observability but is \emph{not} used directly to determine whether
          a retry is triggered (see below).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Retry dispatch logic.}
The decision to trigger a re-extraction cycle is \emph{not} made by the Judge's
LLM output.  Instead, it is made by a separate deterministic routing function
(\texttt{\_judge\_routing}) that inspects \texttt{validated\_rules} directly.
A file is queued for re-extraction only when it exhibits a \emph{complete
structural failure}: zero rules of the expected primary class are present in the
current rule set for that file.  The specific failure conditions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A \texttt{tabular} SOP with no \texttt{ThresholdRule} entries.
    \item A \texttt{matrix} SOP with no \texttt{AccessRule} entries.
    \item Any SOP with zero rules of any class.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Partial} gaps detected by the LLM (e.g., a narrative SOP that has
\texttt{OperationalRule} entries but is suspected of missing one specific rule)
are \emph{not} escalated to a retry.  Instead, they are addressed by the
Validator's additive gap-filling mechanism, which operates on a per-file basis
and can insert missing rules without re-running the full merge.  The reason for
this distinction is that a full re-extraction and re-merge over an already-populated
rule set risks over-deduplication: if the merged set already contains correct rules,
passing them through the Merger a second time alongside new extractions may cause
the Merger's agreement collapse (Branch C) to discard valid rules as near-duplicates.

\paragraph{PreRetry node.}
When a structural failure is detected and the retry counter has not yet reached
its cap (one cycle), the routing function directs execution to the
\texttt{PreRetry} node.  This node increments \texttt{retry\_count},
populates the \texttt{retry\_files} dictionary with the filenames of the
deficient SOPs and their document types, and emits \texttt{Send} objects to
dispatch each deficient file to its appropriate extractor with the
\texttt{is\_retry = True} flag set.  The extractor uses this flag to append the
exhaustiveness instruction described in Section~\ref{sec:miners}.  The
re-extracted rules enter the same Merge $\to$ Validate $\to$ Judge sequence as
the original extraction; if the retry itself produces a structural failure, the
routing function observes that the retry cap has been reached and proceeds
directly to normalisation without further re-extraction.

\subsubsection{Identifier Canonicalizer (Normalizer)}
\label{sec:normalizer}

\paragraph{What it receives.}
The \texttt{IdentifierCanonicalizer} node receives \texttt{validated\_rules},
the cleaned, gap-filled, judge-approved rule set.  At this stage, the semantic
content of every rule (class, station, sensor, condition, action, thresholds) is
considered final.  The Normalizer's sole responsibility is to assign a
human-readable, structured \texttt{ruleId} to each rule.

\paragraph{Why ruleId assignment is a separate step.}
Rules produced by the extractor nodes carry auto-generated \texttt{ruleId} values
(typically an index-based placeholder like \texttt{R01}, \texttt{R02}, etc.) that
carry no naming-convention information and cannot be matched against ground-truth
identifiers in the strict evaluation metric.  A separate canonicalisation step is
therefore needed to bring these identifiers in line with the iMAKS naming schema
before evaluation.

Performing this step as a separate LLM call, rather than asking each extractor
to produce canonical identifiers directly, prevents identifier-generation noise
from influencing the extracted semantic fields.  If the extractor were responsible
for both extraction and identifier assignment, a format error in the identifier
could corrupt the rule object or cause the extractor to produce fewer rules.

\paragraph{Few-shot prompt and naming convention.}
The \texttt{qwen/qwen3-4b-2507} model is given a few-shot prompt constructed by
the \texttt{\_load\_gt\_few\_shots()} function, which loads up to 20 ground-truth
\texttt{ruleId}/rule pairs from \texttt{ground\_truth.csv}.  These examples
demonstrate the iMAKS naming schema:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{ThresholdRule} identifiers follow the pattern
          \texttt{RULE-THR-\{STATION\}-\{SENSOR\}-\{SEVERITY\}}, e.g.,
          \texttt{RULE-THR-ST01-TMP-CRIT} for the critical temperature threshold
          at filling station ST01.
    \item \texttt{OperationalRule} identifiers follow
          \texttt{RULE-\{STATION\}-\{NN\}}, where \texttt{NN} is a two-digit
          sequence number, e.g., \texttt{RULE-ST01-01}.
    \item \texttt{MaintenanceRule} identifiers follow
          \texttt{MAINT-\{NN\}}, e.g., \texttt{MAINT-01}.
    \item \texttt{AccessRule} identifiers follow
          \texttt{ACCESS-\{NN\}}, e.g., \texttt{ACCESS-01}.
\end{itemize}

The model is asked to read the validated rules and produce a JSON list in which
each entry contains only two fields: the original rule's extracted content
(used to identify the rule) and the new canonical \texttt{ruleId}.

\paragraph{Surgical field replacement.}
Only the \texttt{ruleId} field from the LLM's output is accepted.  All other
fields, class, station, sensorType, condition, action, threshold values, unit,
severity, and source metadata, are taken verbatim from the corresponding
validated rule, not from the LLM's output.  This surgical approach means that
any formatting error or hallucination in the LLM's response can affect at most
the \texttt{ruleId} of a single rule and cannot corrupt semantic content.

If the number of rules returned by the LLM does not match the number of validated
rules (an indicator that the LLM dropped or duplicated rules), the node falls back
to the original placeholder identifiers for the entire batch rather than attempting
a partial match that could misalign identifiers and rule content.

\paragraph{Metric validity and the strict-F1 caveat.}
Because the Normalizer is shown up to 20 ground-truth identifiers drawn from the
\emph{same} benchmark being evaluated, the resulting \texttt{ruleId} values may
more closely match the annotation conventions than would be achievable in a blind
deployment, giving F1\textsubscript{strict} a modest artificial advantage.

\textbf{This does not affect F1\textsubscript{content}}, which is the primary
reported metric throughout this thesis.  The \texttt{content\_agreement} function
(Equation~\ref{eq:content_agreement}) scores the \texttt{class}, \texttt{station},
\texttt{sensor}, \texttt{condition}, \texttt{action}, and threshold fields; it does
not include \texttt{ruleId} in its computation at all.  All ablation comparisons
and the cross-system comparison in Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results} use
F1\textsubscript{content} as the decisive criterion.
F1\textsubscript{strict} figures are reported for completeness but should be
interpreted with this caveat in mind.

\subsection{Pipeline Topology and Execution Flow}
\label{sec:topology}

\paragraph{Graph construction.}
The complete computation graph is assembled by the \texttt{build\_graph()} function,
which takes an optional \texttt{ablation} tag as its only argument.  The function
instantiates a LangGraph \texttt{StateGraph} over the \texttt{PipelineState}
schema, registers each node, and wires the edges.  The graph is compiled once by
calling \texttt{.compile()}, producing an executable object that can process an
entire SOP corpus in a single invocation.  The ablation tag, if present, causes
\texttt{build\_graph()} to omit or bypass the corresponding node (e.g., when
\texttt{ablation = "no\_cm"}, the Consensus Merger is replaced by a direct
pass-through that forwards \texttt{extracted\_rules} to \texttt{merged\_rules}
unchanged).

\paragraph{Execution flow.}
The directed execution flow for the full pipeline is:

\begin{verbatim}
START → Coordinator → [conditional fan-out]
          ├─ Extractor A (narrative / mixed files)
          ├─ Extractor B (tabular files)
          └─ Extractor C (matrix files)
                 └─ [fan-in: sum reducer on extracted_rules]
        → ConsensusMerger → Validator → QualityGapEvaluator (Judge)
              → [conditional routing]
                    ├─ (structural failure + retry budget remaining)
                    │      → PreRetry → [fan-out to relevant extractor(s)]
                    │             → ConsensusMerger → Validator → Judge → …
                    └─ (pass or retry budget exhausted)
                           → IdentifierCanonicalizer (Normalizer) → Save → END
\end{verbatim}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_full} illustrates the compiled graph topology.  Solid
edges represent unconditional data flow.  Dashed edges represent the two
conditional routing points: the Coordinator's fan-out (which routes each SOP to
exactly one extractor based on its document type) and the Judge's routing decision
(which either sends execution to \texttt{PreRetry} for a retry cycle or to the
Normalizer to finalise the results).

\paragraph{Conditional fan-out from Coordinator.}
LangGraph's \texttt{Send}-based conditional edges are used to implement the
Coordinator's fan-out.  After the Coordinator fills the \texttt{doc\_types}
dictionary, the \texttt{dispatch\_to\_extractors} function is called as a
conditional edge function.  It iterates over \texttt{doc\_types} and emits one
\texttt{Send} object per SOP file, targeting the node name corresponding to the
file's document type.  LangGraph schedules all emitted \texttt{Send} calls
concurrently; the three extractor nodes therefore process their respective files
in parallel, with their outputs merged into \texttt{extracted\_rules} via the
\texttt{operator.add} reducer.

\paragraph{Conditional routing after the Judge.}
The \texttt{\_judge\_routing} function examines the current \texttt{validated\_rules}
and \texttt{retry\_count} to decide the next node.  If at least one SOP file
exhibits a complete structural failure (zero rules of the expected primary class)
and the retry counter is below the cap, execution is directed to \texttt{pre\_retry},
which populates \texttt{retry\_files} and emits \texttt{Send} objects for another
extraction round.  The retry outputs re-enter the graph at the Merger node and
proceed through the full Validate $\to$ Judge sequence again.  If no structural
failure is detected, or if the retry cap has been reached, execution proceeds
unconditionally to the Normalizer.

\subsection{Error Resilience Strategy}
\label{sec:error_resilience}

Running large language models locally on consumer hardware introduces failure modes
that do not appear in cloud API settings: GPU out-of-memory crashes, inference
server overloads, network timeouts on the loopback interface, and occasional
partial responses where the model terminates generation before closing the JSON
array.  The pipeline addresses each of these through a combination of
exponential-backoff retries, graceful empty-list handling, and a cascading JSON
parser.

\paragraph{Exponential-backoff retry loop.}
Every LLM invocation in the pipeline is made through the shared \texttt{llm\_call}
function, which wraps the API call in a retry loop with up to \texttt{MAX\_RETRIES}
(3) attempts.  On the first failure, the function waits \texttt{RETRY\_BASE\_DELAY}
(15\,s) before retrying.  On each subsequent failure, the delay doubles (so the
sequence is 15\,s, 30\,s).  If the third attempt also fails, the original exception
is re-raised.  The doubling schedule prevents hammering an overloaded inference
server while still attempting recovery from transient issues such as a momentary
GPU stall.  If the exception propagates out of \texttt{llm\_call}, it terminates the
current LangGraph node; LangGraph marks the node as failed and halts the pipeline
run with an informative traceback.

\paragraph{Empty-list fallback in the Merger.}
If an extractor node fails entirely and produces no rules (either because all
retries were exhausted or because no SOPs of its type were present in the corpus),
the \texttt{ConsensusMerger} receives an empty \texttt{extracted\_rules} list.
It returns an empty \texttt{merged\_rules} list in this case, which the Validator
and Judge handle gracefully: the Validator's deterministic filter over an empty
list produces an empty list, and the Judge's structural failure check detects a
complete failure and triggers the retry mechanism if the budget allows.  No node
raises an unhandled exception when operating on an empty rule list.

\paragraph{Cascading JSON parser.}
The LLM output in every node is parsed by the \texttt{parse\_rules} function,
which applies four recovery strategies in sequence:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Direct parse.}  The raw string is passed to
          \texttt{json.loads()} after stripping any chain-of-thought
          \texttt{<think>}\ldots\texttt{</think>} blocks that some models emit.
          If the result is a list, it is returned directly.  If the result is a
          dictionary, the function looks for a \texttt{"rules"} key and returns
          its value, printing any \texttt{"reasoning"} value for debug inspection.
    \item \textbf{Bracket repair.}  If direct parsing fails, the function attempts
          to rebalance truncated output by appending the appropriate number of
          closing brackets and braces.  Models that run out of token budget
          sometimes terminate mid-array, leaving a structurally incomplete but
          otherwise correct JSON fragment.  After rebalancing, \texttt{json.loads()}
          is attempted again.
    \item \textbf{Fence and pattern scan.}  If bracket repair also fails, the
          function searches for a Markdown code fence
          (\texttt{```json \ldots ```}) and attempts to parse its content.
          It also tries to locate an outer JSON object or array anywhere in the
          response using a regular-expression scan.
    \item \textbf{Rule-object salvage.}  As a last resort, the function scans
          the raw string for individual JSON objects that contain a \texttt{ruleId}
          key and collects whatever valid rule dictionaries it can find.  This
          recovers partial outputs where the array wrapper is corrupted but the
          individual rule objects are syntactically correct.
\end{enumerate}

If none of the four strategies produces a non-empty list, \texttt{parse\_rules}
returns an empty list rather than raising an exception, allowing the pipeline to
continue with whatever other rules were extracted from other files.

\section{Ablation Study and Results}

conducted using the iMAKS dataset and the evaluation protocol described in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. The study systematically disables or simplifies one
component at a time and measures the resulting change in extraction quality. All
experiments were run with random seed~42 and the same model assignments
(Table~\ref{tab:node_roles}).

\subsection{Implementation of Ablation Variants}
\label{sec:ablation_impl}

Each ablation variant is built as an independent \texttt{StateGraph} by the
function \texttt{build\_graph(ablation)}. Passing the ablation tag at compile
time rather than introducing runtime branches inside shared nodes ensures
every condition is a clean, independently auditable pipeline and avoids
accidentally sharing mutable state between conditions.

% ¶  coordinator_node_no_cm: 3-pattern keyword regex replaces LLM call; patterns for tabular / matrix / mixed.
\paragraph{No LLM Coordinator (\texttt{abl\_noCM}).}
The \texttt{coordinator\_node} is replaced by \texttt{coordinator\_node\_no\_cm},
a keyword/regex classifier that requires no LLM call.
Three ordered patterns are tested against the first 8\,000 characters of each SOP:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the pattern \texttt{CRIT\_LO|WARN\_LO|WARN\_HI|CRIT\_HI} triggers
          \texttt{tabular} (threshold table);
    \item a pattern matching zone/area/sector names with authorisation keywords
          triggers \texttt{matrix};
    \item a pattern matching maintenance identifiers or drift/cross-sensor keywords
          triggers \texttt{mixed}.
\end{itemize}
Any document not matching the first three patterns defaults to \texttt{narrative}.
This ablation isolates the value of having an LLM read the full structural layout
of a document before deciding which extraction prompt to apply.

% ¶  validate_node_noop: merged rules forwarded unchanged, no deterministic filter, no LLM gap-fill.
\paragraph{No Validator (\texttt{abl\_noValidator}).}
The \texttt{validate\_node} is replaced by \texttt{validate\_node\_noop}, a
passthrough that forwards the merged rule set to the judge completely unchanged.
Three operations are bypassed simultaneously:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Deterministic post-filter}: class validation, threshold hygiene
          (e.g.\ ensuring \texttt{critLo} $<$ \texttt{warnLo} $<$ \texttt{warnHi}
          $<$ \texttt{critHi}), and physics ordering checks.
    \item \textbf{LLM-based gap-filling}: a grounding pass that re-reads the source
          SOP to recover rules the extractor missed and fills in incomplete field
          values.
    \item \textbf{Sensor-identifier normalisation}: resolution of abbreviated node
          references to their canonical Knowledge-Graph names (e.g.\
          \texttt{ST01\_FLW} $\to$ \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_FLW}).  This is directly
          visible in the raw output: every SOP-002 rule in the \texttt{abl\_noValidator}
          CSV carries the shortened sensor identifier that the extractor emitted,
          whereas the full pipeline promotes each to the full KG node name.
\end{enumerate}
Even obviously malformed rules, wrong class names, empty condition fields,
threshold values with unit suffixes attached, are forwarded without correction.
The ablation therefore measures the combined contribution of all three operations
in a single condition.  In production the deterministic filter and normalisation
steps are so cheap that removing them is never justified; their inclusion here
maximises the measured contrast.

% ¶  build_graph("no_judge"): unconditional edge validate → normalize; no gap report, no retry loop.
\paragraph{No Judge (\texttt{abl\_noJudge}).}
The \texttt{QualityGapEvaluator} node and the associated retry loop are removed
by connecting the Validate node directly to the Normalizer with an unconditional
edge.  No per-file gap report is produced, no structural failure detection is
performed, and no re-extraction cycle is triggered regardless of how many rules
were found.

% ¶  baseline.py: regex parser, knowledge limited to the 24 KG-seed nodes (ST01–ST04 only);
%     4 dedicated parse functions (SOP-001/002/003/004); no LLM; lower-bound reference.
\paragraph{Deterministic Baseline (\texttt{baseline\_b0}).}
A hand-crafted regex parser whose station-sensor inventory is derived from
and hard-coded to match the 24~nodes of the KG seed file
(\texttt{nodes\_factory.csv}): the four production stations
(\texttt{ST01}--\texttt{ST04}) and their three sensors each.
The five auxiliary sensor stations (\texttt{SRV01}, \texttt{WRH01}, \texttt{CHM01},
\texttt{RND01}, \texttt{CAF01}) do not appear in the seed and are therefore
structurally unreachable; all threshold and maintenance rules for those stations
represent entities that must be \emph{discovered} from the SOP text, a capability
the regex parser lacks by design.
It processes the four SOP document types with dedicated parsing functions, with no
LLM calls.  Its output serves as a lower-bound reference for what pre-existing
domain knowledge alone can achieve without any language understanding.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{sec:ablation_interpretation}

\textbf{Full pipeline superiority.}
The complete multi-agent pipeline achieves
F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.909, outperforming the best single-agent few-shot
baseline (\texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, F1\textsubscript{content}~=~0.728) by 0.181 points
  a relative improvement of approximately 25\%.
Its recall of~0.872 substantially exceeds every single-agent approach, and it
simultaneously maintains very high precision~(0.949), producing only 4~false
positives out of 79~extracted rules.
The 75~true positive content matches represent 87.2\% of the 86~ground-truth rules,
leaving just 11~rules unrecovered.

\textbf{Impact of the Quality Gap Evaluator.}
Removing the judge loop (\texttt{abl\_noJudge}) drops
F1\textsubscript{content} from 0.909 to 0.885, a loss of 0.024 points.
Notably, the noJudge run produces exactly the same number of predicted rules
(79) and the same strict TP count (58) as the full pipeline, but achieves
only 73~content TPs compared to~75 for the full run.
This means the judge+retry cycle does not substantially change \emph{which rules}
are found (the Validator already recovers most), but it does refine the quality of
a small number of rules whose field values are close to but not quite over the
$0.6$ content-agreement threshold.
In practical terms, the judge primarily catches cases where the extractor produced
a structurally correct rule (right station, right sensor) but with imprecise
condition wording or a missing numeric threshold tier, and the retry pass fills
those gaps.  A concrete illustration from the raw CSVs: \texttt{abl\_noJudge}
conditions read ``\textit{Flow drops below 105~L/min at ST01\_FILLING}'', while
after the judge+retry cycle the full pipeline normalises the same condition to
``\textit{Flow $\leq$ 105~L/min at ST01\_FILLING}'', a semantically equivalent
but more precise phrasing that crosses the $0.6$ content-agreement threshold in
the Hungarian assignment.

\textbf{Importance of the Validator.}
Without the Validator (\texttt{abl\_noValidator}),
F1\textsubscript{content} drops to 0.894, a loss of 0.015 points from the full
pipeline.
Counter-intuitively, the noValidator run produces \emph{fewer} predictions
(75) than the full pipeline (79), not more.  This is because the Validator's
dominant role here is the LLM \emph{gap-filling} pass: it actively adds rules
that the extractors never emitted by re-reading the source SOP against the
current rule set and recovering omissions.  Those 4 additional rules in the full
pipeline include 3~that are true positives and 1~false positive.
Without gap-filling, the pipeline emits only what the extractors found directly
(75~rules), and without the deterministic filter some rules that should have been
removed survive, yet the net prediction count is still lower because gap-filling
added more than the filter removed.
Precision therefore rises slightly (0.960 vs.\ 0.949): with fewer total predictions,
and specifically without the 1~spurious gap-filled rule, raw FP count falls to~3.
However, recall drops to 0.837 (from 0.872), recovering only 72~of 86 GT rules
instead of 75, because those 3 gap-filled TPs are absent.
Additionally, the sensor-identifier normalisation step is also bypassed: the raw
\texttt{abl\_noValidator} CSV shows abbreviated names such as \texttt{ST01\_FLW}
in place of the canonical \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_FLW}, which lowers the strict
TP count from 58 (full) to 54 and explains why the strict F1 penalty
(0.703$\to$0.671) is proportionally larger than the content F1 penalty
(0.909$\to$0.894).
The net trade is clear: the Validator contributes 3~additional content-true-positives
at the cost of 1~content-false-positive, a favourable exchange given that each
recovered rule is a safety-critical operational constraint that would otherwise be
absent from the Knowledge Graph.

\textbf{The LLM Coordinator vs.\ keyword heuristics.}
Replacing the LLM-based document classifier with the three-pattern keyword
classifier (\texttt{abl\_noCM}) reduces F1\textsubscript{content} to 0.872, a
loss of 0.037 points and the largest single-component drop in the study.
Interestingly, the recall is \emph{identical} to the full pipeline (both achieve
TP~=~75, FN~=~11): the keyword classifier routes all documents to the correct
extractor often enough that it does not miss any GT rules that the LLM classifier
finds.
The difference is entirely in precision: \texttt{abl\_noCM} extracts 86~rules
and incurs 11~FPs, while the full pipeline extracts only 79~rules with 4~FPs.
This reveals the LLM Coordinator's true contribution: it does not improve
\emph{what} the pipeline finds, but it prevents routing a document to the wrong
extractor prompt, which would cause that extractor to generate spurious rules
for the wrong rule class.  For example, a mixed-type document routed to the
narrative extractor might produce \texttt{OperationalRule} entries for rows
that should have been classified as \texttt{MaintenanceRule}, plausible-looking
rules that score just below the 0.6 content-agreement threshold and inflate FP.

\textbf{Deterministic baseline.}
The hand-crafted regex parser (\texttt{baseline\_b0}) achieves a perfect
\emph{strict} precision of~1.0 (strict~FP~=~0): every emitted rule identifier is
explicitly hard-coded from the known station-sensor inventory, so no spurious rules
can be generated.
Note that the table reports the \emph{content} metric, under which the baseline
still incurs 11~FPs (P\textsubscript{c}~=~0.804) because some extracted rules
match structurally but diverge in condition wording from the ground truth.
Its F1\textsubscript{content} of~0.634, driven by recall~0.523,
establishes the performance ceiling of pure domain knowledge without language
understanding.
Recovering just 45~of 86~GT rules, it misses 41~rules mostly the operationally
nuanced conditions, access constraints, and cross-sensor maintenance patterns that
require reading and interpreting sentence-level semantics.
Any change to the SOP layout or the addition of a new sensor would require
manual re-engineering of the regex rules, underscoring the brittleness that
motivates the LLM-based pipeline.

\textbf{Cross-system comparison.}
All four ablated LLM-based configurations (F1\textsubscript{content} 0.872--0.909)
substantially outperform the best single-agent approach (0.728).
Even the weakest ablation, \texttt{abl\_noCM} at 0.872, exceeds \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}
by 0.144 points nearly 20\% relative improvement despite using only a keyword
classifier to route documents.
This confirms that the multi-stage architecture itself (specialised extractors +
validator + judge) is the primary driver of quality, not any individual model or
any single pipeline stage.

\textbf{Overall conclusion.}
The ablation study demonstrates that every retained component contributes measurably
to the final extraction quality:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{LLM Coordinator} reduces false positives by ensuring each
          document is processed with the semantically appropriate extraction prompt.
    \item The \textbf{Validator} adds rules via LLM gap-filling (recovering rules
          present in the source text that the extractors missed), removes malformed
          candidates through the deterministic post-filter, and normalises sensor
          identifiers to canonical KG node names.
    \item The \textbf{Judge+Retry loop} refines rule quality at the margin by
          triggering a focused re-extraction of documents where the initial pass
          produced structurally incomplete rules.
    \item The \textbf{deterministic Consensus Merger} consolidates parallel extractor
          outputs without introducing LLM-induced merging errors, forwarding genuine
          ambiguities to nodes with grounding access.
\end{itemize}
Together, these stages enable the pipeline to recover 87.2\% of the ground-truth
operational rules with 94.9\% precision, a result that is unattainable by any
single LLM regardless of its prompting strategy.

\section{Multi-Run Stability and Inter-Agent Agreement}
\label{sec:multirun}

A single deterministic run (seed~42, temperature~0) establishes the nominal
performance of the pipeline, but it does not answer whether the extracted rule
set is \emph{stable}, i.e., whether re-running the pipeline under natural
LLM non-determinism produces substantially different outputs.
This is an important practical question for industrial deployment: a pipeline
whose output varies significantly across runs would require expensive human
review of every re-extraction.

To characterise this stability, the multi-run evaluation script
\texttt{step2\_multi\_run\_eval.py} runs the complete multi-agent pipeline $K$
independent times with a temperature of $0.5$ and without a fixed seed, and
then measures agreement across runs using several complementary metrics.

% § 7.1  Patched llm_call omits seed and extra_body.seed; T=0.5; 20 runs; same model assignments as ablation.
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:multirun_setup}

Each run uses a monkey-patched version of the LLM call function.
The original \texttt{llm\_call} in \texttt{step2\_TEST.py} passes
\texttt{seed=42} as a top-level API parameter and also embeds it inside
\texttt{extra\_body=\{"options": \{"seed": 42, \ldots\}\}} for Ollama-compatible
backends.
Before each pipeline invocation, \texttt{step2\_multi\_run\_eval.py} replaces
\texttt{\_pipe.llm\_call} and \texttt{\_pipe.save\_node} at runtime with
patched versions: the patched \texttt{llm\_call} omits both seed fields,
so the inference server draws a genuinely independent sample from the model's
distribution at every step; the patched \texttt{save\_node} redirects output to
a per-run file \texttt{ext\_multi\_agent\_run\{i:02d\}.csv} in the
\texttt{multi\_run/} subdirectory.
The default configuration executes \textbf{20 independent runs at temperature~0.5}
(i.e.\ \texttt{MULTI\_RUN\_TEMPERATURE}~=~0.5, \texttt{--runs 20}), trading a
modest increase in stochasticity against a robust sample size for the statistical
estimates below.
The same model assignments as the ablation study are used
(Table~\ref{tab:node_roles}).
Existing run files are detected and skipped automatically, so interrupted
experiments can be resumed; the \texttt{--skip-runs} flag bypasses pipeline
execution entirely and recomputes all statistics from the already-saved CSVs.

% § 7.2  compute_f1_stats: per-run F1 via run_evaluation(); aggregate mean/std/CI95 (z=1.96/√K).
\subsection{F1 Aggregation Across Runs}
\label{sec:multirun_f1}

For each run CSV, the evaluation framework computes
F1\textsubscript{strict}, F1\textsubscript{content}, and all associated
precision, recall, TP, FP, FN counts.
A summary row is computed by taking the mean and standard deviation of each
metric across all $K$ runs, and a 95\% confidence interval is derived using
the standard formula $\text{CI}_{95} = 1.96 \cdot \sigma / \sqrt{K}$.
This allows claims such as
``the pipeline achieves F1\textsubscript{content}~=~$\mu \pm \sigma$'' to be
made with statistical grounding, rather than relying on a single seed.

% § 7.3  IAAS: pairwise semantic agreement across all K(K-1)/2 run pairs via content_agreement + Hungarian.
\subsection{Inter-Agent Agreement Score (IAAS)}
\label{sec:iaas}

Beyond aggregate F1, the script computes the \emph{Inter-Agent Agreement Score}
(IAAS), a pairwise measure of how much two runs of the pipeline agree on the
semantic content of extracted rules.
Unlike F1, which compares each run to a fixed ground truth, IAAS compares
pairs of \emph{pipeline outputs} to one another, measuring internal consistency
regardless of whether either run is correct.

% § 7.3.1  5-field text (class|station|sensor|condition|action) → SBERT all-MiniLM-L6-v2;
%           shared embedding cache built once for all rules across all runs.
\subsubsection{Rule Embedding and Content Agreement}

Two distinct SBERT encoding steps are used, each serving a different downstream
computation.

\paragraph{Content agreement (pairwise IAAS).}
The \texttt{content\_agreement} function imported from
\texttt{layer\_3.step3\_evaluation\_rules} scores a pair of rules by computing
the cosine similarity between their \texttt{condition} embeddings and between
their \texttt{action} embeddings separately, then combining them with a
weighted sum (equal weights by default).
A pair whose condition texts are semantically equivalent and whose action texts
match closely receives a score near~1; mismatched pairs score near~0.
To avoid redundant SBERT inference, \texttt{\_embed\_all\_rules} collects all
\texttt{condition} and \texttt{action} field texts from every run, deduplicates
them, encodes them in a single batch using the
\texttt{sentence-transformers}/\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model, and stores the
results in a shared dictionary cache keyed by text string.
This cache is passed to every subsequent \texttt{content\_agreement} call,
ensuring each unique text is encoded exactly once across all $K(K{-}1)/2$
pairwise comparisons.

\paragraph{Rule-level embedding (clustering).}
For clustering, each rule is represented as a single concatenated string:
\texttt{class}~|~\texttt{station}~|~\texttt{sensor}~|~\texttt{condition}~|~\texttt{action},
where empty fields are omitted.
All unique such strings across all runs are encoded jointly in one SBERT batch
(duplicates removed via \texttt{dict.fromkeys} before encoding), and the
resulting per-text embeddings are stored in a lookup dictionary used during the
greedy clustering pass.
This encoding is independent of the pairwise cache above.

% § 7.3.2  _pairwise_iaas: Hungarian on CA cost matrix; score / max(|Ri|,|Rj|) penalises cardinality drift.
\subsubsection{Pairwise IAAS Computation}

For each ordered pair of runs $(i, j)$, a cost matrix
$C \in \mathbb{R}^{|R_i| \times |R_j|}$ is constructed where
$C_{ab} = \texttt{content\_agreement}(r^i_a,\, r^j_b)$, i.e.\ the semantic
agreement score (as defined above) between rule $a$ from run $i$ and rule $b$
from run $j$.
The Hungarian algorithm (\texttt{scipy.optimize.linear\_sum\_assignment}) then
finds the one-to-one assignment that \emph{maximises} the total agreement,
yielding the optimal bipartite matching between the two rule sets.
The IAAS score for the pair is:
\begin{equation}
\text{IAAS}(i,j) = \frac{\sum_{(a,b) \in \text{matching}} CA(r^i_a, r^j_b)}
                        {\max(|R_i|, |R_j|)}
\label{eq:iaas}
\end{equation}
where $R_i$ and $R_j$ are the rule sets of runs $i$ and $j$,
$CA(\cdot,\cdot)$ denotes the content agreement score (weighted cosine
similarity over the condition and action fields), and unmatched rules
contribute~0 to the numerator.
Dividing by $\max(|R_i|, |R_j|)$ rather than the size of either set
individually penalises cardinality differences: a run that extracts many
spurious extra rules receives a lower IAAS even if all its matched rules are
semantically close, because the denominator grows with the larger set.
The global IAAS reported in Table~\ref{tab:iaas_results} is the mean over all
$\binom{K}{2} = K(K{-}1)/2$ pairwise scores.

% § 7.3.3  _cluster_rules: greedy SBERT cosine clustering (threshold=0.90, centroid updated as mean);
%           binary presence matrix → Fleiss κ; hallucination <30%, consensus ≥70% of runs.
\subsubsection{Rule Clustering, Fleiss~$\kappa$, and Consensus}

\paragraph{Greedy clustering.}
All rules from all $K$ runs are pooled and clustered greedily using the
five-field rule text embeddings computed in the previous step.
Each rule is processed in order: it is assigned to the existing cluster whose
current centroid embedding has the highest cosine similarity to the rule's
embedding, provided that similarity is at least
\texttt{SIMILARITY\_THRESHOLD}~=~0.90; if no such cluster exists, a new
singleton cluster is created.
After each assignment, the cluster centroid is updated to the mean of all
member embeddings (computed by stacking and averaging the member tensors).
Each resulting cluster represents a single \emph{conceptual rule}, a
semantically distinct operational constraint, that may have been extracted
in some runs but not others.

\paragraph{Binary presence matrix and Fleiss~$\kappa$.}
A binary presence matrix $M \in \{0,1\}^{N_{\text{clusters}} \times K}$ is
constructed, where $M_{c,r} = 1$ if at least one member of cluster~$c$ came
from run~$r$, and~0 otherwise.
In the Fleiss~$\kappa$ framework, the \emph{items} are the $N_{\text{clusters}}$
unique rule concepts, the \emph{raters} are the $K$~pipeline runs, and the
\emph{binary rating} for each item--rater pair is presence or absence of that
concept in that run.
Fleiss~$\kappa$ is computed over this matrix to quantify inter-run reliability
beyond chance: $\kappa = 0$ means agreement no better than random, $\kappa = 1$
means perfect agreement, and negative values indicate systematic disagreement.

\paragraph{Stability labels and consensus representative.}
Each cluster is assigned a stability label based on the fraction of runs in
which it appears ($\text{run\_frac}_c = M_{c,\cdot}\mathbf{1} / K$):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Hallucination proxy} if
          $\text{run\_frac}_c < \texttt{HALLUCINATION\_FRAC} = 0.30$:
          the rule concept appears in fewer than 30\% of runs, suggesting it
          is an artefact of LLM randomness rather than a genuine operational
          constraint.
    \item \emph{Consensus rule} if
          $\text{run\_frac}_c \geq \texttt{CONSENSUS\_FRAC} = 0.70$:
          the rule concept appears in at least 70\% of runs, confirming
          stable, reproducible extraction.
    \item \emph{Borderline} otherwise (30--70\%): the concept is extracted
          inconsistently and warrants human review.
\end{itemize}
For each consensus cluster, the most representative rule, the cluster member
with the highest mean content agreement to all other members in the same
cluster, is selected as the canonical extraction and written to
\texttt{step3\_results/consensus\_rules.csv}, augmented with a
\texttt{run\_frac} column recording the empirical stability fraction.
This file constitutes the most deployment-trustworthy subset of the
pipeline's output.

\subsection{Outputs}
\label{sec:multirun_outputs}

Running \texttt{step2\_multi\_run\_eval.py} (with defaults: 20 runs,
temperature~0.5) produces four output artefacts:
\begin{itemize}

\subsection{Significance of Stability Analysis for Industrial Deployment}
\label{sec:multirun_significance}

The multi-run evaluation addresses a gap that pure benchmark F1 cannot close.
An F1\textsubscript{content} of 0.909 on a single seed (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results})
proves that the pipeline can achieve high quality; the measured IAAS~=~0.942~$\pm$~0.027
and Fleiss~$\kappa$~=~0.596 prove that it does so \emph{reliably}.
In an industrial Digital Twin context where SOP re-extraction may be triggered
by document updates, the consensus rate of 74.7\% (68 of 91 unique rule concepts)
means that operators can trust that the core Knowledge Graph the rules that appear
in $\geq$70\% of runs will remain stable between extraction cycles, reducing the
burden of manual post-verification.
The measured hallucination-proxy rate of 22.0\% (20 of 91 unique rule concepts)
identifies the fraction of extracted rule concepts that are artefacts of LLM
randomness rather than genuine operational constraints: these 20 concepts appeared
in fewer than 30\% of runs and cannot be trusted to survive re-extraction.
A further 3 borderline concepts (30--70\% prevalence) are extracted inconsistently
and also require review.
In total, the 23 non-consensus rule concepts (20 hallucination proxies + 3
borderline) warrant a filtered deployment strategy: the 68 consensus rules (written
to \texttt{step3\_results/consensus\_rules.csv} with their empirical
\texttt{run\_frac} values) are immediately deployable, while all 23 non-consensus
rules should be queued for human review before loading into a production graph.
This two-tier deployment strategy, auto-load consensus, review non-consensus,
is directly enabled by the IAAS framework and represents a practical operationalisation
of uncertainty quantification for LLM-based knowledge extraction systems.

\section{Knowledge Graph Construction and Integration}
\label{sec:neo4j}

Once the multi-agent pipeline produces its best extraction run, the extracted rules
are loaded into a Neo4j graph database where they are overlaid on a hand-crafted
ground-truth graph (the ABox) that encodes the factory's physical structure and
operational history.
The integration proceeds in four sequential steps, all implemented in the
\texttt{layer\_4/} module and executed in order:
(1)~ABox loading (\texttt{step4\_neo4j\_loader.py}) populates the ground-truth graph
from two CSV seed files;
(2)~extracted rule overlay (\texttt{step5\_neo4j\_rules.py}) adds the LLM-produced
rules as \texttt{:ExtractedRule} nodes and wires them to the ABox topology via
\texttt{:COVERS} and \texttt{:APPLIES\_TO} edges;
(3)~typed triplet enrichment (\texttt{step7\_rule\_to\_triplets.py}) converts each
flat rule node into a richer class-aware sub-graph by creating typed intermediate
nodes (\texttt{:ThresholdSpec}, \texttt{:Action}) and semantically labelled edges;
(4)~phase-2 graph coverage evaluation (\texttt{step6\_phase2\_evaluation.py})
traverses the fully assembled graph to check whether the extracted rules cover every
ABox fault and maintenance event, and writes the per-event results to
\texttt{layers/step3\_results/phase2\_evaluation.csv}.
Steps~5, 7, and~6 share the same auto-selection logic: they both read
\texttt{layers/step3\_results/evaluation\_summary.csv} and pick the run with the
highest \texttt{content\_f1} score, so the entire layer-4 pipeline always operates
on the best available extraction without manual configuration.
Connection parameters (URI, username, password, database) are loaded from a
\texttt{.env} file at the project root, with safe defaults of
\texttt{bolt://localhost:7687} / \texttt{neo4j} / \texttt{neo4j} / \texttt{neo4j}.

% § 8.1  Hand-crafted ground-truth graph: 115 nodes, 341 edges; step4_neo4j_loader.py uses MERGE + uniqueness constraint.
\subsection{ABox Design and Loading}
\label{sec:abox}

The ABox is the hand-crafted ground-truth graph that captures the complete
structure of the iMAKS industrial facility.
It is stored as two CSV files (\texttt{nodes.csv}, \texttt{edges.csv}) in
\texttt{data/dataset/kg\_seed/} and loaded into Neo4j by
\texttt{step4\_neo4j\_loader.py}.

% § 8.1.1  8 node types: System(1), Zone(7), Component(9), Sensor(22), AnomalyEvent(14),
%           Maintenance(8), Person(44), SafetyEvent(10), total 115 nodes.
\subsubsection{Node Types}

The ABox contains 115 nodes of eight semantic types:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System (1)}   the top-level \texttt{iMAKS} node representing
          the entire food-packaging facility.
    \item \textbf{Zone (7)}   physical areas of the facility: Production Area,
          Server Room, General Warehouse, Main Entrance, Chemical Storage,
          R\&D Lab, and Cafeteria. Each zone acts as a security and operational
          boundary.
    \item \textbf{Component (9)}   production stations and auxiliary rooms
          (e.g.\ \texttt{ST01\_FILLING}, \texttt{ST02\_SEALING},
          \texttt{SRV01\_SERVERROOM}), each associated with a zone and a station
          type.
    \item \textbf{Sensor (22)}   physical measurement devices attached to
          components (e.g.\ \texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_TMP},
          \texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_CUR}). Each sensor node carries its nominal value,
          warning bounds (\texttt{warnLo}, \texttt{warnHi}), critical bounds
          (\texttt{critLo}, \texttt{critHi}), unit, and sensor type
          (TMP, PRS, FLW, CUR, SPD, VIB, TEN, CNT, HUM).
    \item \textbf{AnomalyEvent (14)}   recorded fault events from four days of
          simulated operations (e.g.\ \texttt{GT-0009}: a correlated speed anomaly
          at the packaging station caused by a current drift at the sealing station
          90 minutes upstream).
    \item \textbf{Maintenance (8)}   scheduled or reactive maintenance procedures,
          each keyed by a \texttt{ruleId} (MAINT-01 through MAINT-08) and a
          priority (CRITICAL, HIGH, MEDIUM).
    \item \textbf{Person (44)}   factory personnel with assigned roles (operator,
          engineer, manager, chemist, etc.) and shift schedules.
    \item \textbf{SafetyEvent (10)}   recorded safety incidents linked to zones
          and personnel.
\end{itemize}

% § 8.1.2  10 edge types and counts: contains(16), part_of(9), monitors(44), triggers(22),
%           resolves(8), feeds_into(3), correlates_with(1), authorized_for(218), detected_in(10), involves(10), total 341.
\subsubsection{Edge Types}

The ABox contains 341 edges of ten semantic types:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{contains} (16)   System or Zone $\rightarrow$ Zone or Component;
          encodes physical containment.
    \item \texttt{part\_of} (9)   Component $\rightarrow$ System or Zone;
          inverse structural membership.
    \item \texttt{monitors} (44)   Component $\rightarrow$ Sensor (bidirectional
          pair); a station monitors its attached sensors.
    \item \texttt{triggers} (22)   Sensor $\rightarrow$ AnomalyEvent or
          Maintenance; a sensor reading outside its bounds triggers the event.
    \item \texttt{resolves} (8)   Maintenance $\rightarrow$ Sensor; the
          maintenance action resolves the root-cause sensor anomaly.
    \item \texttt{feeds\_into} (3)   Component $\rightarrow$ Component;
          material flow in the production line.
    \item \texttt{correlates\_with} (1)   Sensor $\rightarrow$ Sensor with
          \texttt{ruleRef} property; the key cross-station dependency that underlies
          the GT-0009 anomaly (ST02\_SEALING\_CUR correlates with
          ST04\_PACKAGING\_SPD with a 90-minute lag).
    \item \texttt{authorized\_for} (218)   Person $\rightarrow$ Zone;
          encodes access rights for each personnel--zone pair.
    \item \texttt{detected\_in} (10)   SafetyEvent $\rightarrow$ Zone; records
          where each safety incident was observed.
    \item \texttt{involves} (10)   SafetyEvent $\rightarrow$ Person.
\end{itemize}

% § 8.1.3  MERGE-based Cypher loader; --no-clear flag; dual labels (:Node + :SemanticType);
%           _clean() strips empty CSV fields; ruleRef only edge property; summary check.
\subsubsection{Loading Procedure}

\texttt{step4\_neo4j\_loader.py} is a self-contained loader that follows a
fixed four-phase sequence: clean, constrain, load nodes, load edges.

\paragraph{Clean phase.}
By default the loader issues \texttt{MATCH (n) DETACH DELETE n} to wipe the graph
before each run, ensuring that every execution starts from a known-empty state.
Passing \texttt{--no-clear} at the command line suppresses this step, which is
useful when step5 or step7 need to add data on top of an already-loaded ABox
without destroying it.

\paragraph{Constraint phase.}
A uniqueness constraint,
\texttt{CREATE CONSTRAINT abox\_node\_id IF NOT EXISTS FOR (n:Node) REQUIRE n.nodeId IS UNIQUE},
is created before any nodes are written.
The \texttt{IF NOT EXISTS} qualifier makes this operation idempotent: re-running
the loader (e.g.\ after a partial failure) does not raise an error if the constraint
already exists.
This constraint also prevents accidental duplicate nodes if the loader is invoked
twice without the clear flag.

\paragraph{Node loading phase.}
The loader reads all 115 rows from \texttt{nodes.csv}.
Before constructing the Cypher statement, the helper \texttt{\_clean(row)} drops
every CSV column whose value is an empty string, so nodes receive only the
properties that are actually populated for their semantic type (for example,
a \texttt{:Person} node does not receive empty \texttt{critHi} or \texttt{unit}
properties that are only meaningful for \texttt{:Sensor} nodes).
Each node is written with the Cypher pattern:
\begin{center}
\texttt{MERGE (n:Node \{nodeId: \$nid\}) SET n:\$label SET n += \$props}
\end{center}
This assigns every node two labels: the generic \texttt{:Node} label, which
enables label-agnostic traversals across the entire graph, and the node's
specific semantic label (e.g.\ \texttt{:Sensor}, \texttt{:Component},
\texttt{:AnomalyEvent}), which enables type-filtered queries without
scanning unrelated node types.

\paragraph{Edge loading phase.}
The 341 rows of \texttt{edges.csv} are loaded using:
\begin{center}
\texttt{MATCH (a:Node \{nodeId: \$fid\}) MATCH (b:Node \{nodeId: \$tid\})}\\
\texttt{MERGE (a)-[r:\$rel\_type]->(b) SET r += \$props}
\end{center}
Of all the CSV columns in \texttt{edges.csv}, only the \texttt{ruleRef} value
(when non-empty) is stored as an edge property.
This is deliberate: the sole edge that carries a \texttt{ruleRef} is the single
\texttt{correlates\_with} relationship between \texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_CUR} and
\texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_SPD}, whose \texttt{ruleRef: RULE-ST02-04} explicitly
encodes the cross-station causal reference that underlies the GT-0009 anomaly.
All other edges are property-free; their semantics are fully expressed by their
relationship type and endpoint node identities.

\paragraph{Summary check.}
After loading, \texttt{print\_summary()} issues two Cypher aggregation queries
and prints the node count per semantic label and the edge count per relationship
type to the console.
This provides an immediate sanity check that all 115 nodes and 341 edges landed
correctly without requiring a separate verification script.

% § 8.2  step5_neo4j_rules.py: auto-selects highest F1_content run; creates :ExtractedRule nodes
%         with :COVERS (sensor) and :APPLIES_TO (component) wiring into the ABox.
%         Strips 7 step2 meta-columns; stores model/paradigm/ruleClass on the node.
\subsection{Extracted Rule Overlay}
\label{sec:rule_overlay}

\texttt{step5\_neo4j\_rules.py} reads \texttt{evaluation\_summary.csv}, picks
the row with the highest \texttt{content\_f1} score, and loads every extracted
rule from that run file as a \texttt{:ExtractedRule} node in Neo4j.
If the evaluation CSV has not been produced yet (i.e.\ step3 has not been run),
the script falls back to a hardcoded default run file so that the layer-4 pipeline
can be tested in isolation.
A specific run file can always be provided explicitly via
\texttt{--run-file ext\_<model>\_<paradigm>.csv}, bypassing the auto-selection
entirely.
The \texttt{--clear-rules} flag deletes all existing \texttt{:ExtractedRule} nodes
(with their incident edges) before loading, allowing a different run to be
overlaid on the ABox without a full step4 reload.
Before writing anything, the script creates a uid-based index
(\texttt{CREATE INDEX extracted\_rule\_id IF NOT EXISTS FOR (r:ExtractedRule) ON (r.uid)})
so that the \texttt{MERGE} operations in the main loop execute in $O(\log n)$
time rather than full-graph scans.

\paragraph{Property filtering.}
Each CSV row in a step2 result file contains both content columns (the actual
extracted rule fields: \texttt{ruleId}, \texttt{class}, \texttt{sensor},
\texttt{station}, \texttt{condition}, \texttt{action}, \texttt{severity},
threshold fields, etc.)\ and seven bookkeeping columns added by the step2
pipeline runner:
\texttt{source\_file}, \texttt{model\_name}, \texttt{paradigm}, \texttt{level},
\texttt{run\_id}, \texttt{llm\_turns}, \texttt{text\_truncated}.
Before pushing a row to Neo4j, the loader strips all seven bookkeeping columns
(the \texttt{\_META\_FIELDS} set in the source code) so that the
\texttt{:ExtractedRule} node contains only semantically meaningful rule content.
In addition, every empty-string field is dropped, mirroring the \texttt{\_clean()}
logic used in step4.
Alongside the content fields, four derived properties are always stored on the node:
\texttt{uid} (the compound key; see Section~\ref{sec:uid_scheme}),
\texttt{ruleId} (the rule identifier, or \texttt{AUTO-\$i} if missing),
\texttt{ruleClass} (from the \texttt{class} column, used by step6 to filter
maintenance rules), and
\texttt{model} / \texttt{paradigm} (parsed from the run filename by
\texttt{\_parse\_meta()}, enabling provenance queries such as
``which model produced this rule?'' directly from the graph).

% § 8.2.1  uid = run_file::ruleId::row_index, globally unique even across runs with identical ruleIds.
%           step7 mirrors this formula for spec_uid ("SPEC::<uid>") and action_uid ("ACT::<uid>").
\subsubsection{UID Scheme}
\label{sec:uid_scheme}

Each \texttt{:ExtractedRule} node is assigned a globally unique identifier:

\begin{center}
\texttt{uid = <run\_file>::<ruleId>::<row\_index>}
\end{center}

This compound key has three components.
The \emph{run\_file} component (e.g.\ \texttt{ext\_multi\_agent\_langgraph.csv})
scopes the identifier to a specific extraction run, so rules from different
evaluation runs never collide.
The \emph{ruleId} component encodes the rule's logical identity as assigned by
the LLM (e.g.\ \texttt{RULE-ST02-04}); if the LLM omitted a \texttt{ruleId},
the loader falls back to \texttt{AUTO-\$i} where \texttt{\$i} is the CSV
row index (0-based).
The \emph{row\_index} component is the definitive tie-breaker: two rules in the
same run with the same \texttt{ruleId} (a rare but possible LLM error) still
receive different UIDs.

Step7 mirrors this scheme when creating its typed sub-nodes: a
\texttt{:ThresholdSpec} derived from rule \texttt{uid} is assigned
\texttt{spec\_uid = "SPEC::<uid>"}, and an \texttt{:Action} node is assigned
\texttt{action\_uid = "ACT::<uid>"}. This naming convention ensures that step7
can always reconstruct the uid of the parent \texttt{:ExtractedRule} from any
child node's uid, and that the child uids are globally unique for the same
reasons as the parent uid.

% § 8.2.2  :COVERS → Sensor (matched on sensor field name); :APPLIES_TO → Component (station field);
%           unmatched rules (e.g. AccessRule zone refs) stored as isolated :ExtractedRule nodes.
\subsubsection{Wiring to the ABox}

After creating the \texttt{:ExtractedRule} node, the loader attempts two
connections into the ABox for every rule:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{(r:ExtractedRule)-[:COVERS]->(s:Sensor)}, the loader issues:
          \begin{center}
          \texttt{MATCH (r:ExtractedRule \{uid: \$uid\})}\\
          \texttt{MATCH (s:Sensor \{name: \$sensor\})}\\
          \texttt{MERGE (r)-[:COVERS]->(s)}
          \end{center}
          where \texttt{\$sensor} is the value of the \texttt{sensor} field in
          the extracted rule row. This exact-name match against the ABox
          \texttt{Sensor.name} property is the primary coverage link used by
          step6: an AnomalyEvent or Maintenance node is considered covered if
          at least one \texttt{:ExtractedRule} has a \texttt{:COVERS} edge to
          the sensor that triggers it.
          If the rule's \texttt{sensor} field is empty the wiring attempt is
          skipped entirely, so the rule node exists in Neo4j but remains
          topologically disconnected from the ABox sensor layer.
    \item \texttt{(r:ExtractedRule)-[:APPLIES\_TO]->(c:Component)}, matches
          the rule's \texttt{station} field against the \texttt{name} property
          of \texttt{:Component} nodes, recording which production station the
          rule was extracted from. This edge is used by step7 for the
          cross-station dependency heuristic but is not part of the phase-2
          coverage evaluation.
\end{itemize}

For the best run (\texttt{ext\_multi\_agent\_langgraph.csv}, 79 rules), the
loader successfully creates \texttt{:COVERS} edges for all rules that carry a
sensor identifier that exactly matches an ABox sensor name.
Rules that do not produce a \texttt{:COVERS} edge fall into one of two categories:
(a)~\texttt{AccessRule} entries, which store a \emph{zone} name in the
\texttt{sensor} field rather than a sensor identifier, so the
\texttt{MATCH (s:Sensor \{name: \$sensor\})} step finds no node;
(b)~rules where the LLM inferred a sensor name that does not exactly match any
\texttt{Sensor.name} in the ABox (e.g.\ partial names or names with formatting
differences).
These unmatched rules are fully stored as \texttt{:ExtractedRule} nodes with all
their content properties; they are simply not wired to the ABox topology and
therefore do not contribute to the phase-2 coverage counts.
The loader prints the final \texttt{:COVERS} and \texttt{:APPLIES\_TO} counts plus
the number of unmatched sensors and stations so that wiring gaps are immediately
visible after each run.

% § 8.3  step7_rule_to_triplets.py: class-aware sub-graph expansion into typed nodes and edges.
%         Four class groups handle LLM sub-variants. _safe_float() guards against NaN/None/empty.
%         --clear-triplets removes ThresholdSpec+Action nodes and all 7 typed edge types.
\subsection{Typed Triplet Enrichment}
\label{sec:triplets}

\texttt{step7\_rule\_to\_triplets.py} converts the flat \texttt{:ExtractedRule}
nodes that step5 loaded into a richer, semantically typed sub-graph.
The enrichment is class-aware: the script dispatches each rule into one of four
canonical class groups based on its \texttt{ruleClass} property, and each group
produces a different set of typed nodes and edges.

\paragraph{Class groupings and LLM sub-variants.}
LLMs do not always use exactly the four canonical class names defined by the
ground truth. To handle the natural variation in LLM output without discarding
valid extractions, step7 maps every observed class name to one of four groups:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Threshold group} (\texttt{ThresholdRule}): rules that specify
          static numeric alarm limits (critical and warning bounds).
    \item \textbf{Operational group} (\texttt{OperationalRule},
          \texttt{CorrelationRule}, \texttt{AnomalyRule},
          \texttt{CorrelatedFaultRule}): rules that describe conditions under
          which an operational response must be triggered, including causal
          or correlation-based rules that LLMs sometimes give their own
          sub-class names.
    \item \textbf{Maintenance group} (\texttt{MaintenanceRule},
          \texttt{PredictiveMaintenanceRule}): rules that prescribe a maintenance
          or inspection action, including predictive variants extracted from
          condition-monitoring language in the SOPs.
    \item \textbf{Access group} (\texttt{AccessRule}, \texttt{OccupancyRule},
          \texttt{AcknowledgmentRule}): rules that restrict or regulate zone
          access and personnel acknowledgment, including occupancy-limit
          and acknowledgment sub-types that LLMs occasionally produce.
\end{itemize}

A rule that does not match any of the four groups is silently skipped for typed
enrichment (its \texttt{:ExtractedRule} node and its \texttt{:COVERS}/\texttt{:APPLIES\_TO}
edges from step5 remain intact).

\paragraph{Numeric value handling.}
LLM output for threshold fields (\texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi},
\texttt{warnLo}, \texttt{critLo}) is not always well-formed: values may be empty
strings, the literal string \texttt{"nan"}, or \texttt{"None"}.
The helper \texttt{\_safe\_float(val)} converts a field value to a Python float
or returns \texttt{None} without raising an exception for any of these malformed
inputs. Only non-\texttt{None} values are stored in Neo4j (both on
\texttt{:ThresholdSpec} nodes and as properties on \texttt{:GOVERNS} edges),
so the graph always reflects what the LLM actually extracted rather than
placeholder zeros or nulls.

\paragraph{Indexes and the \texttt{--clear-triplets} flag.}
Before the main enrichment loop, two indexes are created with
\texttt{IF NOT EXISTS} guards:
\texttt{CREATE INDEX threshold\_spec\_uid IF NOT EXISTS FOR (t:ThresholdSpec) ON (t.uid)}
and
\texttt{CREATE INDEX action\_uid IF NOT EXISTS FOR (a:Action) ON (a.uid)},
making the per-rule \texttt{MERGE} lookups fast.
The \texttt{--clear-triplets} flag deletes all \texttt{:ThresholdSpec} and
\texttt{:Action} nodes (with \texttt{DETACH DELETE}) and removes all seven typed
relationship types
(\texttt{GOVERNS}, \texttt{HAS\_SPEC}, \texttt{TRIGGERS\_RULE},
\texttt{REQUIRES\_ACTION}, \texttt{RESTRICTS\_ACCESS},
\texttt{SCHEDULES\_MAINTENANCE}, \texttt{DEPENDS\_ON})
before rebuilding, enabling a clean re-run from a different source file without
a full step4/step5 reload.

\paragraph{Station index for dependency detection.}
Before the main loop, step7 builds a \texttt{rules\_by\_station} dictionary that
groups all rules by their \texttt{station} field.
The cross-station \texttt{:DEPENDS\_ON} heuristic (Section~\ref{sec:crossstation})
consults this index to look up rules from a foreign station in $O(1)$ time,
avoiding an $O(n^2)$ inner scan over the full rule list.

% § 8.3.1  :ThresholdSpec node (critHi/warnHi/warnLo/critLo/unit) + :HAS_SPEC (rule→spec)
%           + :GOVERNS with threshold values on the edge (spec→sensor); created if ≥1 numeric field populated.
%           spec_uid = "SPEC::<uid>"; only non-None values stored (sparse).
\subsubsection{ThresholdRule Enrichment}

For each rule in the threshold group that carries at least one numeric threshold
value (i.e.\ \texttt{\_safe\_float()} returns a non-\texttt{None} result for at
least one of \texttt{critHi}, \texttt{warnHi}, \texttt{warnLo}, \texttt{critLo}),
the enrichment creates one \texttt{:ThresholdSpec} node and two typed edges:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \texttt{:ThresholdSpec} node is created with
          \texttt{spec\_uid = "SPEC::<uid>"} (where \texttt{uid} is the parent
          rule's compound key) and properties \texttt{ruleId}, \texttt{unit}, and
          whichever of \texttt{critHi}/\texttt{warnHi}/\texttt{warnLo}/\texttt{critLo}
          are non-\texttt{None}. Only populated fields are written; a
          \texttt{ThresholdRule} that specifies only \texttt{critHi} produces a
          \texttt{:ThresholdSpec} node with exactly that one threshold property,
          not four properties with three null values.

    \item \texttt{(r:ExtractedRule)-[:HAS\_SPEC]->(ts:ThresholdSpec)} links the
          rule to its numeric specification. This edge exists for every
          \texttt{:ThresholdSpec} node.

    \item \texttt{(ts:ThresholdSpec)-[:GOVERNS \{critHi, warnHi, \ldots\}]->(s:Sensor)}
          is created whenever the rule also has a non-empty \texttt{sensor} field
          that matches an ABox \texttt{:Sensor} by name. The threshold values are
          stored \emph{redundantly} on the relationship as well as on the spec node,
          enabling one-hop Cypher queries that skip the spec node entirely:
          \begin{center}
          \texttt{MATCH (ts)-[g:GOVERNS]->(s:Sensor \{name: \$sensorId\})}\\
          \texttt{RETURN g.critHi, g.warnHi, g.warnLo, g.critLo, g.unit}
          \end{center}
          Only the non-\texttt{None} values produced by \texttt{\_safe\_float()}
          are stored as edge properties, so the edge property map is sparse in the
          same way as the spec node.
\end{enumerate}

From the 79 extracted rules, those classified as \texttt{ThresholdRule} with at
least one populated numeric field receive the full \texttt{:ThresholdSpec} +
\texttt{:HAS\_SPEC} + \texttt{:GOVERNS} sub-graph.
Approximately 46--50 \texttt{:ThresholdSpec} nodes and the corresponding typed edges
are created; the exact count depends on how many \texttt{ThresholdRule} entries
carry at least one non-empty, non-NaN threshold value after \texttt{\_safe\_float()}
filtering.

% § 8.3.2  :TRIGGERS_RULE (sensor→rule, if sensor field populated), :Action node + :REQUIRES_ACTION (rule→action);
%           :SCHEDULES_MAINTENANCE (rule→Maintenance, MaintenanceRule group only, via sensor-triggers-maintenance path).
%           action_uid = "ACT::<uid>". ruleId matching unreliable (LLM uses different conventions than ABox).
\subsubsection{OperationalRule and MaintenanceRule Enrichment}

Rules in both the operational group (\texttt{OperationalRule},
\texttt{CorrelationRule}, \texttt{AnomalyRule}, \texttt{CorrelatedFaultRule}) and
the maintenance group (\texttt{MaintenanceRule},
\texttt{PredictiveMaintenanceRule}) receive the same two typed constructs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{(s:Sensor)-[:TRIGGERS\_RULE]->(r:ExtractedRule)}, a reverse
          edge from the sensor to the rule it activates.
          Created whenever the rule's \texttt{sensor} field is non-empty and the
          named sensor exists in the ABox. The direction (sensor $\rightarrow$ rule)
          mirrors the ABox \texttt{triggers} edge (sensor $\rightarrow$ anomaly/maintenance),
          making it natural to traverse from a live reading through the sensor to
          both the ground-truth event and the extracted rule in a single pattern.

    \item \texttt{(r:ExtractedRule)-[:REQUIRES\_ACTION]->(a:Action)}, an
          \texttt{:Action} node is created with
          \texttt{action\_uid = "ACT::<uid>"} and properties
          \texttt{text} (the full prescribed response text from the \texttt{action}
          CSV column) and \texttt{severity} (from the \texttt{severity} column).
          Created whenever the rule carries a non-empty \texttt{action} field.
          This node captures the procedural knowledge that the LLM extracted from
          the SOP (e.g.\ ``shut down station and notify maintenance engineer'') as
          a first-class graph entity, rather than burying it in a single string
          property on the rule node.
\end{itemize}

In addition, specifically for rules in the \textbf{maintenance group} that carry a
non-empty \texttt{sensor} field, a third typed edge is created:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{(r:ExtractedRule)-[:SCHEDULES\_MAINTENANCE]->(m:Maintenance)}
         , matched by traversing the ABox path
          \texttt{(s:Sensor \{name: \$sensor\})-[:triggers]->(m:Maintenance)}.
          Rather than attempting to match on \texttt{ruleId} strings
          (which is unreliable because LLM-extracted identifiers such as
          \texttt{RULE-ST02-03} never match the ABox identifiers such as
          \texttt{MAINT-02}), the script resolves the link entirely via the
          shared sensor name.
          This edge directly connects each extracted maintenance rule to the ABox
          maintenance procedure it encodes, enabling one-hop queries such as
          ``which extracted rules would activate MAINT-05?''
\end{itemize}

% § 8.3.3  :RESTRICTS_ACCESS (rule→Zone) via _STATION_TO_ZONE lookup (station code → human-readable zone name).
%           Lookup handles both station codes (e.g. CHM01_CHEMICALSTORAGE) and zone names directly
%           (e.g. "Chemical Storage") for robustness against LLM naming variation.
\subsubsection{AccessRule Enrichment}

Rules in the access group (\texttt{AccessRule}, \texttt{OccupancyRule},
\texttt{AcknowledgmentRule}) are linked to the zone they restrict via:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{(r:ExtractedRule)-[:RESTRICTS\_ACCESS]->(z:Zone \{name: \$zone\_name\})}
\end{itemize}

The ABox stores zones by their human-readable names (e.g.\ \texttt{Chemical Storage}),
but LLM-extracted access rules typically store the station code in their
\texttt{station} field (e.g.\ \texttt{CHM01\_CHEMICALSTORAGE}).
A static lookup dictionary \texttt{\_STATION\_TO\_ZONE} bridges the two naming
conventions.
It maps every station code to its containing zone name
(e.g.\ \texttt{CHM01\_CHEMICALSTORAGE} $\mapsto$ \texttt{Chemical Storage},
\texttt{SRV01\_SERVERROOM} $\mapsto$ \texttt{Server Room}) but also includes
entries that map zone names directly to themselves
(e.g.\ \texttt{Chemical Storage} $\mapsto$ \texttt{Chemical Storage}).
The second set of entries handles the case where the LLM writes the zone name
directly in the \texttt{station} field instead of the station code, a common
variation for rules extracted from access-control sections of the SOPs, where
zone names appear more frequently than station codes.
If the \texttt{station} field of an access rule does not match any key in the
lookup table, no \texttt{:RESTRICTS\_ACCESS} edge is created and the rule remains
an isolated node in the graph.
In the current best run, this yields 1 \texttt{:RESTRICTS\_ACCESS} edge.

% § 8.3.4  _detect_foreign_stations: scans condition text for known station keywords (both short
%           code ST02 and long name SEALING); :DEPENDS_ON (rule→rule) heuristic; no edges in best run.
\subsubsection{Cross-Station Dependency Detection}
\label{sec:crossstation}

For every rule whose \texttt{condition} field is non-empty, the helper
\texttt{\_detect\_foreign\_stations(condition, own\_station)} scans the condition
text for any of 18 station keywords drawn from two naming conventions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Short station codes}: \texttt{ST01}, \texttt{ST02},
          \texttt{ST03}, \texttt{ST04}, \texttt{SRV01}, \texttt{WRH01},
          \texttt{CHM01}, \texttt{RND01}, \texttt{CAF01}.
    \item \textbf{Long station names}: \texttt{FILLING}, \texttt{SEALING},
          \texttt{LABELLING}, \texttt{PACKAGING}, \texttt{SERVERROOM},
          \texttt{WAREHOUSE}, \texttt{CHEMICAL}, \texttt{RDLAB},
          \texttt{CAFETERIA}.
\end{itemize}

The scan is performed on the upper-cased condition text.
Any keyword that matches a station \emph{other than the rule's own station}
identifies a foreign station dependency.
For each detected foreign station, the script creates a
\texttt{:DEPENDS\_ON} edge from the current rule to every rule in
\texttt{rules\_by\_station[foreign\_station]}:
\begin{center}
\texttt{(src:ExtractedRule)-[:DEPENDS\_ON]->(tgt:ExtractedRule)}
\end{center}
This heuristic is designed to capture the GT-0009 causal pattern (a packaging
rule whose condition text mentions the sealing station as the upstream cause)
without requiring the LLM to explicitly annotate dependencies.
Checking both short codes (\texttt{ST02}) and long names (\texttt{SEALING})
maximises recall across different LLM writing styles: a rule that says
``caused by sealing motor overload'' would match on \texttt{SEALING} even if
it never uses the station code \texttt{ST02}.

For the current best run (\texttt{ext\_multi\_agent\_langgraph.csv}), zero
\texttt{:DEPENDS\_ON} edges were created.
This confirms that none of the 79 extracted rules reference a foreign station
in their \texttt{condition} field under either naming convention, which in turn
confirms that the multi-agent pipeline did not capture the cross-station causal
reasoning required by GT-0009 at the condition-text level
(see Section~\ref{sec:gt0009}).

% § 8.4  step6_phase2_evaluation.py: graph-traversal check, does the extraction layer guard
%         each ABox AnomalyEvent and Maintenance node via :COVERS edges?
%         Output saved to layers/step3_results/phase2_evaluation.csv.
\subsection{Phase-2 Graph Coverage Evaluation}
\label{sec:phase2_results}

The phase-2 evaluation (\texttt{step6\_phase2\_evaluation.py}) tests whether the
extracted rules, as loaded into Neo4j, provide sufficient coverage of the real
operational events recorded in the ABox.
Unlike the F1\textsubscript{content} metric (which compares rules in isolation),
this evaluation traverses the graph: it asks whether the \emph{connected} extraction
layer adequately ``guards'' the ABox's fault and maintenance nodes.
The script runs three independent checks (anomaly events, maintenance procedures,
and the GT-0009 fusion test), prints a formatted console report with per-event
pass/fail indicators, and persists the per-event results to
\texttt{layers/step3\_results/phase2\_evaluation.csv}.
This CSV file records, for each of the 22 ABox events: its identifier, type,
anomaly type, severity, the triggering sensor name, the number of covering
\texttt{:ExtractedRule} nodes, the first three matched \texttt{ruleId} values,
and the overall \texttt{COVERED}/\texttt{GAP} status.
The minimum acceptable coverage threshold is hardcoded as \texttt{MIN\_COVERAGE = 0.70}
(70\%), matching the value specified in the iMAKS dataset guide.

% § 8.4.1  14/14 AnomalyEvents covered; Cypher: MATCH (s:Sensor)-[:triggers]->(ae) OPTIONAL MATCH (r)-[:COVERS]->(s).
\subsubsection{Coverage of Anomaly Events}

For each of the 14 \texttt{:AnomalyEvent} nodes in the ABox, the evaluator checks
whether at least one \texttt{:ExtractedRule} covers the sensor that triggered it:

\begin{center}
\texttt{MATCH (s:Sensor)-[:triggers]->(ae:AnomalyEvent)}\\
\texttt{OPTIONAL MATCH (r:ExtractedRule)-[:COVERS]->(s)}\\
\texttt{RETURN ae.gtId, count(r) AS coveringRules}
\end{center}

\noindent All 14/14 anomaly events are covered, spanning all five fault types
present in the benchmark (SPIKE, DRIFT, STUCK, OUT\_OF\_RANGE, CORRELATED).

% § 8.4.2  8/8 Maintenance nodes covered (MAINT-01–08); RULE-CORR-02/03 provide confirmed redundant
%           coverage for MAINT-05/06; MAINT-02 has 4 covering rules (RULE-CORR-01 is 4th, truncated in CSV).
\subsubsection{Coverage of Maintenance Procedures}

For each of the 8 \texttt{:Maintenance} nodes, the evaluator traverses the
\texttt{Sensor-[:triggers]->\allowbreak Maintenance} path and checks whether any
\texttt{:ExtractedRule} of a maintenance class covers the same sensor:

\begin{center}
\texttt{MATCH (s:Sensor)-[:triggers]->(m:Maintenance)}\\
\texttt{OPTIONAL MATCH (r:ExtractedRule)-[:COVERS]->(s)}\\
\texttt{WITH m, s, [x IN collect(r)}\\
\texttt{~~~WHERE x.ruleClass IN}\\
\texttt{~~~['MaintenanceRule', 'PredictiveMaintenanceRule']]\ AS maintRules}\\
\texttt{RETURN m.ruleId, s.name, size(maintRules) AS coveringRules,}\\
\texttt{~~~~~~~[x IN maintRules | x.ruleId][0..3] AS matchedIds}
\end{center}

The filter includes both \texttt{MaintenanceRule} and
\texttt{PredictiveMaintenanceRule} (the LLM sub-variant for predictive
maintenance conditions), mirroring the maintenance group definition in step7.
Coverage is determined purely by sensor-name matching, not ruleId matching,
because LLM-extracted identifiers (e.g.\ \texttt{RULE-ST02-03}) do not follow
the ABox naming convention (\texttt{MAINT-02}).
The \texttt{matchedIds} list is capped at three entries in the CSV display.

\noindent All 8/8 maintenance procedures are covered by the full pipeline.
The pipeline successfully extracts \texttt{MaintenanceRule} entries for all eight
required sensors across the four SOP documents:
MAINT-01 (\texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_VIB}),
MAINT-02 (\texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_CUR}),
MAINT-03 (\texttt{ST03\_LABELLING\_TEN}),
MAINT-04 (\texttt{ST01\_FILLING\_PRS}),
MAINT-05 (\texttt{SRV01\_SERVERROOM\_TMP}),
MAINT-06 (\texttt{WRH01\_WAREHOUSE\_TMP}),
MAINT-07 (\texttt{CHM01\_CHEMICALSTORAGE\_TMP}), and
MAINT-08 (\texttt{RND01\_RDLAB\_HUM}) are all present in the final extracted rule
set.
In addition, two correlated-fault rules extracted from SOP-003 contribute
confirmed redundant coverage: \texttt{RULE-CORR-02} for MAINT-05
(\texttt{SRV01\_SERVERROOM\_TMP}) and \texttt{RULE-CORR-03} for MAINT-06
(\texttt{WRH01\_WAREHOUSE\_TMP}).
MAINT-02 (\texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_CUR}) receives four covering
\texttt{MaintenanceRule} entries in total; the phase-2 CSV display is capped at
three matched IDs, so the fourth rule (\texttt{RULE-CORR-01}) is present but not
shown in the truncated output.
This comprehensive maintenance coverage is a direct result of the multi-stage pipeline's
Validator performing additive gap-filling and the Judge triggering a targeted re-extraction
of SOP-003 (the maintenance document), which ensures that drift, stuck-sensor, and
correlated-fault patterns are not misclassified as threshold rules.

% § 8.4.4  Hardest test: ST04_PACKAGING_SPD speed anomaly caused by ST02_SEALING_CUR drift (90-min lag).
%           Pipeline passes condition (1), rule covers SPD sensor, but fails condition (2), no
%           :COVERS edge from a rule whose condition names the sealing station as upstream cause.
\subsubsection{GT-0009: Multi-Source Causal Fusion Test}
\label{sec:gt0009}

GT-0009 is the hardest test case in the benchmark: a speed anomaly at
\texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING} is caused by a current drift at \texttt{ST02\_SEALING}
with a 90-minute lag, encoded in the ABox as a
\texttt{correlates\_with \{ruleRef: RULE-ST02-04\}} edge between the two sensors.
A complete extraction requires (1) a rule covering sensor
\texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_SPD} and (2) that rule's \texttt{condition} field
explicitly naming \texttt{ST02} or the sealing station as the upstream cause.

The pipeline \emph{passes condition~(1)}: the extracted rule set contains two rules
covering \texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_SPD}: \texttt{RULE-ST04-03} (an
\texttt{OperationalRule}: ``SPD drops below 1.00\,m/s without operator command;
action: verify RULE-ST02-04'') and \texttt{RULE-THR-ST04-SPD-CRIT} (a full-band
\texttt{ThresholdRule} from SOP-002).

The pipeline \emph{partially captures the causal chain but fails condition~(2)}.
Two supporting rules are successfully extracted: \texttt{RULE-ST02-04} (a
\texttt{MaintenanceRule} at \texttt{ST02\_SEALING} whose condition reads
``\texttt{ST02\_SEALING-CUR CORRELATES with speed reduction at ST04\_PACKAGING due
to shared electrical coupling}'') and \texttt{RULE-CORR-01} (extracted from
SOP-003, capturing the full cross-station causal chain: sealing motor overload
$\rightarrow$ packaging speed drop, with a three-step remediation action).
However, neither of these rules is attributed to \texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_SPD}
via a \texttt{:COVERS} edge: \texttt{RULE-ST02-04} is anchored at
\texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_CUR} and \texttt{RULE-CORR-01} carries no \texttt{sensor}
field.
Consequently the step6 causal-attribution query, which checks whether any rule
that \texttt{:COVERS} \texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_SPD} satisfies any of three
conditions:
\begin{center}
\texttt{toLower(r.condition) CONTAINS 'st02'}\\
\texttt{OR toLower(r.condition) CONTAINS 'sealing'}\\
\texttt{OR toLower(r.ruleId) CONTAINS 'st02'}
\end{center}
Still, this query yields no match.
Both rules that \emph{do} cover \texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_SPD}
(\texttt{RULE-ST04-03} and \texttt{RULE-THR-ST04-SPD-CRIT}) fail all three
sub-conditions: their \texttt{condition} fields do not contain `st02' or
`sealing', and their \texttt{ruleId} values do not contain `st02'.

This reveals a nuanced gap: the pipeline successfully extracts the
cross-station correlation on the \emph{source} side (\texttt{ST02} perspective)
but does not produce a downstream \texttt{ST04} rule whose \texttt{condition}
or \texttt{ruleId} field explicitly references the sealing station as the
upstream cause.
\texttt{RULE-ST04-03}'s \emph{action} field (``verify RULE-ST02-04'') contains an
indirect reference, but step6 queries the \texttt{condition} and \texttt{ruleId}
fields, not the \texttt{action} field.
Future work should either propagate causal attribution to the \texttt{condition}
field of the downstream rule during extraction, or augment the step6 query to
traverse \texttt{:DEPENDS\_ON} edges created by the cross-station heuristic in
step7, or extend the causal check to the \texttt{action} field.

% § 8.5  Two systematic error classes visible in :GOVERNS edge properties (field-level quality, not coverage gaps).
\subsection{Extraction Error Analysis in the KG Context}
\label{sec:error_analysis}

Although the pipeline achieves 100\% structural coverage, loading the extracted rules
into Neo4j exposes residual quality issues that are invisible when evaluating rules
as isolated text strings.
Two systematic error categories are observable by inspecting the \texttt{:GOVERNS}
edges and their numeric properties.
Importantly, these are \emph{field-level quality issues}, not coverage gaps: the rules
are structurally present and correctly typed at the class level, but the numeric values
on some \texttt{ThresholdSpec} nodes are semantically incorrect.

% § 8.5.1  Drift magnitude (e.g. CUR DRIFT > 1.0 A / VIB DRIFT > 0.05 mm/s) misclassified as static critHi
%           on a ThresholdSpec; correct MaintenanceRule still present (MAINT-01/02) but duplicate ThresholdSpec added.
\subsubsection{Temporal Drift Threshold Confusion}

Several \texttt{:GOVERNS} edges carry threshold values that are semantically
inconsistent with the sensor's nominal operating range stored in the ABox.
For example:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{ST02\_SEALING\_CUR} (nominal 12.4\,A, \texttt{critHi}~=~15.0\,A
          in the ABox) has a \texttt{GOVERNS} edge with
          \texttt{\{unit: A, critHi: 1.0\}}.
          The value 1.0\,A derives from the SOP-003 maintenance rule
          ``CUR DRIFT $>$1.0\,A for 30+ min'' a \emph{drift magnitude},
          not a static alarm limit.
          The LLM extracted the drift threshold as a \texttt{critHi} value and
          assigned it to a \texttt{ThresholdRule}, causing a 15$\times$ underestimate
          of the true critical current on this particular \texttt{ThresholdSpec} node
          (note: the pipeline also correctly extracts \texttt{MAINT-02} as a
          \texttt{MaintenanceRule} for the same sensor, so the maintenance procedure
          is covered, but the drift magnitude leaks into a \texttt{ThresholdRule}
          numeric field as well).
    \item \texttt{ST04\_PACKAGING\_VIB} (\texttt{critHi}~=~0.35\,mm/s in the ABox)
          has a \texttt{GOVERNS} edge with \texttt{\{unit: mm/s, critHi: 0.05\}}.
          The value 0.05\,mm/s originates from the maintenance condition
          ``VIB DRIFT $>$0.05\,mm/s over 60\,min'' and was similarly
          misclassified as a static critical threshold on a \texttt{ThresholdSpec},
          even though the correct \texttt{MaintenanceRule} (MAINT-01) is also present.
\end{itemize}

In both cases the LLM failed to fully distinguish between a \emph{static bound}
(absolute sensor limit, appropriate for a \texttt{ThresholdRule}) and a
\emph{temporal drift constraint} (rate of change over a time window, appropriate
for a \texttt{MaintenanceRule} condition field).
The pipeline correctly extracts both rule classes for these sensors, but the
drift magnitude also appears as an incorrect \texttt{critHi} value in a separate
\texttt{ThresholdSpec} node.
This motivates a post-processing step that cross-checks \texttt{ThresholdSpec}
numeric values against the ABox sensor bounds and flags anomalously small thresholds
as candidate drift magnitudes.

% § 8.5.2  ST03_LABELLING_TEN STUCK condition expressed in "samples" (operationally valid) vs ABox unit Newton;
%           motivates unit normalisation and dimension consistency post-processing in future versions.
\subsubsection{Unit Dimension Mismatch}

A secondary error is visible in the \texttt{ST03\_LABELLING\_TEN} sensor, where one
\texttt{:GOVERNS} edge carries \texttt{\{unit: samples, critLo: 10.0\}}.
The sensor's native unit is Newton (N), but the LLM correctly identified the STUCK
condition (``$>$10 consecutive samples without change'') and expressed it in the
operationally meaningful unit of \emph{samples}.
While semantically valid for the STUCK detection use case, this introduces a
unit-dimension mismatch relative to the ABox sensor properties, which would cause
a type error in any downstream reasoner that directly compares
\texttt{GOVERNS.unit} against \texttt{Sensor.unit}.
This class of error motivates unit normalisation and dimension consistency checking
as post-processing steps in future pipeline versions.
